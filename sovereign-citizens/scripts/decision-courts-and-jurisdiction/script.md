# The Courtroom Escape Hatches Sovereign Citizens Swear By

## Hook

Picture an Ohio courtroom, and a man facing four criminal charges from a run-in with police at the end of 2023, standing up to read a statement he plainly believed would protect him. [s01 ok] He told the court he reserved his "unalienable rights per UCC 1-308 without prejudice", and then he added: "I give the Court no consent". [s02 ok] If you have spent any time in certain corners of YouTube, you know what those words are supposed to do. They are supposed to switch the court off. They did not: he was found guilty of all four charges against him and fined for each one, though he was not sent to jail. [s05 ok] So is there anything at all you can say, sign or point to that takes you out of a court's reach? We will take the popular escape hatches one at a time, read the document each one points to, and see what the courts have said about it.

## Setup

The belief underneath them is simple: a channel called Out of Babylon tells viewers that "there are various jurisdictions that do not have jurisdiction over you unless you grant them that jurisdiction". [s08 ok] Rob Menard, a Canadian freeman-on-the-land figure, claimed in a television interview that Canada's chief justice had said "no one can provide adjudication Services without the consent of both members of the adjudication"; that is his account, not a ruling. [s09 ok] From that one idea, that a court needs your consent, the movement has built a whole toolkit. When you sign something, you are told to add a code section beside your name; The Grown Academy, for example, tells viewers to "write UCC 1-308 next to your signature All Rights Reserved". [s11 ok] Another channel, Americans In Action, calls UCC 1-308 "a Lifeline for free men and women to uphold the constitution". [s12 ok] When you walk into the courtroom, you are told to look at the flag: a letter to the editor printed in the Mountain Democrat newspaper says "The fringe denotes Admiralty law’s jurisdiction". [s13 ok] Behind it all sits a history lesson: the act of 1871, says a channel called SYDLIS, is "exactly what changed america from a constitutional republic to a corporation". [s14 ok] If the government is a company, its laws are company rules: Americans In Action says "these codes and statutes are only the bylaws of the corporation", and the website jonhoyle.com sums up where that leads: "All law is commerce. All commerce is contract. No contract, no case". [s15 ok] So that is the toolkit: refuse your consent, reserve your rights under a commercial code, point at the fringe, invoke an old statute, and treat every law as a contract you never signed. Each trick points at something real. The question is whether the real thing says what the trick needs it to say.

## Investigation

Start with the code section, because you can read it yourself. UCC 1-308 says that a party who goes ahead and performs "with explicit reservation of rights" does not thereby give up the rights it reserved, and that phrases such as without prejudice or under protest are enough to do it. [s19 ok] The official comment explains what that is for: it "provides machinery for the continuation of performance along the lines contemplated by the contract despite a pending dispute". [s20 ok] Picture a builder who takes delivery of timber she thinks is the wrong grade: she can accept it and pay under protest, keep the job moving, and still argue about the price later. That is the whole job of the section: keeping a contract moving during a dispute. Nothing in its text or comment mentions courts, criminal charges, or consent to a judge's authority. [s22 ok] Courts have noticed: in the Ohio case we started with, the appeals court wrote that "Any arguments related to the UCC are unavailing". [s23 ok] So the code section is not a shield; it is a tool for business disputes. But what about simply refusing to consent? In United States v. Benabe, the federal appeals court for the Seventh Circuit dealt with defendants who said their status put them beyond the court's power; the trial court dismissed those arguments. [s25 ok] The appeals court's answer was blunt: whatever status a person claims, sovereign citizen, secured-party creditor or flesh-and-blood human being, "that person is not beyond the jurisdiction of the courts". [s26 ok] It added that such theories "should be rejected summarily, however they are presented", and it listed its own earlier cases, one of which described a sovereign citizen defense as having "no conceivable validity in American law". [s27 ok] The same argument turns up on the other side of the world: a review of pseudolaw cases in Australia and New Zealand describes three consent arguments, one from a man held on a warrant who treated the court's authority as a matter of his consent; all three were unsuccessful. [s28 ok] The authors put the problem in one line: "no one needs to explicitly and affirmatively consent to the authority of a jurisdiction to be subject to it, especially for purposes of tax or criminal law". [s29 ok] Next, the flag. In McCann v. Greenway, a federal court in Missouri faced the gold-fringe argument and noted that other courts had already called it frivolous, totally frivolous and preposterous. [s31 ok] Then, "in the interest of killing this argument for good", the judge worked through the law of the flag. [s32 ok] The flag statute describes stripes and stars, and an Army statement from the 1920s, quoted by the court, said that a fringe "is not considered to be a part of the flag, and it is without heraldic significance". [s33 ok] And even if a fringe did mean something, the court said, it could not shrink the court's power, because jurisdiction "is a matter of law, statute, and constitution, not a child’s game". [s34 ok] The Lux Law Firm, which explains these cases online, puts it simply: "There are no cases attributing any kind of legal significance to the fringes on a flag in an American courthouse". [s35 ok] Now the history lesson. In 1871 Congress did pass a law, titled "An act to provide a government for the District of Columbia", the federal district around the capital. [s37 ok] In Metropolitan Railroad Company v. District of Columbia, the Supreme Court read it as making the District "a body corporate for municipal purposes", with power to make contracts and to sue and be sued, like any city. [s38 ok] The Court held that the District's "character as a mere municipal corporation has not been changed", and that the sovereign power over it is lodged "in the government of the United States", with Congress as its supreme legislature. [s39 ok] It even spelled out what that means for anyone charged with an offence there: "Crimes committed in the District are not crimes against the District, but against the United States". [s40 ok] Which leaves the biggest claim of all: that laws are contracts, and you can simply decline to sign. In Indiana, a man who called himself a sovereign citizen sued the state, arguing that it could not tax him without a contract between them, and asked for a refund of $11.5 billion. [s42 ok] The Seventh Circuit said it had "repeatedly rejected such claims", called the suit frivolous, and said he "was lucky to be spared sanctions for filing such a suit". [s43 ok] In New Zealand, a self-described freeman on the land appealed a traffic notice on the ground that he had never consented to the Land Transport Act, and the court struck out his claim as an abuse of process. [s44 ok]

## Findings

Put the tricks side by side and the pattern is hard to miss. Each real document says something narrower than the trick: a commercial-code rule about carrying on with a contract under protest, a fringe that the law gives no meaning, and an 1871 act that set up a municipal government for the District of Columbia. [s46 ok] And in every ruling in this story, refusing consent, reserving your rights and calling statutes contracts got the same answer: the court had power over the person in front of it. [s47 ok] Researchers who reviewed pseudolaw in Australia and New Zealand put it flatly: "No courts accept these arguments, and no courts absolve the claimant (or defendant) from their legal obligations and responsibilities". [s48 ok] A history of these movements in Canada says Canadian courts "consistently rejected them", and the study of Supreme Court of Canada appeals concludes: "These ideas are pseudolaw, and devoid of merit". [s49 ok] In Queensland, an appeal court judge described one self-styled citizen sovereign's challenge to the police in a single line: "The argument was obvious nonsense and the magistrate rightly rejected it". [s50 ok] And back in Ohio, the appeals court noted that despite a wealth of material debunking them, "the claims continue to make their way to court and fail". [s51 ok]

## Qualifications

Does that mean these arguments have never worked anywhere, for anyone? The evidence answers a narrower question: the broad reviews come from Canada, Australia and New Zealand, while in the United States we have a run of appeals courts saying they have rejected these theories again and again. [s53 ok] Even the Canadian record is incomplete, as one of its own authors points out: many of these proceedings never produce a reported judgment at all. [s54 ok] The IRS says claims of being solely a citizen of a sovereign state, outside federal taxation, have been uniformly rejected by the courts, but it is writing about tax cases only. [s55 ok] Nothing in that record shows a jurisdiction trick that held up, though it cannot count unreported hearings or every American courtroom.

## Implications

So what does that mean if you are the one standing in front of the judge? First, the words buy you nothing: the man in Ohio reserved his rights and withheld his consent, and was convicted anyway. [s58 ok] Second, they can cost you: in Benabe, the defendants kept interrupting after the judge had ruled, and the appeals court said they were "entering the territory of abuse of the judicial process". [s59 ok] The Canadian history counts the price in plainer terms: "wasted court resources, unnecessary litigation expense, and sometimes devastating negative consequences to those who attempted to implement these schemes". [s60 ok] Say you get a ticket, and a video tells you the fix is a few words under your signature. Whatever you write there, the court that hears the ticket gets its authority from the law, not from your signature. [s62 ok] So when someone promises you a phrase that switches a court off, ask them for the ruling where it worked.

## Conclusion

Back to the question we started with: is there anything you can say, sign or point to that takes you out of a court's jurisdiction? On the evidence here, no: not a reservation of rights under UCC 1-308, not a refusal to consent, not a fringed flag, not the act of 1871, and not the claim that laws are contracts. [s65 ok] Jurisdiction comes from law, not from what you sign or say in the room. [s66 ok]
