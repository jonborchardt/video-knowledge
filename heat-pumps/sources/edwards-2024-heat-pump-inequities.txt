Assessing inequities in electrification via heat pumps across the

U.S.

Morgan R. Edwards1,2, Jaime Garibay-Rodriguez1,2,3, Jacob Shimkus Erickson2,4, Muhammad
Shayan1,6, Jing Ling Tan1, Xingchi Shen5,6, Yueming (Lucy) Qiu5, Pengfei Liu7

Abstract

Heat pumps are an energy-efficient and increasingly cost-effective solution for reducing
greenhouse gas emissions in the building sector. However, other clean energy technologies
such as rooftop solar are less likely to be adopted in underserved communities, and thus
policies incentivizing their adoption may funnel tax dollars to well-resourced communities. Unlike
previously-studied technologies, the effects of heat pumps on household energy bills may be
positive or negative depending on local climate, fuel availability and costs, and other factors.
Here we propose a framework for assessing heat pump inequities across the U.S. We find that
households in communities of color and with higher percentages of renters are less likely to use
heat pumps across the board. Moreover, communities of color are least likely to use heat pumps
in regions where they are most likely to reduce energy bills. Public policies must address these
inequities to advance beneficial electrification and energy justice.

Main Paper

1. Introduction

Policy responses to the climate crisis are expanding rapidly in the U.S. at national, state, and
local levels.1 Research suggests that carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions must decline dramatically
over the next decade and reach net zero by mid-century to limit global temperature change to
well below 2°C, and preferably 1.5°C, as set out in the Paris Agreement.2 Strategies to meet
these targets in the energy sector include two key elements: (1) transitioning electricity
generation to low-carbon technologies such as wind and solar and (2) electrifying energy needs
in transportation, industry, and buildings. Transitioning buildings off natural gas (and other fuels
such as propane, heating oil, and wood) offers a meaningful pathway to reducing emissions
from the building sector. Natural gas emits CO2 when it is burned in home appliances and also

thus be a valuable part of meeting net zero and other climate policy goals.6

Heat pumps are an attractive option for electrifying space heating as they are roughly 3-5 times
more efficient than traditional electric resistance heaters and can provide heating in the winter
and cooling in the summer (see S1).7 However, the net private and social benefits of heat
pumps vary across locations and depend on many factors including local climate, electricity mix,
prices of electricity and fuels, gas access, installation costs, and the insulation and air-tightness
of a building. Heat pumps are cost effective in many locations across the U.S.8–11 However, they
can also be more expensive to operate, especially in colder regions (where they are less
efficient) when compared to natural gas furnaces. High heat pump penetration will also increase
electricity demand, which may place stress on electric grid capacity.7 Moreover, the climate
impacts of heat pumps (and other equipment that uses refrigerants) can increase if there is high
leakage or improper management of refrigerants.12 Nevertheless, high heat pump use rates (up
to 90%) are likely to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and provide other benefits across much
of the U.S., especially as the electric grid continues to decarbonize.13

There is a strong rationale for public policy to support heat pump adoption in the U.S. The net
social benefits on a national scale are positive, yet penetration remains low (at just over 10% of
households). Globally, heat pump penetration varies dramatically, with high penetration in
countries such as Norway, Finland, and Sweden and low penetration in many others.14 A rich
literature on the energy efficiency gap identifies many likely barriers to heat pumps adoption,15
including high upfront costs, credit and liquidity limitations, low familiarity with technologies,8 low
awareness of policy incentives,15,16 personal preferences or behavioral factors,17 and limited
financial and engineering literacy.18 Split incentives for rental units19 or homeowners who may
wish to move in the future20 also hinder adoption (although homes with heat pumps may enjoy a
price premium when sold21). A variety of policies have been proposed to address these barriers
(see S2).21–23 Previous incentives often take the form of tax breaks, rebates, or low- and
no-interest loans at the federal, state, local, non-profit, and utility scale. Increasingly, these
incentives offer additional support for low-income and qualified households, building on
longstanding energy poverty programs such as the Weatherization Assistance Program (WAP)
and Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP).24

The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) substantially expands incentives for heat pumps and other
clean energy technologies at a federal level,25 and local, state, and utility policies are also
increasingly promoting household electrification. However, the extent to which these incentives
will benefit underserved communities remains uncertain. Prior research indicates that
low-income households are less likely to take advantage of energy efficiency and clean energy
incentives.26–30 However, the IRA provides more targeted incentives than past federal policies,
and recent research on rooftop solar31 (and prior research on income-targeted incentives for
broadband27) suggests that income-qualified incentives may be effective at driving increased
adoption among low-income households.30,32 As heat pump sales grow rapidly in the U.S. and
globally,33 disparities in adoption may exacerbate current inequities in the energy system.34 With

                                                                                                                               2

higher levels of electrification, households that electrify sooner are also less likely to bear the
rising costs of stranded natural gas assets.35 An assessment of current inequities in heat pump
adoption and implications for policy is thus important and timely.

Here we investigate spatial patterns in heat pump use across the U.S. to inform the design and
implementation of equitable and beneficial electrification policies. Although space heating and
cooling represent on average 55% of household energy use and 44% of energy bills (with high
regional variation36), inequities in heat pump use have yet to be comprehensively explored. Two
recent studies assess the social and economic determinants of heat pump adoption nationally37
and in New York38, but these analyses do not explicitly focus on equity. They also do not
account for an important conceptual challenge: unlike technologies such as rooftop solar and
energy efficiency, operating costs of heat pumps are highly variable and may exceed the costs
of fossil fuel alternatives. We develop a framework for evaluating inequities in this class of
technologies and apply it to heat pumps using a national property dataset and local
socioeconomic data (see Methods). Specifically, we address the following questions: (1) How
does heat pump use across the U.S. vary with respect to income, race/ethnicity, and renter
status? (2) Where do these use patterns suggest possible inequities (and where do they not)?
(3) What are the implications for policies to promote fair and just energy transitions?

2. Framework for estimating heat pump inequities

Energy justice calls for everyone to have access to clean, safe, affordable, and reliable energy
and to be able to participate in energy decisions that affect them.39,40 Energy justice has many
dimensions: distributive, procedural, restorative, recognition and cosmopolitan.41 Researchers
and communities have identified many injustices across the energy life cycle.40,42–44 Underserved
communities are simultaneously more likely to experience energy-related pollution and less
likely to benefit from energy services.45 Energy poverty remains a important policy challenge,
with over one in four U.S. households reporting difficulty paying energy bills.24,46 Households
experiencing energy poverty may limit their energy use,47 forgo other essential services to pay
energy bills, or experience shut-offs if bills are not paid.48,49 They are also more vulnerable to
shocks (e.g., COVID-1950), price changes,51 and the effects of extreme weather.52 Households in
communities of color are 60% more likely to be energy burdened compared to majority white
communities.53 They are also more likely to live in energy-inefficient homes and less likely to use
technologies such as rooftop solar, even controlling for income and resource potential.15,54

Our framework for assessing inequities in heat pump use builds on prior work on distributive
energy justice by distinguishing between three concepts: inequality, inequity, and injustice55 (see
Figure 1). Inequality refers to a difference across populations.56–58 Inequalities have many
potential drivers and do not necessarily indicate a need for policy intervention. For example, we
expect to see more heat pumps in mild climates, where they are more efficient and cost
effective.13 However, differences may persist even after controlling for these factors. Specifically,
there may be fewer heat pumps in underserved households (e.g., racial or ethnic minorities,
low-income, renters, etc.). Previous literature on other clean energy technologies characterizes
these differences as an inequity.30–32,42,59 Inequity is a normative concept that refers to what is
considered unfair, with roots in philosophical and legal scholarship.60 If a technology provides

                                                                                                                               3

benefits, it is unfair for underserved households not to have access. Finally, an injustice occurs
when public policies or other systemic factors exacerbate, or perpetuate, existing inequities (see
Discussion for policy recommendations to advance energy justice).

Many clean energy technologies, such as energy efficiency and rooftop solar, provide benefits to
households by reducing energy bills. Heat pumps, in contrast, may raise or lower bills compared
to fossil fuel alternatives. Lower heat pump use among underserved households in areas where
they lower heating bills (or provide other benefits such as access to cooling, for households that
do not otherwise have air conditioning) represents a potential inequity. It may also suggest that
there are disproportionate (and thus unjust) barriers to access, and that heat pump incentive
programs unfairly divert resources away from low-income households. However, higher heat
pump use among underserved households in areas where they raise bills also represents a
potential inequity. For these cases, adoption by well resourced populations can provide a
service to society by buying down the cost curve. To operationalize this framework, we integrate
spatial data on heating technologies and a cost/benefit model of energy bill impacts. We then
assess patterns in heat pump use overall and across regions where they are likely to cause

                                                                                                                               4

energy bills to increase, decrease, or stay the same. We aim for this framework to complement
and extend existing theories on justice in energy transitions.41

3. Patterns in heat pump use across the U.S.

Heating technologies vary across the U.S. (see Figure 2). As heat pump use is not reported with
high granularity in other datasets, we use national household property data from the Zillow
Transaction and Assessment Database (ZTRAX).21,61 We develop a logistic regression (logit)
model to estimate household heat pump use as a function of census-tract variables. Using this
model, we assess whether differences in race, ethnicity, income, and renter status significantly
correlate with differences in the likelihood of household heat pump use. We also control for
factors expected to affect the likelihood of heat pump use such as heating and cooling degree
days (HDD and CDD, which affect heat pump efficiency), electricity and natural gas prices, and
building age (as a proxy for electrification readiness) and type (i.e., single family, multifamily,
etc.). Finally, we include state dummy variables to account for state-level policies (such as
electrification subsidies or fuel-switching restrictions) and other state-specific differences. (See
Methods and S3 for a full description of data sources and S6 for robustness checks using
household sample data from the Residential Energy Consumption Survey [RECS].)

After controlling for state effects, several control variables correlate meaningfully with heat pump
use (see Figure 3a and Table S4.1).16 We find a negative relationship between HDD and CDD

and heat pump use, with a 10% increase corresponding to a 9.4% and 2.6% decrease in odds,
respectively.8 This effect is likely explained by better heat pump performance in milder climates
within states. There is also a large negative relationship with building age, with evidence of a
nonlinear relationship (see S8.7). This effect is likely explained by the fact that older buildings
are generally less electrification ready. Heat pump use is also lower in multifamily buildings,
which face higher barriers to adoption, and higher in small dwellings such as duplexes and
mobile homes. Natural gas prices and home value both have smaller correlations. Higher
electricity prices are associated with higher heat pump use (with a 10% increase corresponding
to a 7.3% increase in odds). This effect may be explained by areas dominated by electric
resistance heating, where higher prices provide an incentive to switch to a more efficient heat
pump. This explanation is supported by robustness checks, where we find that electricity price is
positively correlated with heat pump use where electric resistance is dominant and negatively
correlated where direct fuel use is dominant (see S8.15).

4. Nonlinear estimates of heat pump use

We next explore non-linear relationships between heat pump use, income, and renter status
across different racial/ethnic majority census tracts (see Methods and Figure 4). We normalize
heat pump use by state to control for state-level variation in climate, policies, electricity and fuel
prices, and other variables. Households of color are disproportionately low-income and more
likely to be renters, but distinct patterns emerge when comparing areas with similar median
income and homeownership rates.64,65 For white-majority census tracts (69% of tracts), heat
pump use increases slightly with median income up to approximately $60,000 and then
declines. In contrast, Black-majority census tracts (7.3% of tracts) show a sharp rise in heat
pump use at approximately the same income level. Hispanic and no-majority census tracts have
installed fewer heat pumps compared to their white counterparts across all income levels.
Similarly, for white-majority and Black-majority census tracts, heat pump use generally
decreases as the percentage of rented households increases, while other racial majority census
tracts have lower heat pump use overall and flatter trends. We also find evidence of nonlinear
relationships between heat pump use, electricity prices, and home age (see S9).

5. Regional analysis of heat pump inequities

We examine patterns in heat pump use in areas where they are highly likely to provide net
benefits (or costs) to households in the form of changes to energy bills. First, we calculate the

                                                                                                                               7

net effects of heat pump adoption on energy bills at the county scale by comparing the operating
costs of a heat pump to those of the most common primary heating technology, taking into
account device efficiencies, energy prices, and climate data (see Methods). Following previous
studies,66–68 we focus on differences in operating costs because (1) we are assessing inequities
in heat pump use rather than new adoption and (2) differences between average installation
costs of a heat pump compared to other heating devices (e.g., natural gas, propane, or fuel oil
furnace or electric resistance heater) plus air conditioning are small (see S7.6). Similarly, we do
not include operating costs of cooling because differences across cooling technologies (e.g.,
heat pump versus a stand-alone air conditioner) are smaller than those across heating
technologies. We recognize that not all homes with heating also have cooling; for these homes,
switching to a heat pump may entail higher energy bills but greater access to a service (i.e.,
cooling) that may be increasingly important under climate change.69

We perform a Monte Carlo analysis to identify counties that exhibit either net benefits or costs in
90% of simulations and a neutral region where at least 10% of simulations yield net benefits and
net costs. Net benefits vary across the U.S., with 53% of households in our dataset seeing
benefits in terms of reduced energy bills, 19% seeing costs, and 28% seeing no clear benefits
or costs (see Figure 5a). While our dataset does not contain all U.S. homes (see S3.11), our
results are consistent with findings from recent studies that focus on operating66–68 or life
cycle11,13 (including installation) costs. Primary heating fuel is a key driver of benefits: in regions
where the heating source is electricity or fuels other than natural gas, heating is generally more
expensive and thus the net benefits of heat pumps are higher (see Figure 5b). However, climate
and energy prices also play an important role. To examine inequities in heat pump use, we then
run separate logistic regressions for these three cost/benefit regions (see Figure 5c-e). Using
the framework presented in Section 2, we define inequities as occurring where there is (1) a
lower odds of heat pump use in underserved communities in the net benefits region or (2) a
lower odds in the net costs region. We use the same variables as in our national analysis (see
S5) but focus our discussion on results related to race, income, and renter status.

Race and ethnicity are significantly correlated with lower heat pump use (within states) across
all three regions. However, the negative relationship between minority populations and heat
pump use is larger when heat pumps provide net benefits to households relative to regions with
net costs. For example, a 10% increase in the Black population in a census tract leads to a 13%
decrease in the odds of heat pump use in regions with net benefits (coincidentally the same
value as in our national analysis), compared to a 5.1% decrease in regions with net costs. For
the neutral region, we observe a larger decrease (25.8%) for the Black population; however, the
overall effect across racial/ethnic minorities is less pronounced in comparison to regions with net
benefits. The percentage of the population living in regions where heat pumps provide net
benefits versus net costs varies across racial and ethnic groups, with 39% of the white
population and 52% of the Black population living in regions where heat pumps provide net
benefits, compared to 39% overall (see SI Tables 5.2 and 5.3). However, all racial and ethnic
groups are represented in each of our three regions (see S5 for further discussion).

We also observe differences in the relationship between income and renter status and heat
pump use across the three cost/benefit regions. To explore these differences further and assess
potential nonlinearities, we repeat the LOESS analysis described in Section 4 for each of the
three cost/benefit regions (see Figure S9.1). We observe a generally positive relationship
between income and heat pump use across all three regions up to a median income of
approximately $100,000 (for net benefit regions), $65,000 (for neutral regions), and $77,000 (for
net cost regions). After these levels, the effect of income is negative but smaller for the net
benefit and neutral regions and larger for the net cost regions. Renter status negatively
correlates with heat pump use in the net benefits and net costs regions but has the largest
coefficient in the latter, whereas it exhibits a small positive relationship in the neutral region. As
in the results on race and ethnicity, these results also suggest that low-income and renter

communities are less likely to use heat pumps, even in regions where they provide benefits,
suggesting an equity that will be important to address in electrification policies.

6. Discussion

Household electrification is an emerging energy justice issue in the U.S. At the national scale
and controlling for state fixed effects, we find that higher percentages of racial and ethnic
minority households, lower median income, and higher percentages of renters at the census
tract level all correlate with lower heat pump use. The negative correlations between racial and
ethnic minority populations and heat pump use are largest in areas where heat pumps are
expected to provide direct benefits to households. While directionally the same, the negative
correlation between renter status and heat pump use is largest in areas where heat pumps are
likely to provide a net cost to households. Income effects are nonlinear but suggest that
moderate income is generally associated with higher heat pump use across net cost and benefit
regions. Taken together, these results suggest that, within states, minority communities are
disproportionately less likely to reap the benefits of using a heat pump. Additionally, our
nonparametric analysis shows that higher income among Black-majority census tracts is
associated with a higher likelihood of heat pump use relative to other groups, suggesting distinct
relationships between heat pump use, race/ethnicity, and income.

Our analysis contributes new insight to the field of energy justice by assessing inequities in a
technology that may have higher or lower operational costs than incumbents. Widely studied
technologies such as rooftop solar or energy efficiency may have high upfront costs but have
lower operational costs across the board. By separately analyzing dynamics in regions where
heat pumps have positive, negative, or neutral effects on energy bills, we can provide a more
nuanced assessment of whether lower (or higher) heat pump use in underserved communities
suggest potential inequities. Because we find evidence of racial/ethnic, income, and (to a lesser
extent) renter status inequities in regions where heat pumps provide bill savings, policies,
markets, or other drivers may be implicitly under-serving these marginalized groups.32,57,70,71
Conversely, lower heat pump use in regions where heat pumps raise energy bills does not
represent an inequity and can help drive down costs, particularly for heat pumps designed to
operate in cold climates. Notably, race and ethnicity are the strongest negative correlates of
heat pump use in regions where heat pumps are expected to result in energy bill savings,
leading us to conclude that there is evidence of an emerging inequity.

We present a quantitative assessment of national racial and ethnic patterns in heat pump use
and find robust evidence of heat pump inequities for racial/ethnic minorities, with smaller effects
for low-income households and renters. Looking forward, we note several areas for future
research. Because we do not measure demographic variables at the household level, our
results may under- or over-estimate inequities. Robustness checks using nationally
representative household data from RECS along with prior literature (which shows that
environmental inequities often increase with data granularity) provide further evidence for the
inequities we observe.64,65 However, future work may leverage more granular data to assess
these dynamics at the household level. We also focus on current heat pump users, but the
demographics of new adopters may be different. Future work could explore these dynamics and

                                                                                                                             10

investigate patterns of technology diffusion over time and across different countries.72 It can
also investigate the effects of changes in the relative prices of electricity and fuels over time,
either due to market forces or tax policies, and the effects of technology adoption on heating
and cooling demand.73 Finally, while the mechanism behind the correlations we observe is
outside the scope of this paper, the evidence presented warrants further causal analysis.

Our framework can be applied to other clean energy technologies where the net household
benefits may be positive or negative.74,75 While we focus specifically on effects on energy bills,
there are a variety of other potential costs and benefits of new technologies. For example, heat
pumps may increase cost reliability (particularly in regions where fuel prices are highly
variable14), improve thermal comfort, and provide access to cooling for households without air
conditioning. They may also have other costs such as increased backup requirements during
electricity service interruptions. Utility incentive programs can also affect adoption decisions,
and future work could provide targeted recommendations for state and utility policy to advance
beneficial electrification. Beyond the individual household, heat pumps also have costs and
benefits to society, including decreasing or increasing air pollution and greenhouse gas
emissions, depending on the electricity source. As more households electrify, cost dynamics can
change and potentially create larger injustices. For example, as the customer base for natural
gas utilities shrinks, the burden of cost recovery for durable infrastructure is placed on a smaller
number of remaining customers and may raise energy bills.76–78 Underserved communities may
disproportionately bear these stranded asset costs.

Addressing heat pump inequities is important globally and perhaps particularly in the U.S., given
recent policies.79 Our analysis suggests that care will need to be taken to ensure that the $43
billion in consumer incentives for energy efficiency and electrification directly benefits
underserved communities. By using insights on current patterns in heat pump use and their
costs and benefits, policies can be designed to reduce inequities, and ultimately injustices, in
electrification transitions. We identify three key policy recommendations:

    1. Building electrification policies must be location and context specific. For locations where
         the heat pumps reduce energy bills, targeted outreach can be coupled with free or
         low-cost installations.19 However, in areas where they may increase bills, incentives must
         be accompanied by efforts to reduce energy costs, such as energy efficiency upgrades
         and on-bill financing. It is important that the effects of heat pump adoption on energy bills
         are accurately communicated,80 both to avoid increasing energy poverty and to prevent
         community backlash, as has been observed with time-of-use pricing programs.81,82 The
         structure of the IRA, which includes funding for state and local governments, may help
         initiatives be more responsive to community needs and priorities.

    2. Targeted incentives can increase adoption in underserved households. Historically,
         uptake of incentives and outreach by private firms has focused on higher income
         households who are more likely to be early adopters.83 By deliberately reversing this
         pattern with targeted incentives and outreach, the IRA may more effectively reach
         otherwise underserved populations. Research suggests that means-tested incentives
         can be effective at increasing adoption of other technologies (e.g., energy efficiency and

                                                                                                                             11

         rooftop solar) among targeted populations.83 Targeted outreach to contractors, installers,
         and landlords will also be important to promote access for underserved households.
    3. Beneficial electrification requires a community and system perspective. Heat pump
         adoption can have important interactions with other energy policies and infrastructure.
         For example, areas without access to natural gas tend to have higher heating costs and
         can be targets for early electrification, even in relatively cold climates. Additionally, prior
         research has noted that policies such as increasing block pricing in California create
         incentives to adopt rooftop solar for specific demographics.84 Clean energy technologies
         such as rooftop solar have also been shown to follow a seeding pattern,72 and thus these
         policies could encourage future adoption. These same perspectives will be important to
         consider in future research and policy discussions.

Methods

7. Data sources

We analyze spatial patterns in heat pump use across the U.S. using household-level property
data from the Zillow Transaction and Assessment Database (ZTRAX), accessed January
2021.85 The dataset includes approximately 163 million buildings (147 million residential) across
the U.S., 63.7 million of which report at least one of 38 heating and cooling technologies (e.g.,
heat pumps, furnaces, boilers, radiators, space heaters, central A/C units, window A/C units,
etc.). We designate a household as using a heat pump if it is listed as the primary heating
source. ZTRAX also provides information on property characteristics including building type
(single-family, multifamily, shared-wall single-family, mobile home, other), location, and market
and assessed value. Additional characteristics such as number of stories, number of rooms,
number of units, building area code, building area square feet, occupancy status, effective year
built, and value certification date were excluded from the analysis as over 15% of the data in
these variables were missing. For residences that did not have data on home value, the
assessed value was used as a substitute. The ZTRAX database includes hundreds of additional
variables including home sale features and information on architecture and finishes that were
deemed less relevant to this analysis. See S3 for a full description of variables.

We then added census tract data (racial and ethnic identity, median income, homeownership
status, and house age) from the American Community Survey (ACS) 5-year estimates for
2016–202086 via the R tidycensus package version 1.2.3.87 The ACS data was merged into the
ZTRAX data using the household 2020 census identifiers (GEOIDs) calculated using latitude
and longitude. This merge resulted in a data loss of 0.1% of ZTRAX records due to invalid
location data. We also included heating and cooling degree days (HDD and CDD) calculated
from average daily outdoor temperature data from the nearest NOAA weather station between
1991-202088 (number of days with an average temperature above or below the standard 65°F66
outdoor temperature benchmark). We obtained natural gas utility boundaries from the Homeland
Infrastructure Foundation-Level Database (HIFLD)89 and price data (for 2020) from the
EIA-17690 form. Average prices for residential electricity customers at the utility level were

11. Net benefits analysis

We estimate the net benefits of switching to a heat pump as the change in operating costs (i.e.,
energy bills). Our analysis uses inputs on the primary heating technology (from the ACS
2016-2020 survey) and average annual electricity prices at the county level. We use state-level
data on natural gas, propane, fuel oil, and wood prices. All prices are from 2020. We calculate
the coefficient of performance (COP) of a heat pump in each county using hourly temperature
profiles from typical meteorological year data.99 Following previous studies, we use a function
that assumes a linear relationship between temperature and COP for temperatures between 5
and 47°F for heat pumps of similar capacity (25,000 BTU) based on the Northeast Energy
Efficiency Program (NEEP) (see S7.3).13,66 We assume average efficiencies of 80% for natural
gas and propane furnaces, 83% fuel oil furnaces, and 79% for wood stoves (based on the 2015
Federal standard for residential furnaces100 and average efficiency reported by the EPA-certified
wood stove database).101 We exclude differences in installation costs but find these to be small
when comparing a heat pump with a conventional heating and cooling system (see S7.6). We
also assume constant heating demand and electricity prices.

Section 1: Heat pump technologies and costs

Heat pumps are an efficient, electric heating and cooling technology. They are generally
substantially more efficient than other sources of heating and cooling and can be a
lower-emissions alternative to traditional heating systems, particularly when powered by
electricity from low-carbon sources.1,2 While traditional furnaces are powered by the combustion
of fuels such as natural gas (or use electric resistance heating), heat pumps use electricity to
transfer heat. The underlying operation of a heat pump is based on using mechanical work (i.e.,
a compressor unit) to increase the pressure (and thus, the temperature) of a refrigerant fluid and
circulate it through a heat exchanger inside a building to provide space heating.3 Typically, heat
pumps can also be used for indoor space cooling by reversing the direction of the refrigerant
fluid, which draws heat from indoors and expels it to the ambient environment. The ability of
heat pumps to provide both heating and cooling is a key advantage over traditional heating and
cooling systems that rely on two separate devices (e.g., a furnace and an air conditioner) and
can be particularly beneficial for homes that do not already have air conditioning systems. This
analysis, however, focuses on heat pumps used as the primary source of heat as this
represents the greatest opportunity for displacing on-site fossil fuel combustion.

Three types of heat pumps are currently available on the market: air-, ground-, and
water-source. Air-source heat pumps (ASHPs), which transfer heat between indoor and outdoor
air, are the most common type used in the U.S2 and have a global market share of over 60%.
ASHPs may be connected by ducts but are also available as ductless devices (i.e., mini-split) for
homes without ducts. While there is heat in outdoor air even in winter, ASHPs become markedly

                                                                            2

less efficient in extremely cold climates.4 Traditionally, dual fuel systems are used in these
cases, where a gas furnace or electric resistance heating system provides backup heat.
However, improvements in two-stage heat pump systems, which have an additional
compression cycle, can reduce the need for backup heating in colder regions and maintain
comfort on colder days.5 Ground-source heat pumps (GSHPs), which transfer heat between
indoor space and the nearby ground, can also operate more efficiently in colder climates but are
less common due to their higher capital costs and specific technical skills required for
installation.6 Water source heat pumps use submerged pipes to transfer heat to and from a
water source and are thus limited to areas with an appropriate source.

The costs of heat pumps vary substantially across households and regions within the U.S.7 For
air source heat pumps, typical installation costs range from $5,000 to $10,000, with an average
of $7,500 (adjusted to 2024 prices).8 Policy incentives, including through the Inflation Reduction
Act (IRA), can reduce these costs. Home size, efficiency, and climate can also lead to different
sizing specifications and equipment costs. Costs of labor and technician experience with
recommending and installing heat pumps can also vary across locations. As demand for heat
pumps increases with policy support and market incentives (and high natural gas prices driven
by geopolitical events and other factors), the costs of heat pumps may also rise temporarily as
value chains adjust.9 Large regional differences in operating costs of heat pumps are also
common and depend on local climate and the costs of electricity compared to natural gas and
other fuels.10 The cost landscape for heat pumps is also changing. For instance, previous
studies have found that a smaller portion (~30%) of households would see net benefits when
switching to a single speed heat pump11,12, whereas more recent findings that include cold
climate heat pumps find they can be cost effective for a large portion (~60%) of households.13
This large variation in costs is a key motivation for the framework we propose that uses net
benefits in assessing inequities in heat pump use across the U.S.

Several factors are likely to change the net private and social benefits of heat pumps in the
future. Technological improvements will allow heat pumps to operate more efficiently (requiring
less electricity to provide the same heating output), particularly in colder climates where
efficiency poses the greatest barriers. New business models that lower installation costs and
provide greater operational flexibility could also increase the value of heat pumps for users and
electric utilities.14 Furthermore, net benefits could change if the price of natural gas and other
fuels rise or fall relative to the price of electricity. From a climate perspective, studies suggest
that heat pump use rates of up to 90% can cost-effectively reduce greenhouse gas emissions in
the U.S. by 2050.12 However, potential cost setbacks could also occur, including reductions in
fossil fuel prices with reduced demand and as well as challenges for electric grid operations with
large-scale use.15 Nevertheless, taken together, research suggests that future decarbonization
of electricity and heat pump cost declines due to technological improvements can further
increase the public and private benefits of heat pump use across the U.S.

Section 2: Heat pump policies and incentives

There is a strong rationale for public policies to support heat pump adoption. The net public
benefits (including health and environmental benefits) of heat pumps are positive in many parts
of the U.S., but the private costs to households may be greater than fossil fuel alternatives
(particularly natural gas).16 Even in locations where heat pumps have lower life cycle costs, high
up-front costs can impede adoption,17 a challenge that is further exacerbated by credit and
liquidity constraints for some households.18 These costs may include the heat pump itself as
well as electrical panel and other home upgrades that may be required.19 Cost barriers may be
especially acute in emergency installation cases, where the current heating system has failed
and there may be limited time for homeowners to upgrade home systems to be heat pump
ready or research and learn about new heating alternatives. Furthermore, in some areas, the
operating costs of heat pumps exceed those of natural gas furnaces. This feature makes heat
pumps different from many other household technologies such as energy efficiency or rooftop
solar, where operating costs are relatively low or zero.

Policies to incentivize energy efficiency and clean energy technologies typically aim to improve
access to capital, reduce the burden of high upfront costs, lower financing costs, and address
split incentives.20 At the federal level, schemes like the Weatherization Assistance Program
(WAP) install energy efficiency upgrades for low-income residents, such as insulation, furnace
repair or replacement, duct sealing, and refrigerator replacements.21 State and local
governments may also provide tax incentives, direct rebates, or grants. Additionally, in most
states, utility regulatory commissions mandate utilities to use ratepayer dollars to fund
low-income energy efficiency programs,22 and utilities may also provide incentives through
mechanisms such as on-bill financing. These approaches have also been used by various
states to incentivize rooftop solar, including on-bill financing for solar installations and no-cost
installation for low-income households.23 These policies and programs continue to serve an
important role in motivating residential energy efficiency and clean energy use. However, new
policy mechanisms will also be important; for example, policies targeting increased adoption by
landlords will be important to increasing technology use by renters.

Heat pump incentives often take the form of tax breaks, rebates, or low-/no-interest loans.24
Many of these are administered by utilities at the behest of state regulators. For example, Duke
Energy in North Carolina offers customers a rebate of several hundred dollars for replacing
baseboard (i.e., electric resistance) heating with a heat pump.25 Others are overseen by
statewide organizations that facilitate rebates and incentives. For instance, Focus on Energy in
Wisconsin administers rebates for customers of participating utilities.26 Other states provide
direct support for heat pumps, including northeastern states such as Maine, Vermont, and
Massachusetts. Policies differ by state, but most take the form of rebates for either customers
(downstream) or installers (upstream or midstream). The rebate amount is generally determined
by household annual income, heat pump size, fuel type, and/or duct status. The Inflation
Reduction Act (IRA) substantially expands incentive programs nationally to provide up to
$14,000 in direct consumer rebates for heat pumps and other energy-efficient appliances.27

                                                                                                                               4

Most incentives are contingent upon the installation of heat pumps that meet certain
performance and efficiency standards and the replacement of select fuels and equipment such
as lower-efficiency electric heat or propane and fuel oil systems. Incentives can also be targeted
to different home types. For instance, Energy Trust of Oregon provides rebates for single-family
homeowners, but most incentives require that the heat pump replaces an electric furnace or
resistance heater.28 Additionally, to promote workforce development and control installation
quality, many incentive programs, such as the New York State Clean Heat program by the New
York State Energy Research and Development Authority (NYSERDA), requires that heat pumps
be installed by a “trade ally” or approved contractor.29 Other programs, such as Southern
California Edison, provide incentives directly to installers to pass along to customers, effectively
incentivizing customers to work with approved contractors.30

An growing number of incentive programs also offer additional funding and opportunities for
financing for low-income and qualified households. For example, the Puget Sound Energy
Efficiency Boost program previously provided up to an additional $4,000 for income-qualified
homes to install a heat pump on top of their standard rebates.31 Likewise, Vermont offers
additional rebates for people below a certain income, while New Hampshire offers whole-house
incentives to offset greater portions of energy use.32 Such policies seek not only to incentivize
the adoption of energy-efficient technologies like heat pumps but also to bring down household
energy bills and invest in energy justice. The IRA also provides additional funding for
households below 80% of the area median income (AMI).

Section 3: Description of variables

This section describes the equity and control variables included in our analysis, including their
theoretical basis, data sources, and interpretation (see Table 3.1 for descriptive statistics). The
ZTRAX dataset includes roughly 164 million buildings of which 65 million are residential
buildings that report a heating type. While this reduced sample raises potential concerns around
missing data biases, robustness checks suggest there to be little to no bias introduced as a
result of missing records (see Section 3.11 for the results of this robustness check).

3.1 Median Income

Income can predict household use of energy-efficient and clean energy technologies, many of
which have high up-front costs. Following previous work, we use the logged median annual
household income at the census tract level using table S1901 of the five-year estimates from
the American Community Survey (ACS) for 2016-2020.33–36 To further account for tail effects at
the far upper and lower ends of income, we also include the square of logged income.

3.2 Tenancy Status

Homes are less likely to have energy efficiency and clean energy technologies if they are renter
and not owner occupied, due to the split incentive problem between landlords and tenants.34 We
extract data from ACS table B25002, which classifies every housing unit as either vacant or
occupied, and ACS table B25008, which divides the occupied housing units into renter-occupied
and owner-occupied categories.35 Combining both the tables, we establish the percentage of

                                                                                                                               5

homes that are vacant, owner-occupied, or renter-occupied in each census tract. These
variables are used to estimate the impact of homeownership on heat pump use.

3.3 Race/ethnicity

Race and ethnicity can predict use of energy efficiency and clean energy technologies, even
after controlling for income and tenancy status, indicating possible systematic racial/ethnic
barriers to use of these technologies. We use categories for race/ethnicity defined in ACS table
B03002, which include: White, Black, Hispanic, Asian, Pacific Islander, Native, “Two-or-more,”
and “Other”.35 The Hispanic category refers to those identifying as only Hispanic or Latino or
Hispanic or Latino along with another race category. These values are divided by the total
population in each census tract to calculate the percent of the population that identifies as
different racial/ethnic groups. We use white as the baseline category.

3.4 Building Age

Newer buildings are more likely to use electricity for space heating, whereas older buildings are
more likely to heat with natural gas, oil, propane, or wood.37 We include both the
census-tract-level building age from the five-year estimates from the ACS35 and the individual
year of construction or last code-triggering renovation for each unit included in the ZTRAX
database. The primary analysis utilizes the latter to develop a continuous building age variable.
We also use building age for ACS as a robustness check. ACS reports building age in decade
groupings and coded as dummy variables: pre-1940 construction, 1940-49, 1950-59, 1960-69,
1970-79, 1980-89, 1990-99, 2000-10, and post-2010.38 We use new construction (post-2010) as
the baseline because installing heat pumps in new buildings is widely seen as easier and less
expensive compared to retrofits in older buildings.39

3.5 Electricity prices

Higher electricity prices increase the costs of operating a heat pump as well as an electric
resistance heater, with costs increasing more for electric resistance heaters due to their lower
operating efficiencies (compared to a heat pump). We use electricity price data from EIA form
861, which reports average prices per kWh for residential customers at the utility-county level,
for the years 2016 through 2021.40 Total revenue from residential sales by utility-county were
divided by total kWh sales by utility-county to assess average price for each year. Three Texas
utilities also did not report prices during the period of the analysis (Oncor, CenterPoint, and
Texas-New Mexico). For homes serviced by these utilities, we use average county data from
their FERC form 1 reports (page 300).41 We also cleaned the data by reassigning county names
or boundaries that had changed since the time of the last report (namely in Alaska). Prices were
then normalized to 2020 prices using the EIA national electricity price index. To identify
county-level prices for counties serviced by more than one utility, we calculated a
customer-weighted average electricity price. Electricity prices were subsequently standardized
by mean centering and dividing by their standard deviation.

Prior literature has leveraged state-level average electricity prices to assess the relationship
between electricity costs and heat pump use.42 However, due to the collinearity of other state

                                                                                                                               6

effects (including policies) and state-level prices, such analyses may be biased. By leveraging
sub-state (i.e., county-level) electricity prices, our analysis identifies the role of within-state
electricity price variation in heat pump use. We note that these effects can depend strongly on
the dominant heating technology because an increase in electricity price decreases the relative
benefits of replacing a furnace with a heat pump but increases the relative benefits of replacing
an electric resistance heater with a heat pump. We perform several robustness checks on the
role of electricity prices including: (1) replicating our main analysis without electricity and gas
prices (see Section 8.16), (2) performing an analysis using state-level average electricity prices
for both the ZTRAX and RECs data without including state fixed effects (see Sections 6 and
8.14), and (3) replicating our main analysis separately for regions where either electricity or
other fuels (including natural gas) are the dominant heating technology (see Section 8.15).
These robustness checks provide additional support for analysis.

3.6 Natural gas prices and access

Low natural gas prices make switching to a heat pump comparatively less advantageous. Prior
research shows that operational cost is a significant predictor of household decisions to install a
heat pump, with natural gas price (and access, as 17% of homes do not have gas access)
predicting lower heat pump use.12 First, we map each household in ZTRAX (latitude and
longitude) onto the publicly available natural gas service territories from Homeland Infrastructure
Foundation-Level Data (HIFLD).43 Second, we obtain natural gas prices for each utility boundary
from the EIA-176 forms for 2016 (in USD per thousand cubic foot).44

Approximately 16% of territories were not matched to a record in the EIA-176 form. We used
fuzzy string matching to match additional utility territories to pricing data. We matched
approximately 7% of utility boundaries with strict criteria, which left approximately 9% of utilities
unmatched. For the remaining utilities, we used state average prices reported from EIA44 for
2020. We use standardized prices in our logistic regression models. In addition, we included a
binomial utility gas access variable which was defined as 1 if the household was served by a
natural gas utility service company and 0 otherwise and used this variable in place of gas prices
as a robustness check on our model (see Section 8.8).

3.7 Climate

Heat pumps are less efficient in cold climates, and thus less likely to be cost effective.45
Additionally, they may be less likely to be present in areas with minimal heating needs, where
the benefits of their increased efficiency relative to electric resistance heating are less impactful
compared to the higher upfront cost. We include heating and cooling degree days (HDD and
CDD) in our model to control for heating and cooling requirements. For cooling degree days, the
value reflects the number of days the average outside air temperature of a given location was
above 65°F (the standard temperature used in the U.S. to maintain indoor comfort) multiplied by
the difference between 65°F and the average daily temperature. For heating degree days, the
value reflects the inverse (i.e., days with average temperature below 65°F, when heating is
needed to maintain comfort). Annual HDD and CDD values with a standard reference of 65°F
were obtained from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric (NOAA) 1991-2020 weather normals

                                                                                                                               7

for 7300 weather stations nationwide.46 We then matched each household to the nearest
weather station and assigned the recorded HDD and CDD values.

3.8 State fixed effects

We expect some local fixed effects on heat pump use given differences in the policies, histories,
geographies, markets, and trends of individual states. To control for these effects, we include
state-level fixed effects in our parametric analysis. Owing to reporting differences between
jurisdictions, several states also reported zero heat pumps within their service territory and were
excluded from the analysis. These states include Hawaii, Louisiana, New Jersey, New York, and
Vermont. Alaska was used as the reference state.

3.9 Housing type

To control for differences in household type, we use dummy variables for housing type
designations in the ZTRAX database. We use five different housing types in our analysis: (1)
Single-family, (2) Single-family shared wall, (3) Multi-family, (4) Trailer/Mobile home, and (5)
Other. Multi-family was used as the reference category.

3.11 Missing data robustness check

The ZTRAX data includes the universe of buildings in the U.S. including both residential and
commercial spaces. Of the roughly 164 million units in the dataset, only 65 million provide data
on heating systems. As such, there may be concerns that the results are subject to missing data
bias. Returning to the original, uncleaned data (prior to dropping entries with missing data), we
calculated a Pearson’s correlation coefficient matrix for the rate of missingness in each of the
variables of interest (see Table 3.2). Missingness is approximated by the number of null values
in the original dataset. The correlation coefficients thus represent the relationship between a
missingness and each of the other variables. None of the correlation coefficients for any of the
demographic variables of interest (or electricity prices) are significant, suggesting that our data
cleaning process and the omission of data from our analysis do not ultimately bias our results.
This alongside the analysis of the nationally-representative RECS data provides additional
evidence that the findings in this analysis are not biased by missing data.

Section 6: Replication results

As with many previous studies investigating equity in clean energy technologies,34,47 our study
evaluates inequities at the census tract level. However, it is possible that this approach can
under- or overestimate inequities (for example, if Black households in a location with a higher
proportion of white residents are more likely to use heat pumps than Black households in a
location with a higher proportion of Black residents). To explore this possibility, we replicate our
analysis using the 2020 Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS).48 RECS is a survey
conducted by the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) that includes household-level
variables such as geography, climate, income, household quality, race/ethnicity, and renter
status. The 2020 RECS sample was designed to meet precision requirements on energy
consumption for all 50 states and Washington, DC, with a total of 18,496 respondents.49 We use
RECS data to assess how the use of heat pumps as the main heating equipment correlates with
equity (race/ethnicity, income, and renter status) and other control variables at the household

                                                                  13

level. The model is implemented in R using the survey package,50 recommended by EIA to
capture the replicate weights used in the RECS methodology.51

The results for the relationship between household race/ethnicity and heat pump use are
generally consistent with the results from our main analysis (see Tables 6.1 and 6.2).
Specifically, heat pump use is significantly lower among Black or African American households
than white ones, and among households of Hispanic and Latino descent than
non-Hispanic/Latino households (RECS codes for Hispanic/Latino as a separate ethnicity
variable). This result remains robust when controlling for additional factors: income, renter
status, building type and year, climate, natural gas access, and state (Table 6.1). The direction
of the relationship for other racial categories (except American Indian or Alaska Native) is also
negative, consistent with our main analysis, although it is not statistically significant. In a model
where we included natural gas and electricity prices (Table 6.2), all racial categories are
negatively correlated with heat pump use. Overall, these results suggest that the disparities
observed in our analysis are robust and likely not due to systematic biases in ZTRAX.

The following list elaborates on the variables included in the RECS analysis, their interpretability,
and key differences from the ZTRAX dataset variables, if any:

    1. Race: The racial categories in the RECS are (1) White alone, (2) Black or African
         American alone, (3) American Indian or Alaska Native alone, (4) Asian alone, (5) Native
         Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander alone, and (6) multiracial. As in the main analysis,
         white is used as the baseline group.

    2. Ethnic Descent: RECS delineates Hispanic/Latino descent as a separate variable from
         race. This binary variable is added to all logit models, with 0 referring to
         non-Hispanic/Latino and 1 referring to Hispanic/Latino.

    3. Income: While the median annual income (at the census tract level) in the primary
         analysis is a continuous variable, RECS groups income into 16 ranges, from less than
         $5,000 to $150,000 or more. We use the smallest range as the baseline group.

    4. Building Age: In RECS, building age is grouped into five or ten year ranges from 1950
         to 2020, with an additional pre-1950 category, whereas this variable is continuous in our
         main analysis. The age range closest to present is used as the baseline group.

    5. Housing Type: There are five building types in RECS: (1) mobile home, (2) single-family
         house detached from any other house, (3) single-family house attached to one or more
         other houses (for example, duplex, row house, or townhome), (4) apartment in a building
         with 2 to 4 units, and (5) apartment in a building with 5 or more units. As in the main
         analysis, detached single-family house is used as the baseline group.

    6. Natural Gas Price and Access: RECS includes a dummy variable for natural gas
         access. We use 1 to refer to a household that has natural gas available in underground
         pipes, and 0 otherwise. In another model, we include state level natural gas prices from
         EIA and remove the natural gas access variable.52

    7. State Level Electricity Price: Electricity prices from the EIA reported at the state level
         and included in one of our models. We excluded state fixed effects in models with state
         electricity prices to avoid collinearity issues.

                                                                                                                             14

    8. Other Variables: The remaining variables in RECS are largely similar to our main
         analysis, with minor differences. Tenancy in RECS includes an additional status of
         occupancy without payment of rent.

Section 7: Estimation of heat pump benefits

This section describes our approach to estimating net energy bill savings from switching to a
heat pump relative to the current primary heating fuel for each county in the U.S.

7.1 Data sources
We extract heating technology data from the ACS five-year survey 2016-2020.35 Five primary
fuels are used: (1) utility gas, (2) electricity, (3) bottled, tank, or liquified propane (LP) gas, (4)
fuel oil or kerosene, and (5) wood. We use Typical Meteorological Year (TMY3) weather data
from NOAA53 (reported as hourly temperatures at the county level) and estimate county-level
heating degree hours using a standard reference temperature of 18.3°C (65°F), which is also
used by NOAA to calculate heating and cooling degree days.46

We use energy prices for 2020 at the state level (for natural gas and other fuels) and at the
county level (for electricity). For natural gas, we use annual state average residential prices
reported by EIA (in USD per thousand cubic feet)52 and convert them to USD/kWh using the
state-average heat content of utility gas delivered to customers (in BTU per cubic foot) from
EIA.54 For other fuels, we use residential pricing data inferred by the State Energy Data System
(SEDS)55 at the state level. For fuel oil, we use the prices reported for kerosene. For bottled,
tank, or LP gas, we use the prices provided for propane.

For electricity prices, EIA form 861 gathers electricity prices at the utility level across the US.40
Electricity prices are population-weighted averages per county inferred from EIA form 861 as
the ratio of utility sales (USD) and total energy sold (kWh). For our calculation of net energy bill

                                                                                                                             17

impacts, we assume that counties that currently report electricity as the primary heating source
in ACS use electric resistance heating as the main technology.

7.2 Heating load

We use the heating degree-day method to estimate the annual heating load of a typical
single-family detached house.17 This method uses local heating degree day data (at the hourly
resolution) and a heat loss factor to calculate energy demand for heating, which is the product
of the heat transfer coefficient U and the area A. We focus our analysis on the sign (positive or
negative) of net energy cost savings of using a heat pump in a given location, relative to the
current primary heating technology in that location. Housing characteristics such as insulation
and type of construction can substantially impact heating load. However, heating load affects the
costs of both heat pumps and the comparison technology, and thus differences in heating load
are less likely to change the sign of the net benefits of switching to a heat pump.

We use the RECS 2015 dataset to infer an average UA factor for a single-family, detached
house in the U.S.56 We filter RECS data to include only single-family detached houses, which
includes 3,752 houses. The RECS dataset includes the average total energy use for space
heating (Qavg, 41.69 million BTU per year) and average annual heating degree days (HDDavg,
3,773°F-day, with a reference of 65°F). We calculate the UA factor as follows:

7.5 Uncertainty analysis

Many inputs that impact the net bill savings of heat pumps relative to other heating technologies
are subject to uncertainty, including energy prices and device efficiencies. To assess the effects
of these uncertainties on the sign of the net benefits, we use a Monte Carlo simulation. We use
the following probability distributions for parameters in the analysis (with the exception of typical
meteorological data, which for simplicity is deterministic in our analysis):

    ● Heat pump efficiency. We use a triangular distribution for the slope of the heat pump
         COP function with minimum, average, and maximum slope values of 0.03, 0.05, and
         0.07, respectively, taken from the NEEP database. This yields a COP no higher than 5
         and no less than 3 for an operating temperature of 47°F. The COP value can fall below 3
         for operating temperatures lower than 47°F.

    ● Other heating device efficiencies. For natural gas, propane, fuel oil, and wood
         heaters, we use triangular distributions for device efficiencies. We use a minimum
         efficiency of 78% for natural gas, propane and fuel oil based on the first Federal
         standard (established in 1992)60 for residential furnaces and 58% for wood (based on the
         EPA-Certified wood stove database). We use an average efficiency of 80% for natural
         gas and propane furnaces, 83% for fuel oil furnaces (based on the 2015 Federal
         standard for residential furnaces), and 79% for wood space heaters (based on the
         average efficiency in the EPA-certified wood stove database). We use a maximum
         efficiency of 99% for natural gas and propane, 96.7% for fuel oil furnaces (based on the
         highest efficiency Energy Star certified furnaces database),61 and 90% for wood space
         heaters (based on the maximum efficiency in the EPA-certified wood stove database).

    ● Fuel prices. We use national average residential fuel prices from 2000 to 2020 reported
         by EIA,62 relative to 2020, to calculate uncertainty ranges for fuel prices at the state
         (natural gas, propane, fuel oil, and wood) and county (electricity) level. We calculate the

                                                               20

         coefficient of variation in the time range for each fuel and use it to construct triangular
         distributions by setting an uncertainty range as a percentage from 2020 prices in each
         county. The price coefficients of variation using two standard deviations are: 30% for
         natural gas, 29% for electricity, 21% for propane, 79% for fuel oil, and 68% for wood.
We simulate 10,000 random samples for our Monte Carlo simulation and separate counties into
three categories: (1) net benefits, if more than 90% of realizations yield energy savings, (2)
neutral, when energy savings occur less than 90% and more than 10% of realizations, and (3)
net costs, if energy savings occur in less than 10% of realizations. The results include 1749,
736, and 623 counties in the net benefits, neutral, and net costs zones.

7.6 Installation costs
We focus on the net benefis of heat pump operation (in the form of changes in energy bills).
However, installation costs may also affect the net benefits of new heat pump adoption. We
compare the costs of installing a heat pump to conventional heating technologies. We construct
random triangular distributions using data from the National Residential Efficiency Measures
Database (adjusted to $2020 USD)8, which contains minimum, typical, and maximum installation
costs for the U.S. While this data is only available as a national average, it includes the cost of
labor and electrical components (e.g., wiring). We compare the installation costs of a heat pump
against natural gas, propane, and oil furnaces and an electric resistance (baseboard) heater
(see Figure 7.1). Since a heat pump can function both as a heating and cooling device, we
compare the costs of each heating device plus the cost of installing a central air conditioning
(AC) system.1 The heat pump, heating devices, and air conditioning capacities are assumed to

Section 8: Additional regression results

We perform several robustness checks for our analysis. We first summarize the results of these
robustness checks and then provide tables with the results. Generally, these robustness checks
(in addition to our replication results using the Residential Energy Consumption Survey [RECS]
presented in Section 6) provide evidence that our analysis and policy takeaways are largely
robust to details or potential limitations of our data and modeling approach.

We run a series of analyses where we sequentially add variables to our model. We begin with a
basic national model that includes race/ethnicity, climate, energy prices, and state dummies
(Section 8.1) and sequentially add income (Section 8.2), building age (Section 8.3), tenancy
status (Section 8.4), home value (Section 8.5), and housing type (Section 8.6). We also run an
additional analysis that replaces the continuous building age variable with a categorical variable
for buildings built each decade from pre-1940s to the 2000s from the American Community
Survey (Section 8.7) and an analysis that replaces natural gas price with a dummy variable for
natural gas access (Section 8.8). We also run analyses that consider interaction terms for
race/ethnicity and income (Section 8.9), race/ethnicity and tenancy status (Section 8.10),
race/ethnicity and building age (Section 8.11), and race/ethnicity and income, tenancy status,
and building age simultaneously (Section 8.12). These alternative model specifications do not
change the direction of our key results on race/ethnicity, income, and renter status, except in
cases where the direction of main coefficients changes due to the introduction of interaction
terms (e.g., interaction with Hispanic, Multiracial, and other race in Sections 8.9 and 8.12).

                                                                                   22

We consider the inclusion of state fixed effects in our model to be important given the potential
influence of state factors (including policies) on heat pump use. However, we also perform
logistic regressions without state dummy variables (see Section 8.13) and with state-level rather
than county-level electricity and natural gas prices (see Section 8.14). Notably, this model has a
lower pseudo R2 value (0.125 compared to 0.333 for our main model). Most of the coefficients
for race/ethnicity remain directionally robust compared to our results using state dummies
except for Black (where the direction of the effect is positive without state fixed effects) and
Native American (where the direction of the effect is negative without state fixed effects). This
can be explained by the fact that state fixed effects also control for geographical bias in
race/ethnicity composition (specifically, there are higher Black populations in the U.S.
Southeast, where heat pump penetration is also higher). Our analysis is focused on identifying
racial/ethnic differences in heat pump use after controlling for these differences. While the
direction of the effects of electricity and natural gas prices remains the same when removing
state fixed effects, the relationship with electricity price flips (from positive to negative) when
including state rather than county electricity prices. We similarly attribute this change to potential
confounding between electricity prices and other state effects (e.g., policies).

To further explore the relationships between energy prices and heat pump use, we also run
separate analyses for regions where electricity or other fuels (including natural gas) are
dominant with (Section 8.15) and without (Section 8.16) including energy prices. In regions
where electricity is the main heating fuel (assumed to be electric resistance heating), we find
that there is a significant and positive relationship between electricity price and heat pump use,
as in our main analysis. In regions mainly heated by other fuels, we observe the opposite (i.e., a
significant and negative relationship between electricity prices and heat pump use). This
reinforces our observation that there is a greater incentive to switch to a heat pump with higher
electricity prices when electric resistance heating is dominant, whereas there is less incentive
when other fuels (e.g., natural gas) are dominant. We also perform an additional regression on
our national dataset by removing both electricity and natural gas prices. Although energy prices
are an important factor influencing heat pump use, we observe no substantial differences in our
sociodemographic variables compared to our national regression including energy prices.
Nonetheless, the nuanced and nonlinear relationship between electricity prices and heat pump
use is complex and warrants further exploration in future work.

Finally, we run analyses using more granular data at the census block group level instead of the
more aggregated census tract level, for both our national (Section 8.17) and regional (Section
8.18) results. Our results with census block-group data do not show meaningful differences from
our main results using census tract data, suggesting that our findings are not primarily driven by
aggregation biases at this level. We also note that not all census block groups report income,
resulting in approximately 50 million buildings in our census block group analysis (compared to
64 million in our main analysis). At the national level, coefficient directions remain unchanged
when compared to our main analysis using census tract data with minor differences in
magnitude. At the regional level, results are overall qualitatively the same as our main analysis,
with a few differences. For instance, income coefficients have a larger magnitude in the net
benefits region. However, the coefficient is still larger in the net costs regions, which is the same

                                                                                                                             23

trend when using census tract data (see Figure 5 in the main paper). Other coefficients that are
not significant in our main analysis, such as renter status in the neutral region, change direction
from positive to negative when using census block groups. As with our main analysis, missing
data at the census block group level does not correlate with other variables such as heat pump
use, race/ethnicity, and renter status (with the largest coefficient equal to 0.08).
