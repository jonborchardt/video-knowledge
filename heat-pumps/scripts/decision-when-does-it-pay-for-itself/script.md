# Does a Heat Pump Ever Pay for Itself?

## Hook

A common pitch for a heat pump ends the same way: it pays for itself. One HVAC company, Atlas AC, tells its viewers that the extra cost of converting a gas system "could be offset in savings by year five". [s02 ok] In a Skill Builder video about a British home, the verdict is even quicker: "So, four years you got your money back". [s03 ok] Then the Washington Post ran the numbers for one gas-heated house with a basic heat pump, and got a very different answer, "making the payback period around 48 years". [s04 ok] That is not a rounding error. So which homes get their money back from a heat pump, how fast, and which ones never do?

## Setup

Pitches like these describe payback as if it were a property of the machine. Even an electrification group that promotes heat pumps is more careful, telling readers that "a heat pump will often pay off in about 15 years". [s08 ok] And one British owner who publishes detailed running data, Tom Bray, concluded that "a simple calculation would suggest around 18 to 25 years". [s09 ok] Every one of those numbers could be right for the house it describes. The question is what happens across millions of houses, so that is where we will look: at national models that run the numbers home by home, and at meters in real homes.

## Investigation

Start with a national laboratory model that simulated about 550,000 representative American homes. [s12 ok] Over the life of the equipment and without any subsidy, compared with replacing the old equipment like for like when it wears out, 59 percent came out ahead if every home picked its single best option. [s14 ok] But no real buyer gets to pick with perfect hindsight, and no single heat pump did that well: the cheapest, minimum-efficiency units came out ahead in 55 percent of homes, and the premium cold-climate units in only 21 percent, because their higher price ate the extra savings. [s15 ok] A second national model of single-family houses was harsher still. It found a heat pump would pay off for about 16.7 million houses, 21 percent of all single-family homes, at the energy prices it assumed, again counting only the extra cost over replacing the old heater with a similar one when it nears the end of its life. [s17 ok] So both figures are already the good-timing case: the heat pump bought at the moment the old equipment would have had to be replaced anyway. [s62 ok] So is it a majority, or a minority? A third team, mapping energy bills county by county, found 53 percent of households would see lower bills, 19 percent higher ones, and 28 percent no clear change either way. [s19 ok] Even the most generous lifetime figure here, the 59 percent, is a thin majority, and it assumes every home picks its best option. [s20 ok] If only a slim majority of homes come out ahead over the whole life of the equipment, a payback of a few years cannot be the typical case. [s21 ok] Which raises the more useful question: who is in the winning group? The national lab model names one of the biggest factors, and it is what you heat with today. [s23 ok] For homes that now heat with electricity, fuel oil or propane and already have air conditioning, the national lab model found that almost all would see lower bills, with median savings of $300 to $650 a year. [s24 ok] For gas-heated homes with air conditioning, it depended on the equipment: anywhere from 38 to 99 percent saved on their bills. [s25 ok] The second model put it more bluntly: switching from natural gas "rarely produces a benefit", especially in cold climates. [s26 ok] On oil, the models disagree. The national lab model, counting the cost of the equipment, found a basic heat pump came out ahead over its life for most homes heated with fuel oil; the second model found a switch from oil "usually causes the homeowner a private economic loss", in part because nearly 65 percent of oil-heated houses are in cold climates. [s29 ok] Meters in New England homes found ductless heat pumps "always operate less expensively than propane heating systems", and cheaper to run than oil too, except on very cold days. [s30 ok] Both of those national figures are already the good-timing case; the field data show what it costs to switch before the old equipment is due. [s31 ok] A Minnesota study priced a cold-climate heat pump with a propane furnace as backup at $9,450 installed, using a national cost database. [s32 ok] Installed while the old furnace and air conditioner still worked, its simple payback came to 14.5 years against an older furnace and 28.8 years against an efficient condensing one, longer than the 18 years the study expects the equipment to last. [s33 ok] Installed when both had failed and needed replacing anyway, the extra cost fell to just $370, and the payback to between 0.6 and 1.1 years. [s34 ok] Same house, same machine, same bills. The only thing that changed was what you would have had to buy anyway. [s36 ok] The project's later report, from the same Minnesota research, put it plainly: for retrofits, paybacks will typically run "longer than 10 years, unless equipment replacement is necessary", and drop to 5 to 6 years when either the heating or the cooling system has failed. [s37 ok] A model of gas-heated homes in Chicago, Providence, Houston and Oakland, at the energy prices it used, found the same pattern. Switching paid off for gas customers who "would otherwise need to replace both a furnace and air conditioner simultaneously". [s39 ok] Otherwise, existing gas homes that switched both heating and water heating to electric either paid more for energy, in colder Chicago and Providence, or saved too little to make up the extra cost, in Houston and Oakland. [s40 ok]

## Findings

Put the pieces together and a pattern appears. If you are leaving propane or electric resistance heat, a heat pump usually pays. [s42 ok] Buying when the old equipment is due for replacement is the case the national figures already describe; in the Minnesota example, with a propane furnace as backup, replacing a failed furnace and air conditioner together cut the payback from decades to about a year. [s43 ok] And if you are tearing out a working gas furnace, the models mostly say it will not pay back on energy savings alone. [s44 ok]

## Qualifications

There are things that move these numbers. The first is insulation: for older or leaky homes, Consumer Reports says "it’s always a good idea to first upgrade your insulation and seal any air leaks". [s46 ok] In one group of homes the national lab model examined, sealing and insulating let the medium- and high-efficiency heat pumps be smaller, cutting their installed cost by $2,500 to $4,000. [s47 ok] But the insulation work itself added about $9,000 in that group, so the total upfront cost went up. [s48 ok] It did cut the share of homes whose bills went up with the cheapest units, from 39 percent to 19 percent, and it nudged the lifetime numbers up slightly in cold climates while lowering them in warmer ones. [s63 ok] The second is your own house: the model found the spread between homes in the same place was bigger than the spread between places. [s49 ok] And the savings the model finds depend on local climate and energy prices, so an average is not a forecast for your house. [s50 ok]

## Implications

So what should you do with this? If you heat with propane or electric baseboards, you are in the group where the models here found the biggest savings, so getting quotes is worth your time. [s53 ok] If your furnace or air conditioner is near the end of its life, that is the moment the extra cost of a heat pump shrinks the most. [s54 ok] And if both are working fine and you heat with gas, switching early is a worse case than anything these models priced; even at replacement time, the second model found gas homes rarely came out ahead, while the national lab model found most gas homes with central air in warmer climates did. [s55 ok] In the model of Chicago, Providence, Houston and Oakland, switching paid off for gas homes that needed a new furnace and air conditioner at the same time, or that paired it with rooftop solar. [s64 ok] Whatever a seller promises, ask one question: payback compared with what, a new furnace and air conditioner, or nothing at all?

## Conclusion

So, does a heat pump pay for itself? For some homes, quickly; for many, never: even when bought at replacement time, one national model found a lifetime gain for 21 percent of all single-family houses, the other for at most 55 percent of homes with any single heat pump. [s58 ok] Which group you are in depends on the fuel you are leaving, the prices where you live, and whether you buy when your old equipment needs replacing anyway. [s59 ok] A quick payback is possible. It just is not a promise anyone can make about your house before looking at it.
