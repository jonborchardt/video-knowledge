# What happens to a heat pump when it gets really cold [meta.headline fp=d70f7b8f5d]

Story `decision:what-happens-when-it-gets-cold` (heat-pumps), revision 2. SEO title: Do heat pumps work in extreme cold? The field data [meta.seoTitle fp=88df965905]. Validation: **passed**, 0 blocking, 56 warnings, 64/64 assertions verified.

> In field tests in cold-climate homes, most metered heat pumps kept heating below 0 °F, though less efficiently, and the cost risk was backup heat, which ran more as it got colder. [meta.dek ok fp=a506907acf]

Description: What metered field tests show about heat pump efficiency, output and backup heat below 0 °F, and why gas can still be cheaper to run. [meta.description ok fp=69bb7c7fec]

## Does a heat pump still heat the house on the coldest nights? [heading:hook fp=304a9ab117]

Picture a heat pump in a northern backyard on the coldest night of the winter, frost on the windows and its fan still turning. [s01 fp=b75604349b] Is it still warming the house, or has it quietly handed the job to an expensive electric heater? [s02 fp=8d24ffe230] Field studies that put meters on heat pumps in lived-in homes can answer that, and they also show which part of a cold winter matters most for the bill. [s03 fp=299fb4b146]

## Who says heat pumps fail in the cold, and who says they do not [heading:setup fp=84a62b63f2]

An NH Patch headline in New Hampshire framed the doubt as "Taxpayers On The Hook For Tech That Falls Flat In The Cold". [s04 ok fp=fb090d7645] Massachusetts' Mass Save program says the opposite of heat pumps, that "many provide efficient heat at outdoor temperatures as low as -15 °F". [s05 ok fp=5c47a0a658] Between those views, the channel Technology Connections names what actually changes: "along with getting less efficient, their actual heat output also drops." [s06 ok fp=c940e6617c] And Jordan Dearden warns of the electric backup strips that take over when the heat pump cannot keep up: "They'll also absolutely destroy your electric bill." [s07 ok fp=b98ad947c2] Which of these claims do the meters bear out? [s08 fp=87077bd358]

**unresolved (gap 0):** A building-economics report from the Rocky Mountain Institute is sometimes cited on this point, but its line on how cold today's models can operate rests on product ratings, not on tests of units in the cold, so it cannot settle the question. [s09 ok fp=472d51b340]

## What the meters show below freezing [heading:investigation fp=6bc493c688]

Heating efficiency is scored as a coefficient of performance, or COP, and plain electric resistance heat scores 1. [s10 ok fp=5b229b1c23] A New England evaluation metered the heat delivered by dozens of ductless mini-splits over back-to-back winters, and in the later winter they averaged a COP of 1.5 between -10 and 0 °F. [s11 ok fp=46d3c99c45] The evaluators called that far superior to resistance heat, with heating efficiency 50 percent higher; in the earlier winter, with deep snow and ice around the outdoor units, the same units ran less efficiently. [s12 ok fp=80b3c74ace] Even the ordinary models in that evaluation, not built for cold climates, kept running below 0 °F, though less efficiently than the cold-climate ones. [s13 ok fp=365e3867da] In a federal field test of prototype cold-climate heat pumps in occupied homes, the median COP between 0 and 5 °F was 1.9, counting only the periods when neither backup heat nor defrost was running. [s14 ok fp=916b6b3927] Most of those units cleared 1.75, the COP Energy Star requires of a cold-climate heat pump at 5 °F, although the prototypes had done significantly better in the lab. [s15 ok fp=4fdfc6a462] A study of whole-home systems in Maine found the metered COP across 166 indoor units averaged 2 at -10 °F, which it describes as twice as efficient as electric resistance even at those temperatures. [s16 ok fp=df396edf07]

So the heat keeps coming at lower efficiency, and how much heat a unit can still deliver is a separate question. [s17 fp=7af5b36da7] In northern Minnesota, researchers logged one small ductless unit holding a steady median output from 10 °F down to -15 °F. [s18 ok fp=1b79339177] Below that it kept running at reduced output, and between -20 and -25 °F it delivered about 20 percent of its rated output at 5 °F. [s19 ok fp=30cdaa517d] The researchers concluded that units like it should not be used without backup below -15 °F. [s20 ok fp=4d6ec904ec] Efficiency falls the same way: in an earlier Minnesota study, outdoor temperature had the largest effect on COP, and heat-pump-only cycles managed around 1.3 at 10 °F against around 3.0 to 4.5 on mild days of 50 to 60 °F. [s21 ok fp=bad5a9cb43]

Figure decision-what-happens-when-it-gets-cold/sc14: Heat-pump-only COP in the Minnesota study, at the colder change point and on mild shoulder-season days. [decision-what-happens-when-it-gets-cold/sc14 fp=aa65e99b74]

**unresolved (heat-pumps/heat-pump-efficiency-capacity-fall-outdoor):** In both the Maine and the Minnesota studies efficiency fell as the temperature fell, but the field output readings at the coldest temperatures come from a single ductless unit, so how much capacity a given model keeps is not settled by this evidence. [s22 ok fp=90acc97653]

Then there is frost. [s23 fp=551787141e] In cold, humid air, frost builds on the outdoor coil and cuts its heat exchange; to clear it, the heat pump reverses and draws heat from the house, which can cool the air coming out of the vents. [s24 ok fp=96e75fd5aa] In the federal field test, defrost took a median of 5 to 8 percent of the energy at 5 to 35 °F, where it cost most, but one site spent 16 percent of its time in defrost below 40 °F against an average of 5 percent. [s25 ok fp=d9b27ccf47] Which leaves backup heat. [s26 fp=ae4e3c7f3b] In the same field test the units ran mostly on the heat pump alone, even at the lowest temperatures, and backup-only operation was minimal above 0 °F. [s27 ok fp=5ae419917e] Below that it was another matter: in the coldest range with enough data, -10 to 0 °F, backup elements were on 25 percent of the time, though only 8 and 11 of the 22 sites had data in the two coldest ranges. [s28 ok fp=8a3e4e44a4] The researchers caution that three out of the four sites with data at the very coldest temperatures used more backup heat at every temperature than the rest, so those readings may overstate a typical home. [s29 ok fp=ee025c48f2] Some of the backup use came from the thermostat: switchover to backup was seen at around 30 to 35 °F, and the researchers expect it would start much lower without homeowners changing setpoints, or with better setpoint controls. [s30 ok fp=3051019c3f] Some came from faults: two sites fired higher stages of backup heat during defrost, at least one did so in normal heating too, and the manufacturer called that a controls problem, which the researchers expect to be fixed before the units go on sale. [s31 ok fp=9a3c3795d7] In another field study, one site's compressor would not run below 0 °F at all, even with its lockout disabled, likely because of an on-board control setting that cannot be adjusted in the field. [s32 ok fp=f42cccae34] Backup does not have to erase the gain: in a Minnesota all-electric system, resistance booster heat joined in below 30 °F, but because the heat pump ran first, the system COP stayed above 1.0 even at the coldest reading, around -7 °F. [s33 ok fp=3ac8d7ea05] Sizing matters too: one study estimated that in Minnesota a high-efficiency heat pump sized for the full heating load cut median bills by $182 a year, while one sized for the cooling load raised them by $96, because it used more electric resistance backup. [s34 ok fp=f334517212]

**qualification (heat-pumps/cold-climate-heat-pumps-often-rely):** How much a heat pump leans on electric backup depends on the installation: in the Maine whole-home study, the extra heat that rose below 10 °F came mostly from fossil fuel, and although 13 percent of homes had some electric resistance heat, only one home regularly used it. [s35 ok fp=a62e3f69a4] Across these studies, backup ran more as it got colder, where a unit was sized for cooling, and where controls misbehaved. [s36 ok fp=ecf73ea711]

What does all of that do to the heating bill? [s37 fp=c54e3d7ed5] For Minnesota homes that had heated with propane or electricity, researchers combined performance maps, heating loads and typical weather to estimate cost savings of 30 to 56 percent. [s38 ok fp=4980217057] Those are running costs: for a retrofit, the same researchers expect paybacks longer than 10 years unless the old equipment needs replacing anyway. [s39 ok fp=b5d1b603c5] Against propane, the New England mini-splits were cheaper to run at every temperature. [s40 ok fp=dcbfb5d460] Against gas they were not: at the energy prices of those winters, they beat an 80 percent efficient gas system only above 70 °F, and the evaluators said they were no direct replacement for gas, though that analysis leaves out zonal savings, and heating 30 percent less of the home would bring the break-even down to 50 °F. [s41 ok fp=e57ad0a8b4] The units listed as cold-climate models broke even with gas only above 58 °F; on paper, the rating curve of a then-current cold-climate model lowered that to 40 °F in the earlier winter and 28 °F in the later one. [s42 ok fp=69310d46aa]

A Canadian cost model that counts equipment as well as energy also finds that the answer turns on place: in Toronto, standard heat pumps with gas backup were the lowest-cost option for nearly all households, but in Edmonton only for some house types, and gas with air conditioning won the rest. [s43 ok fp=925677d9f8] The model put Edmonton's result down to cheap gas and the cold itself, which makes heat pumps less efficient and calls on the backup more. [s44 ok fp=2feb1237a7]

**unresolved (gap 1):** Whether a heat pump with a gas furnace as backup is cheaper to run than either alone is not decided by any study here: the New England evaluation found gas cheaper below 70 °F for the units it measured, and the Canadian model counts lifetime costs, equipment included, not running costs alone. [s45 ok fp=a565760351]

## So, do heat pumps still work, and still save money, in real cold? [heading:answer fp=6d96ec7d40]

In these field tests most units kept heating below 0 °F, less efficiently than in mild weather, with less output in the single unit whose output was measured that cold, and still got more heat from each unit of electricity than resistance heat would. [s46 ok fp=cd7b961689] The cost risk is the backup heat described above. [s47 ok fp=b7a05da017] On running costs, the studies here favour a heat pump over propane or electric heat, though retrofit paybacks are long; against gas the running-cost answer turned on the unit and local prices, and for the mini-splits measured in New England gas won, while a lifetime-cost model that counts equipment finds the answer differs from city to city. [s48 ok fp=2cabc2cb97]

**unresolved (heat-pumps/heat-pumps-not-work-cold-climates):** That answer rests on small samples, dozens of metered mini-splits, 22 prototype sites and a single ductless unit for the coldest readings, so it shows what heat pumps can do in the cold, not what every installation will. [s49 ok fp=e545942f0a]

**unresolved (heat-pumps/modern-cold-climate-heat-pumps-keep):** Whether modern cold-climate units keep heating efficiently well below 0 °F is not settled either: the prototypes mostly ran on the heat pump alone, but one ducted site's compressor stopped below 0 °F, and the Minnesota researchers wanted backup below -15 °F. [s50 ok fp=399dcbb5d3]

**unresolved (heat-pumps/heat-pump-still-uses-less-energy):** Nor do the studies show that every heat pump beats resistance heat by a wide margin in deep cold: the ordinary models in the New England evaluation ran less efficiently there than the cold-climate ones, and a study of bill savings notes that a single-stage unit’s COP commonly falls to 1 below 5 °F, no better than resistance heat. [s51 ok fp=6ae9c82bdb]

**unresolved (heat-pumps/defrost-cycles-reduce-heat-pump-output):** What defrost really costs is not measured here: the field test counted the energy used during defrost, which its authors say is not a perfect indicator of the penalty, because a unit that runs backup heat during defrost can lower the heating needed just after it, while one that does not leaves an extra load they could not separate from ordinary heating. [s52 ok fp=077939df3a]

**unresolved (gap 2):** Many sources point to Scandinavia as proof that heat pumps cope with cold; Simon Clark tells doubters to "just ask Scandinavians in Norway Finland and Sweden". [s53 ok fp=6520eabd72] A Canadian report adds that Norway, with average temperatures like Canada's, leads the world in heat pump use and cites studies of their real-world performance, but no study in this evidence measured those homes, so it cannot decide how well heat pumps serve cold countries. [s54 ok fp=4924b907a9]

## What to ask before buying a heat pump for a cold climate [heading:takeaway fp=a1bab18ae9]

Ask whether the unit on the quote is a cold-climate model, and whether it is sized to carry your heating load rather than only your summer cooling. [s55 fp=0eb08b718d] Find out at what outdoor temperature the backup is set to come on, leave the thermostat alone once it is set, and keep an eye on how often the backup runs through the winter. [s56 fp=c9b1a8f728] If you already heat with gas, work out the break-even temperature at your own gas and electricity prices before deciding what the furnace should still do. [s57 fp=2ee61392e3]
