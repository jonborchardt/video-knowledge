# What a Heat Pump Really Costs to Install, and What Help Is Left

## Hook

Say your furnace gives out in January, and the quotes land on the kitchen table: a new furnace, or a heat pump, and the heat pump is the bigger number. Is that the real price gap, or are the quotes pricing different jobs? And once you know the price, is any of the federal heat pump money you have heard about still there to help?

## What does a heat pump really cost compared with what you would buy anyway?

The channel Atlas AC puts the usual view plainly: "for upfront cost the furnace is going to be the cheapest the heat pump is going to be the most expensive". [s04 ok] EnergySage says nearly the opposite: "Installing a heat pump often costs less than simply swapping in a new AC and furnace or boiler". [s05 ok] The difference is what you compare the heat pump with: a furnace on its own, or a furnace plus the air conditioner beside it. Start with the furnace alone: in one database of recorded HVAC jobs, traditional fuel-fired heating systems averaged $3,000 less than heat pump installations, though a heat pump also does the cooling. [s07 ok] Another analysis put the extra cost of replacing a house's existing heater with a heat pump, rather than with the same kind of heater, at an average of $6,600. [s08 ok] Now count the air conditioner. One study's median installed costs put a heat pump at $6,185, just $270 below a gas furnace with central air, and most of their price ranges overlap. [s10 ok] The first database's report points the same way: in its model projects for homes with no cooling, an all-electric heating, cooling and hot-water upgrade cost more than a gas one, but the gap was almost exactly the cost of adding central air on its own. [s11 ok] A Canadian model disagrees on the upfront price: it found heat pumps cost more upfront than gas heating with air conditioning, and come out the lowest-cost option for most households anyway, thanks to their efficiency and doubling as the cooling. [s12 ok] Another study adds a catch: heat pumps designed to carry all of a home's heating are often more expensive to install than an air conditioner plus gas furnace, because bigger heating loads need bigger heat pumps, new wiring and sometimes panel upgrades. [s13 ok] If you only need a new air conditioner, the gap shrinks: that study says wholesale heat pumps cost only $200 to $500 more than air conditioners of the same size. [s14 ok] A cold-climate report quoting NREL's cost database put an efficient heat pump just $370 above an equally efficient central air conditioner. [s15 ok] The study from earlier disagrees, though: its installed medians put the heat pump about 32 percent above central air alone. [s16 ok] And real installs spread widely: in the first database, a heat pump's price depended most on its size and type, the home's floor area, the efficiency and the climate zone, and ground-source systems cost far more than other types. [s17 ok] Then there is the electrical work: in a field test of cold-climate heat pump prototypes at 21 sites, 38 percent needed a new panel. [s18 ok] Where electrical upgrades were needed, they alone cost between $3,750 and $8,970 in labour and materials, before the heat pump itself. [s19 ok] That was a small test, and a typical panel upgrade on its own is put at $1,954 in the first database's report, plus the wiring for each new dedicated circuit. [s20 ok] So against a furnace alone, a heat pump costs more; against a new furnace and air conditioner together it can come out about even, unless it has to be sized up to carry all the heating, and the wiring can add to either. [s21 ok] So what help is left to bring those prices down: which tax credits and rebates still exist?

## What heat pump tax credits and rebates are left in 2026?

In an earlier video, The Thought Provocateur told viewers "technically these heat pump tax credits based on the bill that was signed are available through 2033". [s23 ok] That credit was real: from 2023 it was worth 30 percent of the cost of a qualifying heat pump, capped at $2,000 a year. [s24 ok] But it was cut short: the IRS says the credit is not allowed for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025, so a heat pump installed after that date cannot claim it. [s25 ok] The Rebate Report pointed viewers elsewhere: "the tax credit for heat pumps expired, but the rebate program for heat pumps survived". [s26 ok] Those federal rebates, though, were aimed at low- to moderate-income households and run by each state's energy office. [s27 ok] And some states were slow to start: in one state-by-state tracker, Alabama, Alaska and Arkansas had their applications only conditionally approved, with no expected launch date. [s28 ok] Owen Marsh, in a video on what survived, counted "12 states and the District of Columbia" with programs launched as of June. [s29 ok] And on May 29, 2026, the Department of Energy changed the rules: the rebate no longer pays to replace gas or other fossil-fuel equipment, only to upgrade existing electric equipment. [s30 ok] North Carolina's energy office, for one, says its program had to make the change by September 1, 2026. [s31 ok] Some state and utility programs are generous: in Massachusetts, Mass Save's whole-home rebate is $8,500, or $16,000 at the income-based level, against an average install of $22,000 in its 2022 data. [s32 ok] But that is one program: in the first database, which predates today's rules, the median total rebate across its projects was $1,327, about 21 percent of gross project cost. [s33 ok] And a Canadian model found heat pumps there still cost more upfront despite rebates meant to bring capital costs down. [s34 ok] So for a heat pump installed in 2026 there is no federal tax credit, the federal rebate no longer covers switching from gas, and where rebates exist, they usually trim the price rather than erase it. [s35 ok]

## Close

So before you compare quotes, decide what you would otherwise buy: a furnace alone, or a furnace and an air conditioner. Ask every installer up front whether your panel and service can carry the heat pump, and check your state energy office and your utility for rebates before you sign.
