## Hook

Picture neighbours on the same street who put in heat pumps the same autumn: by February, one has a smaller heating bill and the other a bigger one. Neither of them is lying. So is a heat pump actually cheaper to run than a gas furnace, and does it ever pay for itself?

## Is a heat pump cheaper to run than a gas furnace?

The channel Atlas AC makes one case: heat pumps "are more energy efficient than gas systems and should be able to save you $300 to $500 in energy cost each year". [s04 ok] Jonathan Tracey, comparing his own heat pump's costs with gas, says the opposite: "when you're told that having a heat pump will save you money, the reality is for most people, that is not true". [s05 ok] Guy Martin, testing a heat pump on his channel, put the catch in one line: "Trouble is, electric's four times the price of gas, so it's all meeting the middle." [s06 ok] So which wins: the efficiency, or the price? The arithmetic fits in one line. Divide the price of electricity by the price of gas, for the same amount of energy, and multiply by the furnace's efficiency: a heat pump whose average efficiency over the season, its seasonal COP, beats that number heats the home for less. [s09 ok] At 2024 US average prices, electricity cost 3.45 times as much as gas for the same energy, so a heat pump needed a seasonal COP of 3.28 to match a high-efficiency furnace. [s10 ok] State by state, that bar moves: in 11 states, Florida and Texas among them, a seasonal COP of 2.5 beats that same furnace, while in most of the rest it takes 3.0 or more, in places from California to New York. [s11 ok] Those are averages, and the rule leaves things out: it ignores time-of-use rates and backup resistance heat, and an average gas price includes the fixed monthly charge, which a home that keeps its gas hookup still pays and one that disconnects saves. [s12 ok] So where you live sets the bar. Does the heat pump you buy clear it? Researchers at the national laboratories NREL and Lawrence Berkeley estimated bills, at the energy prices of winter 2021 to 2022, for American homes that heat with gas and already have air conditioning, and the share that would save ranged from 38 percent to 99 percent, depending on the heat pump's performance level. [s14 ok] For gas homes without air conditioning, the shares were lower still, partly because those homes start paying to cool with the heat pump. [s15 ok] And those shares move with energy prices: at 2019 prices, the researchers found, fewer gas homes would save. [s16 ok] What happens in real homes? In Britain, a study of homes that replaced a gas boiler with a heat pump found their total energy use fell by 40 percent, yet because electricity costs more per kilowatt-hour than gas, the running costs came out slightly higher. [s18 ok] At Octopus Energy's standard prices in June 2024, that came to £190 a year more than the gas boiler, though a home that cuts off its gas connection saves about £100 a year of that: a British result at British prices, not a forecast for an American furnace. [s19 ok] The same working paper then looked at Cosy, Octopus's own tariff that prices electricity by the time of day, and estimated that customers who switched to it saved money against Octopus's standard tariff, most of it from shifting their use into cheaper hours. [s21 ok] In the US, an RMI model of Houston found a tariff is not enough by itself: a standard heat pump on a free-nights plan cost more than on a flat rate, and only one controlled to heat and cool ahead while power was free saved, around $1,000 in net present cost. [s22 ok] So is a heat pump cheaper to run than gas? For a gas home that already has air conditioning and gets a well-performing heat pump, the national model says it usually is; with a weaker unit, it often is not, and the more your electricity costs next to your gas, the better the heat pump has to be. [s23 ok] And if you have heard that a heat pump halves the bill, against gas the evidence here does not back it: the British homes cut their energy use sharply and still paid more. [s25 ok] But running cost is only half of the decision; the other half is whether those savings ever pay back the price of the heat pump.

## Does a heat pump pay for itself, and does it matter when you buy?

The pitch is that they do, and fast: Atlas AC tells viewers that "the increased cost to convert a gas system to a heat pump could be offset in savings by year five". [s27 ok] Now picture your own gas furnace: it is not new, but it still works. Is that pitch true for you? The national laboratory model, and a second study by Deetjen and colleagues, set the comparison up carefully: they count only what a heat pump costs beyond replacing the old heater with a similar one, at the end of its life. [s30 ok] Even on that footing, the national laboratory model found the best single option, a cheaper minimum-efficiency heat pump, came out ahead before subsidies in 55 percent of homes, and the more efficient models cost more up front, which cut the share to 41 and 21 percent. [s31 ok] That group takes in most homes heated with oil, propane or electricity, and, of the gas homes, most of those with central air in a warmer climate. [s32 ok] Deetjen's study found that only 21 percent of American single-family houses would gain economically from replacing their heater with a heat pump, and that switching from natural gas rarely pays even when the old heater is due, with almost no houses in cold climates where it makes sense. [s33 ok] In Britain, the same study as before put the net private cost of swapping a gas boiler for a heat pump at £4,121 over 20 years, what the household pays after a grant, and that already subtracts the boiler the household no longer has to buy. [s34 ok] But the authors valued the Cosy tariff's savings, if they persisted, at £4,675, more than that net cost. [s35 ok] Which brings us to when you buy. A Minnesota study priced a cold-climate heat pump with a propane furnace as backup at $9,450 installed: with nothing broken, that whole price was the extra cost, and the payback ran to well over a decade, depending on the furnace it was compared with. [s37 ok] When the air conditioner alone had failed, the payback roughly halved, and when the furnace and the air conditioner both needed replacing, the extra cost fell to $370 and the payback to about a year or less. [s38 ok] Those homes ran on propane, not gas, so the years themselves do not carry over to a gas home. [s39 ok] For gas homes, RMI's model finds the same exception: in many retrofits a heat pump costs more than new gas equipment, except for the customer who must replace both a furnace and an air conditioner, because a single heat pump does both jobs. [s40 ok] And in Lawrence Berkeley's cost study, the heat pump's price premium over gas equipment disappeared once a home was getting cooling as well as heating. [s41 ok] So does a heat pump pay for itself? For a gas home whose furnace still works, the evidence says it rarely does on energy savings alone; bought when the furnace and the air conditioner both need replacing, or when the home is adding cooling anyway, the extra cost can shrink to little or nothing, while with only the air conditioner due it shrinks but does not vanish. [s42 ok] And even within one area, the national laboratory study found bill savings varied more from house to house than from place to place, so none of these figures is your house's number. [s43 ok]
