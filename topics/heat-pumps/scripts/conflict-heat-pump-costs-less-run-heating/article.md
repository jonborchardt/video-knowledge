## Oil, propane and baseboards: is a heat pump a sure win? [heading:hook fp=9f4191bdab]

Most heat pump arguments are about gas, but homes that burn heating oil or propane, or run electric baseboards, are usually told that for them a heat pump is the easy win. [s01 fp=f5d72930f8] This article checks that promise fuel by fuel, against what studies modelled and what they measured in real homes. [s02 fp=16cfcfac66]

## Who says a heat pump always wins, and who disagrees [heading:setup fp=b328e7d7d6]

EnergySage, a marketplace that connects homeowners with installers, states it most broadly: "Heat pumps consistently save against propane, oil, and electric resistance heating almost everywhere." [s03 ok fp=d360c98afc] Efficiency Maine's homeowner FAQ is a little more careful, saying "heating with heat pumps is usually less expensive than heating with oil, kerosene, or propane". [s04 ok fp=4187eece19] Rewiring America ranks the fuels, writing that "the biggest savings will come if you switch from fuel oil, propane, or electricity as your main source of heat". [s05 ok fp=e32b6a7829] The Boston Globe reported the other side: in a winter when oil was cheaper, even Massachusetts homeowners leaving oil "could see their energy costs increase by a few hundred dollars over the cold season". [s06 ok fp=f264f5dc2e]

## What the studies found, fuel by fuel [heading:investigation fp=cf006d88fb]

The widest view comes from a national model by researchers at NREL and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory: of the homes heating mainly with electricity, fuel oil or propane that already have air conditioning, it estimates that 95 to 100 percent would pay less with a heat pump, at a median saving of $300 to $650 a year depending on the unit's efficiency. [s07 ok fp=d4ab52326a] Air conditioning matters because the model has the heat pump cool the house too: for the 6 million homes on those fuels without it, only 73 to 86 percent come out ahead, and most of the homes whose bills rise are in hotter climates, where the added cooling costs more than the heating saves. [s08 ok fp=6383e6bf60] Gas homes with air conditioning, where most heat pump advice starts, fare worse in the same model: their median runs from a $70 loss with the lowest-performing heat pump to a $380 saving with the highest. [s09 ok fp=a5a85f177c]

Table t1: Modelled bill savings from a heat pump, US homes by heating fuel and air conditioning (NREL and LBNL) [t1 fp=90bf3b95f6]

| | Share with lower bills | Median saving per year |
|---|---|---|
| Homes heating with electricity, fuel oil or propane, with air conditioning | 95%–100% | $300–$650 |
| Homes heating with electricity, fuel oil or propane, without air conditioning | 73%–86% |
| Homes heating with natural gas, with air conditioning | 38%–99% | $70 loss to $380 saving |

A model is an estimate, so the next question is what heat pumps did in homes where they were measured. [s10 fp=113e840950] A field assessment of cold-climate heat pumps found that the all-electric systems cut heating costs by 53 percent on average, measured against a baseline that assumed an electric resistance heater would have met the same load. [s11 ok fp=472869485b]

| | Saving |
|---|---|

An evaluation of ductless mini-split heat pumps found them cheaper to run than electric resistance and propane heat at every energy price and every outdoor temperature it examined. [s13 ok fp=ad053b09ad] Oil was the exception: the price of oil relative to electricity set the outdoor temperature above which the mini-split was the cheaper heat. [s14 ok fp=1eef0ee8b8]

**unresolved (gap 2):** The evaluators advised oil-heated homes to switch the mini-split off in very cold weather, when the oil system would run cheaper: below 8 degrees Fahrenheit in 2015 and below 25 degrees in 2016, depending on energy prices and on how the units performed in the cold. [s15 ok fp=363d372b3f] They added that if oil prices rise against electricity rates, that switchover temperature may move lower, allowing the heat pump to run all winter. [s16 ok fp=3e049b0edb]

Another study found the same ordering by fuel: where homes heat with electricity or fuels other than gas, heating is generally more expensive and the net benefits of a heat pump are higher, though climate and energy prices also play an important role. [s17 ok fp=a3d37707a0] Data from 273 completed electrification retrofits point the same way: the large cuts in annual energy cost were most likely in homes that had high bills beforehand, such as those heating with propane or fuel oil, while some projects raised costs, by as much as $1,000 a year in some cases. [s18 ok fp=76c84c4a5d]

Figure conflict-heat-pump-costs-less-run-heating/sc16: Home electrification projects with cost data: how many there were and the largest yearly cost increase among them. [conflict-heat-pump-costs-less-run-heating/sc16 fp=d981aeaf04]

Counting what the equipment costs as well as the fuel, RMI found that in parts of New England without gas, heat pumps have a lower net present cost than both oil- and propane-fired systems, and recommends prioritising oil and propane customers, for whom it finds the greatest immediate cost benefit. [s19 ok fp=583f60abf4]

**unresolved (heat-pumps/heat-pumps-save-most-money-homes):** A study of the private economics of US home heat pumps, counting the cost of buying the heat pump over its life as well as the fuel, reached a different result for oil: switching usually leaves an oil-heated homeowner worse off, and nearly 65 percent of oil-heated houses are in cold climates, while replacing resistance heat almost always produces a clear benefit and replacing propane is often economical. [s20 ok fp=171dc3494c]

**unresolved (gap 3):** A British study of heat pump customers fits the resistance-heat case: electricity use rose least after installation, and in fact fell, for those who had heated with electricity before; but it measures electricity use rather than bills, and the great majority of its customers had a gas boiler before, so it cannot speak to oil or propane. [s21 ok fp=7a7ea4f092]

**unresolved (gap 4):** A Canadian policy report, citing Natural Resources Canada, puts the energy saving against baseboards at up to 65 percent and infers from that figure significant savings on the power bill for those switching from baseboards; the bill saving is the report's inference, not a measurement, and it compares heat pumps with baseboards only. [s22 ok fp=88b3dea826]

## So does a heat pump always cost less to run? [heading:answer fp=d47a0b1fac]

Against propane and electric resistance heat, this evidence says nearly always: the mini-split evaluation found it cheaper at every price and temperature it examined, and the national model found lower bills for almost all such homes that already had air conditioning. [s23 ok fp=0e3b392ce5] Against oil, not always: the heat pump wins above a switchover temperature that moves with the price of oil, and the studies that count equipment cost disagree on whether leaving oil pays. [s24 ok fp=a908cd11e6]

**unresolved (gap 0):** Why the studies split on oil is not something this evidence settles: they range from national models to measured homes and lifetime-cost estimates, and the mini-split evaluation found that cost-effectiveness depends on the heating system being replaced, the energy prices of the period and the outdoor temperature. [s25 ok fp=44cfbd2bbb]

Nor can it price a particular house: the results moved with whether the home already had air conditioning, with climate and with energy prices. [s26 ok fp=46e23c127d]

## What to check before you switch [heading:takeaway fp=3d1709107c]

If you heat with propane or baseboards, the open question is less whether a heat pump will run cheaper than what it will cost to install, so set installed quotes against what you spend on fuel each winter. [s27 fp=4c54a8cb9c] If you heat with oil, ask an installer below what outdoor temperature your old system becomes the cheaper heat at this winter's prices, and whether you will keep it for those days. [s28 fp=b8c0594f13] And if you have no air conditioning now, remember that a heat pump brings cooling with it, and cooling adds to the bill. [s29 fp=92b2e702bf]
