## Is a heat pump cleaner when the power plant burns gas or coal? [heading:hook fp=dedf6fbf8c]

A heat pump burns nothing inside the house, but the power station that feeds it may burn gas or coal, so does trading a gas furnace or boiler for one actually lower a home's carbon emissions? [s01 fp=ba054ba974] This article sets out the evidence study by study, with the grid and the years each result assumes, because those are what decide the answer. [s02 fp=ccfc22428a]

## Who says heat pumps are cleaner, and who doubts it [heading:setup fp=530d04bbf3]

Sceptics point at the power plants: a letter in the Boston Globe objected that "the majority of electricity in Massachusetts is sourced from gas- and coal-fired power plants", and the American Gas Association contends that a household heating with gas can have a lower carbon footprint than one with a heat pump. [s03 ok fp=051d4295e0] On the other side, the channel Technology Connections argues that efficiency wins even when the electricity comes from a gas-fired power plant, because "a heat pump can move more heat energy into a living space than the natural gas itself contains". [s04 ok fp=3d71aacc9c]

## What the studies measured and what they modelled [heading:investigation fp=fd53397550]

The most direct evidence comes from British households that swapped a gas boiler for a heat pump: a working paper published by the National Bureau of Economic Research converted the change in their energy use into emissions, and in today's terms found a saving of 1.16 tonnes a year of CO2 equivalent for each household, or 36 percent of what their energy use emitted before the switch. [s05 ok fp=ba7ee33d0f] Assuming the grid keeps decarbonizing over a 20-year working life, the same authors put the saving across the heat pump's lifespan at 68 percent; that figure is a projection, and the saving above is the one for today's grid. [s06 ok fp=829c81c9cc] A Fraunhofer ISE study of heat pumps in existing buildings, counting the German electricity mix quarter by quarter, found that in 2024 the heat pumps it examined emitted on average 64 percent less CO2 than gas heating systems. [s07 ok fp=c93f38d661] The Canadian Climate Institute, drawing on the International Energy Agency and a consultancy rather than on measurements of its own, puts the cut at between 20 and 30 percent against gas furnaces even where the electricity is relatively emissions-intensive. [s08 ok fp=e1d94b3929]

RMI's analysis of electrifying buildings tested the coal objection directly, comparing electric heating with natural gas on the grid of its day, and found it reduced carbon in Oakland, Houston and Providence. [s09 ok fp=85ed3c6977]

**unresolved (gap 1):** Chicago went the other way: because coal was the generator answering extra demand there for most of the year, the short-term effect of electrifying heat was higher carbon emissions, though RMI expected that to swing in favour of electrification, in Chicago and nationally, as coal plants retire. [s10 ok fp=0283d54dd3]

How common is Chicago's case across the United States? A national model of US houses found that adopting a heat pump raises CO2 emissions in only 2.1 percent of the housing stock, about 1.7 million houses, counted across all houses rather than gas-heated ones, and the estimate assumes grid CO2 emissions fall by 45 percent over a 15-year heat pump life. [s11 ok fp=9418452e80]

Figure conflict-heat-pumps-cut-home-carbon-emissions/sc11: A US national model: the share of houses where a heat pump raises emissions, shown with the grid assumption and equipment life behind it. [conflict-heat-pumps-cut-home-carbon-emissions/sc11 fp=4b4b4e82ce]

Researchers at NREL and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory modelled every home adopting an air-source heat pump at once, whatever it heated with before, and found average greenhouse gas savings positive in every state under every heat pump and future grid scenario they ran, including the conservative high renewable energy cost scenario. [s12 ok fp=2e214cdabb] Those are state averages on grids projected forward, so they say nothing about how many individual homes come out behind on today's grid. [s13 ok fp=be79c5e2e5]

**unresolved (gap 2):** A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study of home retrofits shows the grid's weight from the other side: its electrification projects with solar panels sat mostly in states with low-carbon electricity, where fuel switching sharply boosted carbon reductions. [s14 ok fp=1b58218b1a] Its authors expect similar projects to save less carbon in states whose electricity is more carbon-intensive, so those results do not carry over to a coal-heavy grid. [s15 ok fp=23047bdb28]

## So does a heat pump cut emissions on today's grid? [heading:answer fp=8d4ae9aa3e]

On most grids the studies here say yes: the British saving above was measured against today's grid, and RMI found that electrification already cut carbon compared with natural gas in all but the most coal-heavy systems, as those systems stood when it wrote. [s16 ok fp=a56fdb3902]

**unresolved (heat-pumps/heat-pumps-cut-home-carbon-emissions):** What the evidence cannot yet say is how many homes on today's grid come out behind: the national US model assumes a grid that gets cleaner, the national laboratories' study projects future grids, and the coal exception rests on RMI's reading of the grid as it stood then, so the answer is well supported on most grids but not settled for all of them. [s17 ok fp=707b3601ad]

**unresolved (gap 0):** Why the studies differ is not something this evidence settles, and they measure against different baselines; RMI and the British study do tie their results to the grid: RMI links its Chicago exception to coal answering extra demand, and the British saving grows in the projection that has the grid decarbonizing. [s18 ok fp=9565ea2d93]

## What to ask before counting on the carbon saving [heading:takeaway fp=12d7b44604]

If cutting carbon is your reason for switching, ask your electricity supplier which plants run when winter demand rises, and whether coal is still among them. [s19 fp=69cc2084e4] The answer tells you which of the results above describes your home, and whether it is likely to change while your heat pump is still running. [s20 fp=27c50dd952]
