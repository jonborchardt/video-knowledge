## Hook

Picture a pair of shoppers: the first fills the cart with organic food, the other buys whatever is cheapest. Years later, the organic shopper is in better health. Was it the food, or the kind of person who buys it? And behind that sits a second question: does organic food lower your risk of cancer?

## Does eating organic make you healthier?

The channel Seeker makes one case: "it seems to me that organic has lots of health benefits". [s05 ok] Dr. Jason West goes further, saying eating organic "helps to prevent autoimmune and chronic diseases". [s06 ok] Harvard Health disagrees: "there isn’t clear evidence that organic foods are also healthier for people". [s07 ok] A systematic review found that, in observational studies, eating organic went along with lower rates of metabolic syndrome, high body weight, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, infertility and birth defects, among other outcomes. [s08 ok] The same review warns that organic eating often comes with a healthier diet overall and less overweight, and that this is likely to sway those results. [s09 ok] A second review saw similar signals, for childhood allergies, adult overweight and non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and concluded that it is not yet possible to say whether organic food causes any of them. [s10 ok] Its authors point out that people who prefer organic also eat more fruit, vegetables, whole grains and legumes and less meat, and they expect those people to have less diabetes and cardiovascular disease as a consequence of that diet. [s11 ok] What about trials, where researchers change what people eat and watch what happens? Those trials have been short, and have mostly measured stand-in markers rather than disease itself, with limited positive results. [s13 ok] Another review counted 17 studies in people, and only 3 of them looked at actual illness: they found no significant differences in allergic conditions or in symptomatic Campylobacter infection. [s14 ok] Studies in adults that measured nutrients and other markers in blood, urine, breast milk and semen found no clinically meaningful differences. [s15 ok] And a report on organic foods found no well-powered human study that directly shows a health benefit, or protection from disease, from eating an organic diet. [s16 ok] So no health benefit of eating organic has been proven in the people who eat it. [s17 ok] Not proven is not the same as ruled out: studies consistently find fewer pesticide breakdown products in the urine of people on an organic diet, and the first review's authors think it possible that lower exposure, kept up over time, could turn into health benefits. [s18 ok] If a benefit is there, could it show up in the outcome people fear most: who gets cancer?

## Does eating organic lower your risk of cancer?

Tim Spector, on the ZOE channel, says one study found "the organic food group were protected by about 25 percent". [s20 ok] A Dana-Farber Cancer Institute video describes a large study of British women that found "organic food intake was not associated with a decreased cancer risk for the cancers that they evaluated". [s21 ok] The NutriNet-Santé cohort followed 68,946 adults for an average of 4.6 years, and after adjusting for other factors, the quarter who scored highest for eating organic had a 25 percent lower rate of cancer than the quarter who scored lowest. [s22 ok] In absolute terms, though, the difference in the share of people who got cancer was 0.6 percent. [s23 ok] The lower risk showed up for non-Hodgkin lymphoma and postmenopausal breast cancer, and not for other types of cancer. [s24 ok] In the United Kingdom, the Million Women Study followed its participants for about 9 years. [s25 ok] Women who said they usually or always ate organic had no lower rate of cancer overall than women who said they never did, and a slightly higher rate of breast cancer. [s26 ok] The one cancer that was lower was non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and the authors' conclusion hedges even that: "except possibly for non-Hodgkin lymphoma". [s27 ok] So these cohorts agree on lymphoma and point in opposite directions on breast cancer. [s28 ok] Why would the cohorts disagree? In NutriNet-Santé, the higher people scored for eating organic, the more likely they were to be women, better paid, better educated and physically active, and the more fruit, vegetables, nuts and legumes and the less meat they ate. [s30 ok] In the British study, women who usually or always ate organic were more likely to report strenuous exercise and less likely to smoke or eat red and processed meat, though differences in socioeconomic status were smaller. [s31 ok] The French team found that adding more diet measures did not change its result, but the systematic review from earlier notes that studies adjust for diet in different ways, so a benefit may still be partly down to whole-food diets and healthier lives rather than the organic food itself. [s32 ok] So the evidence does not show that organic food lowers cancer risk: one cohort found no overall drop, and the other found a small absolute difference among people who also ate and lived differently. [s33 ok]

## Close

So the next time a label or a video promises that organic food will keep you well, ask one question: was the benefit measured in people who were given the food, or in people who chose it? Then ask what was counted, an illness or a number on a blood test, and for how long.
