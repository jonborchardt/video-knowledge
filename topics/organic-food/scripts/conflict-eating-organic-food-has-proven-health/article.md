Story `conflict:eating-organic-food-has-proven-health` (organic-food), revision 2. SEO title: Organic food and health: what the studies show [meta.seoTitle fp=2588f1cdf3]. Validation: **passed**, 0 blocking, 30 warnings, 49/49 assertions verified.

> Reviews of the research find no health benefit of eating organic that has been proven in people, and the large cancer cohorts disagree, in groups whose diets and habits also differ. [meta.dek ok fp=8146529add]

Description: Organic eaters look healthier in observational studies, but reviews find no proven benefit, and the cancer evidence is split and confounded. [meta.description ok fp=cf80dfa10b]

## Organic eaters are healthier. Is the food the reason? [heading:hook fp=4ea95bb938]

If you pay extra for organic food, you are probably paying for your health as much as for the farm. [s01 fp=51f0e12eb4] Is that what you get, and does it reach the disease people fear most, cancer? [s02 fp=eb2ae76414] What follows sets out what the reviews and the large cohorts found, and where their evidence stops. [s03 fp=1f1f383a0d]

## Does eating organic food actually make you healthier? [heading:question-1 fp=af4169a37e]

On YouTube, Seeker puts the case plainly: "it seems to me that organic has lots of health benefits". [s04 ok fp=5216040ea0] The Organic Trade Association's site goes further, saying organic "reduces your exposure to birth defects, obesity, diabetes, ADHD, and other health problems". [s05 ok fp=820dc3dd4a] The Mayo Clinic is more cautious: "Aside from lessened contact with pesticides, proven links between health and organic food are less clear." [s06 ok fp=e6f378f320]

A systematic review of the research found that, in observational studies, people who ate more organic food had lower rates of metabolic syndrome, high BMI, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, infertility, birth defects, allergic sensitisation, ear infections and pre-eclampsia. [s07 ok fp=e286a76de3] The same review cautions that organic eating usually travels with a healthier diet overall and less overweight and obesity, which it judges likely to influence those observational results. [s08 ok fp=b4c0c08dda] A second review saw hints of fewer childhood allergies, less adult obesity and less non-Hodgkin lymphoma among organic consumers, but judged that it cannot yet be determined whether organic food causes any of them. [s09 ok fp=da56bbb611] Its authors add that people who prefer organic eat more fruit, vegetables, whole grains and legumes and less meat, a pattern that would by itself be expected to lower their risk of diabetes and cardiovascular disease. [s10 ok fp=85af1530af] Trials, in which researchers change what people eat, have so far run for short periods and mostly tracked surrogate markers, with few positive results. [s11 ok fp=68adca5e78] The Stanford review counted 17 studies in people, of which only 3 measured clinical outcomes, and those found no significant differences in eczema, wheeze, allergic sensitisation or symptomatic Campylobacter infection. [s12 ok fp=b9435cddec]

Figure conflict-eating-organic-food-has-proven-health/sc12: Human studies in the Stanford review, and how many of them looked at actual illness. [conflict-eating-organic-food-has-proven-health/sc12 fp=44207464a0]

In adults, its studies of nutrients and other markers in blood, urine, breast milk and semen showed no clinically meaningful differences. [s13 ok fp=c2a0f0bece] A clinical report from the American Academy of Pediatrics found no well-powered human study that directly demonstrates a health benefit or protection from disease as a result of an organic diet. [s14 ok fp=6399bad848] A meta-analysis of organic milk found it differs in composition, but its authors say virtually no studies have measured what eating organic does to health; a companion analysis of meat adds that no cohort study yet links organic meat to less eczema or other health gains. [s15 ok fp=1dec6b1933] And a risk model of pesticide residues on the Dirty Dozen produce found nothing to indicate that swapping the conventional forms for organic ones would bring a measurable health benefit. [s16 ok fp=f5d3214562]

**unresolved (organic-food/eating-organic-food-has-proven-health):** What remains open is whether eating less pesticide matters: studies consistently find fewer pesticide metabolites in the urine of people on an organic diet, the evidence is insufficient to show that this turns into a meaningful health outcome, and the systematic review's authors still think it possible that lower exposure kept up over time may bring health benefits. [s17 ok fp=9e0bc93e7a] So the benefit is unproven rather than ruled out: the trials that could test it have been short and have mostly measured markers rather than disease. [s18 ok fp=67d76bb84c]

**unresolved (gap 0):** These reviews differ less in their verdict than in what they examined, from the composition of milk to pesticide risk models to outcomes in people, and none of them concludes that eating organic has been shown to cause a health benefit. [s19 ok fp=86f1c9d189]

On the evidence so far, then, no health benefit of eating organic has been shown in the people who eat it, though the reviews leave the question open. [s20 ok fp=58867db926] That brings us to the outcome where the boldest claims are made: cancer. [s21 ok fp=91dceaf78f]

## Does eating organic food lower your risk of cancer? [heading:question-2 fp=89726658b6]

On the ZOE channel, Tim Spector describes a study in which "the organic food group were protected by about 25 percent". [s22 ok fp=65da145a69] Renegade Dietitian gives the reasoning behind such claims: "less pesticide equals lower risk exposure, which equals better long-term odds". [s23 ok fp=3192d97631] F. Perry Wilson, MD, puts the doctor's question and answers it: "Should you encourage your patients to eat organic? Not on the basis of this study." [s24 ok fp=9861a76915]

The French NutriNet-Santé cohort tracked 68,946 adults for a mean of 4.6 years, and after adjustment for confounders, those in the top quarter of an organic food score had a hazard of cancer 25 percent below the bottom quarter's, a hazard ratio of 0.75. [s25 ok fp=80b7289a3c] Expressed as an absolute risk reduction, that gap was 0.6 percent. [s26 ok fp=71bb960475] By cancer type, the lower risk appeared for non-Hodgkin lymphoma and postmenopausal breast cancer, with no association for the other types. [s27 ok fp=9ba84c87b9] The British Million Women Study was far larger, and found that those who usually or always ate organic had no lower rate of cancer overall than those who never did. [s28 ok fp=0f41722a83] Breast cancer was slightly more common in its organic group, and the only cancer that was less common was non-Hodgkin lymphoma, which the authors' conclusion qualifies as "except possibly for non-Hodgkin lymphoma". [s29 ok fp=f1eb514838]

Read together, the cohorts point the same way on lymphoma and opposite ways on breast cancer. [s30 ok fp=2407437c01] In the French cohort, a higher organic score went with being a woman, higher income or occupational status, a degree, more physical activity, more fruit, vegetables, nuts and legumes, and less meat. [s31 ok fp=cb711d4384] In the British cohort, regular organic eaters reported more strenuous exercise and were less likely to smoke or eat red and processed meat, but their socioeconomic status differed less. [s32 ok fp=b209baef4d] The French team reports that accounting for further dietary factors left its result unchanged, yet the systematic review notes that studies measure and adjust for diet in different ways, so an apparent benefit may owe something to wholefood diets and healthier lives rather than to organic food itself. [s33 ok fp=1a043d09d4]

**unresolved (gap 1):** Why the cohorts split is not settled, but an analysis of US cohorts offers one reading: produce low in pesticide residues was not linked to the cancers earlier tied to organic food, which suggests those links reflect education, activity, not smoking and moderate drinking rather than less pesticide in the diet. [s34 ok fp=caec765f73]

**unresolved (gap 2):** The same US authors point out that the French association, in stratified analyses, appears confined to the groups most likely to eat organic, such as women, older people and those with a family history of cancer, which raises concerns about residual and unmeasured confounding. [s35 ok fp=5c4bc0f2d5]

**unresolved (gap 3):** The systematic review that sets both cohorts side by side adds that self-reported organic intake has not been validated, and that the benefit seen may be partly down to diet quality rather than organic food. [s36 ok fp=4fd6fee109]

**unresolved (organic-food/organic-buyers-wealthier-healthier-which-confounds):** That organic eaters live differently holds more clearly for health habits than for wealth: the British cohort found only small socioeconomic differences, and a USDA report describes organic consumers as fairly diverse in income, race and ethnicity, though price remains a barrier for some. [s37 ok fp=70f2d9c6da]

**unresolved (organic-food/eating-organic-food-lowers-cancer-risk):** The question stays open: the one cancer both cohorts tie to organic eating is non-Hodgkin lymphoma, the British authors hedged even that, and the French result comes from people whose diets and lives differed from those who rarely ate organic. [s38 ok fp=3077584f00]

Taken together, the cohorts do not show that eating organic lowers cancer risk: the bigger study saw no overall drop, and the smaller saw a 0.6 percent absolute difference in a group whose diet and habits also set it apart. [s39 ok fp=83050feb53]

## How to read the next claim that organic food keeps you well [heading:close fp=1f3daa5ab8]

Both answers turn on the same test: were the people in the study given organic food, or did they choose it along with the rest of how they live? [s40 fp=9a68ce7303] Then ask what was counted, cases of disease or a marker in blood or urine, and for how long. [s41 fp=5e3d76d546] And when a result comes as a percentage lower risk, look for the absolute difference beside it. [s42 fp=afdf78a656]
