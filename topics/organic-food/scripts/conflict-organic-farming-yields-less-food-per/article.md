## Does organic farming grow less food, and does that matter for the planet? [heading:hook fp=ef221929f0]

If an organic farm harvests less from each field, more land has to be farmed somewhere to put the same food on the table. [s01 fp=f53f74fca5] Videos and articles split on whether it does: some say organic falls short at harvest, while others say it grows as much food or more. [s02 ok fp=15221cbc42] This article settles the yield question first, then asks what a smaller harvest means for the environmental case for organic. [s03 ok fp=dc7014c7cd]

## Does organic farming really grow less food? [heading:question-1 fp=0c26ecde7a]

The science explainer channel AsapSCIENCE states that "organic crop yields are 25% lower on average than conventional ones". [s04 ok fp=1bc3dc6da4] The physician Mark Hyman argues the reverse, that "you can grow as much food or more food better food without all those chemicals". [s05 ok fp=26510dfde1]

The widest test in this evidence is a meta-analysis by Lauren Ponisio and colleagues, which put organic yields 19.2% below conventional, with a 95% credible interval of 15.5% to 22.9%. [s06 ok fp=aecc323f9c] Conventional fields out-yielded organic ones significantly for every crop type in that analysis, and most crop types showed gaps of similar size. [s07 ok fp=a7923b5d01] The authors report that their gap is smaller than the ones recent meta-analyses had found, and they caution that the literature leans towards reporting bigger conventional advantages, so even their figure may be too high. [s08 ok fp=6de917c4fc] Cereals, the crop type with the widest organic shortfall, made up 53% of the comparisons, so the pooled average leans towards them. [s09 ok fp=057e49d2e2]

As for why organic yields lag, a review of organic food and health, citing earlier research rather than analysing yields itself, says nitrogen typically limits crop yield in organic systems but not in conventional ones. [s11 ok fp=8e7ff107b5] A separate meta-analysis of farm profitability by Crowder and Reganold lands nearby: with organic price premiums removed, gross returns, which the authors say mirror yields, were 10% lower for organic crops and 18% lower for organic systems. [s12 ok fp=0294cfe7a0]

Table t1: How much less organic farming yields than conventional, by study [t1 fp=364ce55c31]

| | Organic yield below conventional |
|---|---|
| Ponisio and colleagues, pooled yield comparisons, central estimate | 19.2% |
| Ponisio and colleagues, low end of the 95% credible interval | 15.5% |
| Ponisio and colleagues, high end of the 95% credible interval | 22.9% |
| Crowder and Reganold, organic crops (from gross returns without premiums) | 10% |
| Crowder and Reganold, organic systems (from gross returns without premiums) | 18% |

The dissent here is a clinical report from the American Academy of Pediatrics, whose abstract says well-designed farming studies demonstrate yields comparable to those of conventional farming. [s13 ok fp=d312248465]

**unresolved (gap 0):** That abstract makes its case in a single sentence, without a yield figure and without naming the studies it rests on. [s14 ok fp=d2fe6cf8d5] So the evidence here cannot say what explains the split: the studies that measured yields or returns all found organic behind, and the abstract that disagrees does not show which comparisons it read differently. [s15 ok fp=266e03e1f7]

On the measured comparisons, then, organic farming does grow less food per field, by the 19.2% average given above, perhaps by somewhat less given the authors’ caveat. [s16 ok fp=82bb99fd21] A smaller harvest from each field leads straight to the next question, about land and the environment. [s17 fp=7ea2b3b969]

## Is organic farming actually better for the planet? [heading:question-2 fp=b190d867e2]

Harvard Health tells its readers that "Organic foods are clearly healthier for the planet". [s18 ok fp=aa05512e59] The pediatric report quoted above agrees, stating that organic farming "has been demonstrated to have less environmental impact than conventional approaches". [s19 ok fp=886f0030ee] Clark and Tilman tested this kind of claim per unit of food rather than per field, in a meta-analysis that only compared both systems within the same publication. [s20 ok fp=2dea38ab70]

Table t2: Organic against conventional farming per unit of food, Clark and Tilman meta-analysis [t2 fp=36859fc782]

| | Organic against conventional |
|---|---|
| Land use | 25% to 110% more |
| Energy use | 15% less |
| Eutrophication potential (nutrient pollution) | 37% higher |
| Greenhouse gas emissions (difference not significant) | 4% lower |
| Acidification potential (difference not significant) | 13% higher |

Land showed the widest range: organic systems needed 25% to 110% more of it to produce the same food. [s21 ok fp=034b0670e0] Organic used 15% less energy, but its eutrophication potential, a measure of nutrient pollution, was 37% higher. [s22 ok fp=ed044d8e38] Greenhouse gas emissions per unit of food did not differ significantly, which the authors put down to manure: relying on it turns more of the farm’s reactive nitrogen into nitrous oxide, a potent greenhouse gas. [s23 ok fp=2d8fc7fcf4] Counted per unit of food, organic is not clearly the greener system: it saves energy but takes more land and causes more nutrient pollution, with emissions about level. [s24 ok fp=4b1d622165]

**qualification (organic-food/organic-farming-better-environment-conventional):** The authors reject the opposite conclusion too: conventional farming needs more energy and leans on heavy fertiliser, herbicide and pesticide inputs that can harm health and the environment, so their results do not make it the more sustainable system. [s25 ok fp=5587134b49] Because each comparison sits inside one publication, the results describe differences at a local scale, and the authors say the balance could change at a regional, national or global scale. [s26 ok fp=7fe0649b5e]

**unresolved (organic-food/organic-farming-needs-more-land-produce):** The extra land is a wide range rather than a single figure, and it rests on yield gaps that Ponisio and colleagues think the published literature may overstate. [s27 ok fp=b2d268e084]

**unresolved (organic-food/organic-conventional-farming-have-similar-greenhouse):** The emissions result is an absence of a significant difference, not a measured tie: organic came out 4% lower, a gap the analysis could not separate from chance, and the comparisons are local in scale. [s31 ok fp=9476930e30]

What matters far more is the food itself: on every indicator they examined, ruminant meats had 20 to 100 times the impacts of plant-based foods, a gap much larger than the one between farming systems. [s28 ok fp=b0f1983052]

**unresolved (organic-food/what-you-eat-matters-more-your):** That contrast sets foods against each other; the farming-system differences it dwarfs come from local comparisons, which the authors say could look different at a regional, national or global scale. [s32 ok fp=d63f7df768]

## What to ask when organic is sold as more food or a greener choice [heading:close fp=7f4c687015]

When someone says organic grows as much as conventional, ask what was measured and how many comparisons stand behind the claim. [s29 fp=eb8cb51fb0] When a product is sold as better for the planet, ask whether that holds per field or per unit of food, and which food is on the plate. [s30 fp=81327fc8ce]
