## Hook

Picture two fields growing the same crop, one farmed organically and one not. At harvest, does the organic field come up short, and by how much? [s01 ok] Ask around and you hear both answers with total confidence: organic grows less, or organic grows just as much. [s02 ok] And the answer sets up a second question: if organic does grow less, is it still the greener choice?

## Does organic farming really grow less food?

AsapSCIENCE told its viewers that "organic crop yields are 25% lower on average than conventional ones". [s04 ok] Mark Hyman says the opposite: "you can grow as much food or more food better food without all those chemicals". [s05 ok] So who is right? A meta-analysis by Lauren Ponisio and colleagues pooled side-by-side yield comparisons and found organic yields 19.2% lower than conventional, with a likely range of 15.5 to 22.9%, and the authors warn that even this may overstate the gap, because the published studies lean towards reporting bigger conventional wins. [s07 ok] Conventional came out ahead for every crop type the study grouped, and for most of those types the gap was about the same size. [s08 ok] That gap is real, but it is smaller than the ones earlier meta-analyses had reported. [s09 ok] Why lower at all? A review of organic food and health, citing earlier work, points to fertiliser: in organic systems crop yield is typically limited by how much nitrogen the plants can get, which is not the case in conventional systems. [s10 ok] Not everyone agrees: a clinical report for pediatricians says well-designed farming studies show organic yields comparable to conventional farming. [s11 ok] But in the report's summary, the part we could read, that is one sentence, with no yield figure and no studies named. [s12 ok] So on the pooled data, yes: organic fields grew less, by roughly a fifth or perhaps a little less, but where crops were rotated or grown in mixtures the gap shrank to about 8 or 9%. [s13 ok] Which raises the next question: if each organic field grows less, does organic need more land for the same food, and does that undo its green reputation?

## Is organic farming actually better for the planet?

Harvard Health put it plainly: "Organic foods are clearly healthier for the planet". [s15 ok] The same pediatric report agrees, saying organic farming "has been demonstrated to have less environmental impact than conventional approaches". [s16 ok] But a meta-analysis by Clark and Tilman measured the impacts per unit of food, not per field, using studies that assessed both systems within the same publication. [s17 ok] Measured that way, organic systems needed 25 to 110% more land. [s18 ok] They used 15% less energy, but had 37% higher eutrophication potential, a measure of nutrient pollution. [s19 ok] Greenhouse gas emissions came out similar, because relying on manure turns more of a farm's nitrogen into nitrous oxide, a potent greenhouse gas. [s20 ok] The authors add that these are comparisons at a local scale, and the picture might differ regionally, nationally or globally. [s21 ok] And they warn against reading this as a win for conventional farming, which uses more energy and relies on high fertiliser, herbicide and pesticide inputs that can harm health and the environment. [s22 ok] What does dwarf the organic question is the food itself: on every indicator they examined, ruminant meats had 20 to 100 times the impacts of plant-based foods, a difference far larger than the one between farming systems. [s23 ok] So per unit of food, organic is not clearly greener: it trades lower energy use for more land and more nutrient pollution, with emissions about even. [s24 ok]

## Close

The next time a label or a video says organic grows just as much, ask which studies, and how many. And when something is sold as better for the planet, ask: better per what? Per plate of food, the answer here was mixed. [s26 ok]
