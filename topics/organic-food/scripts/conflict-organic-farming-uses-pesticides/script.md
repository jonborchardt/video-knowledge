## Hook

Dr. Eric Berg puts it in one breath: "what does organic mean it means without pesticides herbicides fungicides and insecticides". [s01 ok] So what are organic farmers actually allowed to spray? And if they do spray, does eating organic still keep pesticides out of you?

## Do organic farmers spray pesticides?

Dr. Mindy Pelz describes organic produce as "a food that has technically not been sprayed". [s04 ok] The quickest way to check is to read the rulebook itself. The US organic regulations say that when a farm's preventive practices are not enough, a biological or botanical substance, or a synthetic one from an approved national list, "may be applied to prevent, suppress, or control pests, weeds, or diseases". [s06 ok] That list is headed "Synthetic substances allowed for use in organic crop production", so not even every synthetic pesticide is off the table. Europe's organic list is no purer: it names paraffin oils, lime sulphur and copper compounds such as Bordeaux mixture. [s07 ok] So organic farms do spray. The sharper argument is about whether what they spray is any gentler. Kurzgesagt tells its viewers, "Organic pesticides are not necessarily safer than conventional ones." [s09 ok] Simnett Nutrition takes the other side: "they're generally not as harmful and they're more closely regulated". [s10 ok] A review of organic food and health went through Europe's approved list: of 385 substances authorised as pesticides in the EU, 26 are also approved for organic farming, "evaluated in accordance with the same legal framework". [s11 ok] On the full list, 340 have some identified toxicity, meaning regulators set a safe intake or exposure limit for them; on the organic list, 10 do. [s12 ok] And 27 substances on the full list are classed as "Suspected human carcinogens"; on the organic list, none are, and none is classed as a reproductive toxicant either. [s13 ok] Keep in mind what those tables measure: a substance's built-in hazard, not how much of it reaches anyone, and only for Europe's list, not America's. [s14 ok] The review also names the exceptions. Pyrethrins, an extract of a chrysanthemum, "share the same mechanism of action as the synthetic pyrethroid insecticides". [s15 ok] And copper, a nutrient we all need, is "toxic at high intakes" and a concern for the creatures living in rivers and lakes. [s16 ok] Europe's organic rules even allow a pair of synthetic pyrethroids, but only inside insect traps, where the review judges the risk of them reaching food negligible. [s17 ok] So organic does not mean unsprayed. In America the rules allow listed sprays once prevention has failed; in Europe, where the hazard tables come from, the organic list is mostly of low concern on those tables, with pyrethrins and copper as the real exceptions. [s18 ok] Which leaves the question most shoppers really care about: if organic farms spray too, does eating organic change what ends up inside you?

## Does eating organic lower the pesticides in you?

In a DW Documentary film, one interviewee goes further: "the primary advantage of organic produce is that there are no pesticide residues". [s20 ok] No residues at all? Food testing says otherwise. Averaged over recent years of European food monitoring, measurable residues within legal limits turned up in 43.7 percent of all samples, and in 13.8 percent of organic ones. [s22 ok] American monitoring data tell the same story from the other side: in the federal Pesticide Data Program, 23 percent of organic samples still tested positive. [s23 ok] A meta-analysis of crop studies puts the gap at residues on 11 percent of organic samples against 46 percent of conventional ones, and an older analysis of American testing programmes found the amounts on organic samples consistently lower too. [s24 ok] Residues on food are one thing, though. What actually ends up in your body? NutritionFacts.org says you can "turn on and off urinary concentrations of pesticide metabolites like a light switch". [s26 ok] That sounds like hype, but a systematic review backs it: all four trials it found that swapped people's whole diet to organic and tested their urine, two in children and two in adults, found pesticide breakdown products fell significantly. [s27 ok] The drop was often dramatic, as much as 90 percent, and it came within a few days. [s28 ok] What these trials measure is exposure, not health, and a review of farming systems notes that lower residues may not translate into better health outcomes. [s29 ok] So yes: eating organic sharply and quickly lowers the pesticides on your food and in your body, but it does not get rid of either entirely, and whether that makes you healthier is a separate question this evidence leaves open. [s30 ok]

## Close

Put both answers together and the organic label reads differently. The answers cover different ground, the field and your body, and the slogan blurs them into a promise the rulebook never makes. So the next time someone tells you organic means no pesticides, ask the better question: which ones, and how much of them ends up on your plate?
