# Organic farms do spray. Does eating organic still cut the pesticides you take in? [meta.headline fp=85ff234e56]

Story `conflict:organic-farming-uses-pesticides` (organic-food), revision 3. SEO title: Organic farming, pesticides and what reaches your body [meta.seoTitle fp=87a4aa8988]. Validation: **passed**, 0 blocking, 27 warnings, 49/49 assertions verified.

> Organic rules allow listed pesticides once prevention fails, most of them milder on Europe's hazard tables though not all, and trials find pesticide levels in urine fall within days of eating organic. [meta.dek ok fp=f1df2008cb]

Description: Organic farms may use listed pesticides, mostly but not all milder. Eating organic cuts residues and urinary pesticide levels, but not to nil. [meta.description ok fp=e54bcd0fd1]

## Does organic mean nothing was sprayed? [heading:hook fp=da06209c0e]

Dr. Eric Berg tells his viewers that organic "means without pesticides herbicides fungicides and insecticides". [s01 ok fp=437144b1f9] If that were so, the organic rulebook would have nothing to say about spraying. [s02 fp=c5962dfe8b] It has a good deal to say: the rules allow listed pesticides once prevention fails, and residue tests find organic food carries pesticides less often than conventional food, not never. [s03 ok fp=020c6b55eb] This article takes the questions in turn: what organic farms may spray and how it compares, then what eating organic does to the pesticides in your body. [s04 fp=92c5b6316f]

## Does organic farming really mean no pesticides, and are the ones it uses safer? [heading:question-1 fp=dab7f96160]

Dr. Mindy Pelz describes organic produce as "a food that has technically not been sprayed", and a College of Naturopathic Medicine video says organic growers rely on natural methods "instead of using chemicals". [s05 ok fp=1ea10a4bf4] A speaker at a Harvard public health forum tells the audience the opposite, asking them to "banish that notion" that organic farming uses no pesticides. [s06 ok fp=f8cb91fe39]

The US regulations settle the first part: when a farm's preventive practices are not enough to control crop pests, weeds and diseases, it may apply a biological or botanical substance, or a synthetic one from the National List, provided the conditions of use are documented in its organic system plan. [s07 ok fp=d31b22b3bf] That list is titled "Synthetic substances allowed for use in organic crop production", and most of its entries may be used only once those preventive practices prove insufficient. [s08 ok fp=386b2556fe] The US Department of Agriculture says most permitted pest-control materials are naturally derived, such as pyrethrum or Bacillus thuringiensis, and names elemental sulfur, insecticidal soap, horticultural oils and copper hydroxide among the synthetic ones allowed. [s09 ok fp=099d45030e] Europe's organic regulation lists paraffin oils, lime sulphur and several copper compounds, Bordeaux mixture among them, and limits copper hydroxide to uses totalling at most 28 kg of copper per hectare over 7 years. [s10 ok fp=715f3e76ae]

On whether those pesticides are gentler, Kurzgesagt says "Organic pesticides are not necessarily safer than conventional ones." [s11 ok fp=c7d79c4afa] Simnett Nutrition answers that "they're generally not as harmful and they're more closely regulated". [s12 ok fp=13ef5ab802] A review of organic food and health set the 385 substances authorised as pesticides in the EU beside the 26 of them also approved for organic farming, all evaluated under the same legal framework, and counted their hazard classes; the table below shows where the lists differ and where they overlap. [s13 ok fp=3c51ae9569]

Table t1: Hazard classes among EU-approved pesticide substances: the full list against those approved for organic farming [t1 fp=b5a4d5bb04]

| | All EU-approved | Approved for organic farming |
|---|---|---|
| Approved active substances | 385 | 26 |
| With any identified toxicity (a set daily intake, acute reference dose or operator exposure level) | 340 | 10 |
| Classed as acutely toxic (any class) | 99 | 3 |
| Classed as suspected human carcinogens | 27 | 0 |
| Candidates for substitution for a low acceptable intake or exposure level | 19 | 0 |
| Candidates for substitution for endocrine disrupting properties | 5 | 0 |
| Candidates for substitution for meeting two of the persistent, bioaccumulative and toxic criteria | 54 | 1 |

The contrast is sharpest where people worry most: 27 substances on the full list are classed as suspected human carcinogens, and none on the organic list is classed as a carcinogen or a reproductive toxicant. [s14 ok fp=a9766c7746] The review judges most organic-approved pesticides of comparatively low concern for consumers: some have no identified toxicity, some are ordinary nutrients or foods such as iron or rapeseed oil, and the synthetic pyrethroids lambda-cyhalothrin and deltamethrin are approved for insect traps only, with a negligible risk of entering the food chain. [s15 ok fp=0bb1de9a17]

**qualification (organic-food/pesticides-allowed-organic-farming-not-necessarily):** These counts describe each substance's intrinsic hazard, irrespective of how it is used or how much of it anyone is exposed to, and they cover the pesticides approved in the EU. [s16 ok fp=e766d10ba2] The review also names the exceptions: pyrethrins, an extract of a chrysanthemum, "share the same mechanism of action as the synthetic pyrethroid insecticides", and copper, a nutrient, is toxic at high intakes and harmful to aquatic organisms. [s17 ok fp=81c6359d4e] So the organic list is milder on these tables, mostly but not wholly: it is not proof that every organic pesticide is safer than a synthetic alternative, and the tables measure hazard, not what reaches food. [s18 ok fp=c5676cb4dd]

**unresolved (gap 0):** A crop meta-analysis states in its introduction that organic standards "prohibit the use of synthetic chemical crop protection products"; it is a passing background line in a study of crop composition, it does not examine the rules, and it cannot decide whether every synthetic pesticide is banned. [s19 ok fp=67767317bf]

**unresolved (gap 1):** The sources split less than they seem to: the USDA says organic standards prohibit most synthetic substances, including most conventional pesticides, while the regulation itself lists the synthetic substances it allows, so "no synthetics" is a summary of the rule rather than the rule. [s20 ok fp=e3ce3b41ea]

That answers what organic farms may spray; the next question is what reaches the people who eat the food. [s21 fp=c4c097b460]

## Does eating organic actually lower the pesticides you take in? [heading:question-2 fp=4648b7654e]

A DW Documentary film puts it plainly: "the primary advantage of organic produce is that there are no pesticide residues". [s22 ok fp=09020f3458] NutritionFacts.org says diet can "turn on and off urinary concentrations of pesticide metabolites like a light switch". [s23 ok fp=4db59b6891]

Food monitoring finds organic food cleaner but not clean: averaged over the last three available years of EU testing, residues below the legal limits were found in 13.8% of organic samples against 43.7% of all samples, a comparison with every sample rather than with conventional food alone. [s24 ok fp=e9ef705e9c] In the US Pesticide Data Program, 23 percent of organic samples tested positive for pesticide residues. [s25 ok fp=f0a2991e8b]

Table t2: How often organic food carries pesticide residues, against all or conventional samples [t2 fp=655c88c50b]

| | Organic samples | Comparison samples |
|---|---|---|
| EU monitoring (EFSA), organic against all samples, residues below legal limits, last three available years | 13.8% | 43.7% |
| EU monitoring (EFSA), organic against all samples, over the legal limit | 0.9% | 2.8% |
| EU monitoring (EFSA), organic against all samples, over the acute reference dose | 0% | 1.5% |
| Meta-analysis of crop studies, organic against conventional crops, detectable residues | 11% | 46% |

The amounts are lower too: an analysis of three US data sets found residue concentrations on organic samples consistently lower crop by crop, and state monitoring in Baden-Wuerttemberg in 2021 found an average of 0.002 mg/kg on organic fruit and vegetables against 0.48 mg/kg on conventional fruit and 0.40 mg/kg on conventional vegetables. [s26 ok fp=758071441c]

**qualification (organic-food/organic-produce-carries-lower-pesticide-residues):** The meta-analysis figure counts how often crop samples carried detectable residues, not how much residue they carried; the amounts come from the US data sets and the German monitoring above. [s37 ok fp=adfc5addd8] And fewer residues is not the same as a safer plate: the Stanford review found the risk of detectable residues 30 percentage points lower on organic produce, but only small differences in the risk of exceeding maximum allowed limits. [s27 ok fp=6c22e06ac1] And those legal limits reflect a pesticide's approved use rather than the toxicological relevance of the residue, with no separate limits for organic products, so the residue counts say how often and how much, not how harmful. [s28 ok fp=36554a673e]

What reaches the body has been measured directly: a systematic review found four whole-diet trials, two in children and two in adults, that tested pesticides in urine, and all of them found a significant difference between the conventional and organic phases. [s29 ok fp=b63184d4c2] The reduction was in most cases dramatic, up to 90 percent, and came within a few days. [s30 ok fp=2d433dd3fe] A small study of 16 people in four US families, comparing days on non-organic food with days on organic food, found urinary levels of 13 pesticide compounds fell significantly, and an analysis of the same families' 158 urine samples found mean glyphosate fell 70.93 percent within six days on the organic diet. [s31 ok fp=1fed851ab3]

Table t3: Falls in urinary pesticide levels after switching to an organic diet [t3 fp=c1cfeddd70]

| | Reduction |
|---|---|
| Whole-diet trials in children and adults, systematic review | up to 90% |
| US families: malathion metabolite (MDA) | 95.0% |
| US families: clothianidin | 82.7% |
| US families: chlorpyrifos metabolite (TCPy) | 60.7% |
| US families: glyphosate, within six days | 70.93% |

So eating organic does lower the pesticides you take in, sharply and within days, though organic food still often carries some residue. [s32 ok fp=a4a372ba43] Whether that lower exposure makes anyone healthier is a separate question these trials do not answer; a review of farming systems notes that lower residues may not translate into better health outcomes. [s33 ok fp=d7975258a8]

## What the organic label does and does not promise [heading:close fp=6b15b235b3]

Put together, the answers separate the field from the plate. [s34 fp=2a93628969] The label tells you which sprays a farm was allowed and when, not that nothing was sprayed, and what it buys you is less pesticide in your food and your body rather than none. [s35 ok fp=21e6be4dbe] When a video promises pesticide-free food, ask which pesticides, used when, and how much of them reaches your plate. [s36 fp=1e582d1b72]
