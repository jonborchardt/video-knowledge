Executive summary

State-based conflict

In 2025, the Uppsala Conflict Data Program (UCDP) reported that approximately 245,000 indi-
viduals were killed in battle-related deaths. This was the third most violent year in the post–Cold
War era. Three conflicts drove this trend: the Russian invasion of Ukraine; the violence in Sudan,
including the massacre in El Fasher; and the bombardments of Gaza.

In 2025, the UCDP recorded the highest number of state-based conflicts since 1946, with 65 con-
flicts, 6 more than ever previously recorded, with approximately 153,000 battle deaths – surpassed
only in 2021, 2022 and 2024. This indicates a persistently high level of state-based violence, with
the past five years accounting for more battle-related deaths than the entire twenty-year period
prior to 2021.

In 2025, 65 conflicts were recorded in 35 countries – an increase from 2024 in both the number of
state-based conflicts and the number of countries experiencing them. Notably, the gap between
the number of conflicts and the number of conflict countries has increased over the past decade,
indicating a rise in countries hosting multiple simultaneous conflicts, such as Myanmar with
five civil conflicts, and Israel with two civil conflicts and three international conflicts. In addition,
a number of countries host more than three conflicts, such as Afghanistan, Cameroon, Mali,
Nigeria and Pakistan. Only 16 out of the 35 conflict countries have only one conflict. This trend
points to a growing complexity in conflict dynamics with more actors involved, which has impor-
tant implications for how we analyse and respond to conflict.

Furthermore, interstate conflicts – conflicts between states – have increased substantially. In 2025
we saw eight international conflicts, a doubling since 2024 and the highest recorded since 1946.
This is a very concerning trend, and we see several latent border conflicts having become violent,
reflecting the current growing global tensions. There has also been a regional escalation in the
Middle East, increasing the risk of a large multi-country conflict in the region. This trend has
continued in 2026 with the USA–Iran war.

Non-state conflict

There were 75 non-state conflicts in 2025, which represents a slight decrease from the 79 non-
state conflicts reported in 2024. Many of the non-state conflicts are low intensity, affecting the
volatility in numbers of conflicts from year to year, since many of these conflicts only flare up
occasionally. The number of battle-related deaths from such conflicts was approximately 14,500
in 2025, representing a downward trend since 2020. Particularly, we saw a decrease in lethal vio-
lence between Mexican drug cartels. Despite this, the number of non-state conflict battle-related
deaths has stabilized at a substantially higher level compared to the period before 2013.

                                                                                                                  7

Africa was the region with the highest number of non-state conflicts, with 34 conflicts recorded.
Of these, 14 occurred in Nigeria, but South Sudan and Ethiopia also experienced several non-
state conflicts. Africa is closely followed by the Americas with 32, with Brazil, Colombia and
Mexico constituting the main share.

One-sided violence

Almost 76,500 fatalities from one-sided violence against civilians were recorded in 2025, mainly
due to the violence in Sudan carried out by non-state actors. This is the highest number recorded
since the Rwandan genocide in 1994. The number of actors conducting one-sided violence
reached an all-time high with 55, and the number of fatalities related to government killings
increased in 2025 to almost 5,900. Particularly notable is the high level of fatalities related to
post-election violence in Tanzania.

1. Introduction

In 2025, the total number of battle-related deaths associated with political violence amounted
to 245,000, making 2025 the third most violent year since 1989, surpassed only by 2021 at the
peak of the Ethiopian civil war in Tigray, and by the Rwandan genocide in 1994. The recent
dramatic increase in battle deaths stems mainly from three conflicts: the Russian invasion of
Ukraine, the airstrikes on Gaza by Israel and the political violence in Sudan including the siege
and massacre in El Fasher. We also saw an all-time high number of state-based conflicts, ­rising
to 65 – 6 more than the second highest number recorded (in 2024). This surge in violence calls
for a ­re-examination of the trends and contexts in which conflict occurs. In this PRIO Paper, we
provide an empirically grounded overview of global trends in conflict, which aims to support
policy­makers, practitioners, and regional and country experts to better understand the contexts
in which they work.

The analyses in this paper are based on the rich conflict data that is collected and published
annually by the Uppsala Conflict Data Program (UCDP). We provide an overview of various
aspects of conflict trends at the global level, particularly since 1989. We examine three types of
violence measured by the UCDP: state-based conflicts, non-state conflicts and one-sided violence.
More specific definitions of these terms are laid out in Section 1.1.1

1.1. Definitions

State-based conflict: a contested incompatibility over government and/or territory, where at
least one party is a state and the use of armed force results in at least 25 battle-related deaths
within a calendar year.

Non-state conflict: the use of armed force between organized groups, none of which is the gov-
ernment of a state, resulting in at least 25 battle-related deaths within a year.

One-sided violence: the use of armed force against civilians – by the government of a state or
by a formally organized group – that results in at least 25 battle-related deaths per year. Extra­
judicial killings in custody are excluded.

Battle-related deaths: fatalities caused by the warring parties that can be directly related to
combat, including civilian losses.

War: a conflict or dyad that reaches at least 1,000 battle-related deaths in a calendar year.2

based (Section 3), non-state (Section 4) and one-sided violence (Section 5) – as shown in Figure 1.

3. State-based conflict

The UCDP categorizes four different types of state-based conflicts: colonial conflict; interstate
conflict; civil conflict; and internationalized civil conflict. Of these, civil conflicts are the most
common.

To be classified as a state-based conflict, there must be a contested incompatibility over govern-
ment control (e.g. between the government of Mali and Jama’at Nusrat al-Islam wal-Muslimin
(JNIM)) and/or territory (e.g. between the government of Israel and Hamas over Palestine), where
violent conflict between two armed groups results in at least 25 battle-related deaths in a calen-
dar year. This measure is crucial, as it indicates the severity of a conflict and helps distinguish
between conflicts and wars.

3.1. Global trends in state-based conflict

The level of state-based conflicts has fluctuated over time. Figure 3 depicts the number and type
of state-based conflicts between 1946 and 2025. The black line shows the number of battle-related
deaths per year. The number of battle-related deaths in state-based conflict in 2025 was approxi-
mately 153,000, approximately the same level as in 2024, and the fourth highest recorded since
the end of the Cold War, only surpassed by those recorded in 2021, 2022 and 2024.

Based on an analysis of state-based armed conflicts over time, we can identify four main trends.

3.1.1. Battle-related deaths

First, despite a sharp decrease in battle-related deaths between 2022 and 2023, the past five years
have been the most violent period since the end of the Cold War. Between 2021 and 2025, more
than 930,000 people were killed in conflict. This is approximately the same as the total n­ umber
recorded in the 20-year period prior to 2021. Three wars have driven this increase. The war
between the government of Ethiopia and the Tigray People’s Liberation Front (TPLF) accounted
for over 288,000 deaths in 2021 and 2022. Russia’s ongoing invasion of Ukraine has resulted in
approximately 370,000 battle-related deaths over the past three years (2022–2025), which is the
highest recorded number of battle deaths for a single conflict since 1989. The Israel–Gaza war
caused an estimated 70,000 deaths between 2023 and 2025. While the ceasefire between Israel
and Hamas has de-escalated the conflict in Palestine, we have also seen an escalation in the con-
flict in southern Lebanon between Israel and Hezbollah, a trend continuing in 2026. In addition,
the brutal conflict in Sudan between rival factions of the military government severely escalated
in 2025, with over 12,000 reportedly killed as part of the civil war. However, the vast majority of
battle-related deaths in Sudan are linked to one-sided violence directed towards civilians by either
the government, the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) or Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF), generating
a total of 75,000 recorded battle-related deaths in 2025. This violence has precipitated the most
severe humanitarian crisis currently facing Africa, with incalculable numbers of indirect conflict
deaths as well.

3.1.2. Increasing number of conflicts

Second, after a decline in state-based conflicts in the 1990s, the past decade has seen a troubling
resurgence. In each of the last 11 years, more than 50 state-based conflicts have been recorded
annually, peaking in 2025 with 65. The early 1990s mark the only other period since 1946 with
comparable levels of conflict. That surge can be explained by two main factors.

First, during the Cold War, the United States and the Soviet Union often supported different
sides in civil wars. Thus, the warring parties had easier access to funding, and we saw an accu-
mulation of civil conflicts during the 1980s and early 1990s (Mumford, 2013). Second, the disso-
lution of the Soviet Union and Yugoslavia triggered a series of new conflicts. However, with the
end of the Cold War, the funding for civil wars dried up, and many of the conflicts related to the
former Soviet Union were relatively short lived, leading to a substantial decrease in conflict in
the late 1990s and 2000s.

The rise in state-based conflicts since 2015 can partly be attributed to the expansion of the
Islamic State (IS) across Asia, Africa and the Middle East. In many cases, IS has become involved

                                                                                                                13

in pre-existing conflicts, such as Boko Haram’s in Nigeria, or the Mindanao conflict in the Phil-
ippines. However, because the UCDP dataset records each IS-related engagement as a separate
conflict, this has contributed to a marked increase in the overall number of state-based conflicts.
Notably, IS was active in 12 countries in 2025 – the lowest level since 2015 – suggesting that the
organization is no longer the main driver of the rising number of conflicts. On the other hand,
the number has been stable for several years, suggesting that it has become well established in
these countries. This points to the growing influence of other non-state actors. For example, the
JNIM – a jihadist coalition formed in 2017 through the merger of several Islamist groups – has
significantly expanded its operations across West Africa and was active in five countries in the
region in 2025.

3.1.3. Conflicts between states

The third main trend emerging from the data is the growing internationalization of conflicts.
While civil conflict has been the most common conflict type since the 1960s, in the past years we
have seen an increase in the number of interstate conflicts (i.e. conflicts between countries). In
2024, the UCDP recorded four international conflicts – the highest number since 1983. However,
this number increased to eight in 2025, the highest recorded in the post–World War II period.
This trend reflects an increasing tension in the world, where protectionism and aggression are
becoming more and more common.

We can split the international conflicts broadly into three categories (see Figure 4): border con-
flicts, regional escalatory conflicts and invasions. The first category includes conflicts over dis-
puted borders or border territory. For example, the longstanding Kashmir conflict between India
and Pakistan flared up in 2025 for the first time since 2020. In addition, enduring tensions along
the Afghan and Pakistani border, as well as between Thailand and Cambodia, also escalated to
conflict levels in 2025. While all of these conflicts are separate, they represent increased tension
on the global scene, and countries’ inclination to protect themselves.

The second group – regional escalatory conflicts – relates specifically to the conflict in the Middle
East, and the increasing hostility between the Arab countries in the region, Israel and the USA,
and to some extent the Western sphere. These are tensions we see growing in 2026, which could
potentially escalate to a larger regional war.

In the third category, we have two conflicts that can be classified as invasions – the Russian inva-
sion of Ukraine, and Israel’s invasion of Syria in the wake of the fall of the Assad regime. Israel
attacked several Syrian military infrastructures, arguing that this was to protect their short bor-
der with Syria. This conflict could also be argued to be part of the regional escalatory category, as
tensions between Israel and Syria are often related to the conflict between Israel and Iran.

3.1.4. Complexity of conflicts

Another emerging trend is the growing complexity of conflict. The data shows that a single
country can host several conflicts. Figure 5 illustrates this trend by comparing the total number
of conflicts (blue line) with the number of countries affected by conflict per year (blue bar).
Up until 2012, the ratio of countries and conflicts was relatively stable: the number of conflicts
slightly exceeded the number of countries, indicating that some countries faced more than one
conflict in a given year. However, after 2012, the gap widened – conflicts became more numer-
ous, while the number of affected countries declined. In 2025, 65 conflicts affected 35 countries.5
This suggests that global conflict levels have not necessarily risen, but that some countries expe-
rience more conflict (a list of all conflict countries can be found at the end of this report). There
could be many reasons for why we see this trend: past conflict increases the likelihood of new

                                                                                                                15

conflict; conflict parties splinter and generate more and new conflicts; weak states cannot control
the monopoly of violence; and the lines between political violence, crime and militias become
indistinct.

3.2. Trends in conflict intensity

Typically, a few high-intensity conflicts – referred to as wars – account for the majority of global
battle-related deaths. Although wars are fewer in number than conflicts, they cause significantly
more fatalities. Figure 6 distinguishes between conflicts and wars. The number of wars peaked
in 2014, reaching 13 – nearly 30% of the total number of violent conflicts. From 2018, the number
of wars began to decline. However, this trend reversed in 2022, with the number rising again.
This trend continued in 2025, reaching the recorded number of 13 wars again, although as the
total number of conflicts has increased, its share is only 20%. These 13 wars included Burkina
Faso, the DRC, Ethiopia, Haiti, Iran–Israel/USA, Israel (Gaza), Mali, Myanmar, Nigeria, Paki-
stan, Russia–U­ kraine, Somalia and Sudan–RSF/SFA.

3.3. Regional variation in state-based conflict

Figure 7 presents regional trends from 1946 to 2025. In 2025, Africa remained the region with
the highest number of state-based conflict (29), followed by Asia (19), the Middle East (13), the
Americas (2) and Europe (1). Compared to a decade ago, the number of conflicts in Africa has
nearly doubled, from 15 in 2013 to 29 in 2025, mainly due to an increase in the number of con-
flicts per country, rather than new conflict countries entering the data. Over the same period, the
continent has also experienced increasingly brutal conflicts, particularly in Ethiopia, Sudan and
the DRC.

We also saw an increase in the number of conflicts in the Middle East from 10 in 2024 to 13 in
2025. This is the highest recorded number of conflicts in the region since 1946. In Asia, we saw
an increase from 17 to 19, he highest seen in Asia since 1994. These regional trends suggest that
the increase in violence is not limited to one region, but is evident across multiple areas. On the
other hand, in the Americas the situation is stable with two conflicts (Haiti and Colombia). How-
ever, we saw a substantial increase in violence in Haiti, from approximately 200 battle deaths in
2024 to more than 1,200 in 2025. Violence there surged in 2025 due to the near collapse of state
authority, coordinated gang expansion, a prolonged political vacuum, a humanitarian crisis and a
delayed international security response. This is a conflict that has received little attention both in
the media and among decisionmakers. Europe is the only region with a decrease in its number
of conflicts from three to one; the two that were previously noted were minor internal conflicts in
Russia against IS and the Freedom of Russia Legion (LSR).

4. Non-state conflicts

Conflicts do not need to involve a state government to inflict considerable human suffering. In
fact, much of today’s violence occurs between organized groups without governmental links.
These groups take many forms, as discussed in Section 3.1.

4.1. Global trends in non-state violence

Non-state conflicts have increased over the past decade. In 2025, there were 75 non-state conflicts,
a slight decrease from 79 in 2024. As Figure 9 indicates, the number of non-state conflicts has
stabilized at a considerably higher level than we saw a decade ago. The UCDP identifies three
types of non-state groups: (i) formally organized groups, which are any non-governmental actors
who have publicly named themselves and use armed force against other similarly organized
groups; (ii) informally organized groups – typically supporters of political parties; and (iii) infor-
mally organized (communal conflicts) groups – loosely organized actors linked by shared identity
markers such as religion, ethnicity, nationality, tribe or clan.

The rise in non-state conflicts is primarily driven by a sharp increase in the number of con-
flicts between formally organized groups. Conflicts between informally organized groups have
remained low in recent years – generally fewer than two per year – while communal conflicts
have increased gradually. The graph indicates that the number of non-state conflicts seems to
have stabilized over the past decade at between 75 and 85 conflicts. Figure 9 also shows the num-
ber of battle-related deaths by type of non-state group. In total, non-state conflicts caused approxi-
mately 14,500 battle-related deaths in 2025, marking a slight decrease from 2024. This decrease is
driven by a decline in battle-related deaths among drug cartels in Mexico, where we have seen a
change from lethal violence to other types of violence such as disappearances and extortion.

4.2. Regional variation

Figure 10 illustrates regional trends in non-state conflicts from 1989 to 2025. Africa recorded
the highest number in 2025, with 34, closely followed by the Americas with 32. However, both
regions have seen a decrease over the past years.

While both Africa and the Americas host a high number of non-state conflicts, they involve dif-
ferent types of non-state actors. In the Americas, non-state conflicts are more likely to involve
highly organized actors, while in Africa, communal conflicts are more common. The Middle
East, which saw the largest increase in non-state conflicts during the 2010s, has since experi-
enced a sharp decline. But in 2025, we saw an increase from three to eight, which coincides with
the increase in state-based conflicts in the region. Europe and Asia continue to report relatively
low levels of non-state conflicts.

5. One-sided violence

Civilians often bear the brunt of violence in ongoing conflicts, whether state-based or non-state.
The UCDP definition of one-sided violence excludes extrajudicial killings, which means that vio-
lence perpetrated by governments is likely to be under-reported in the data.

5.1. Global trends in one-sided violence

Overall, fatalities from one-sided violence rose from 14,200 in 2024 to 76,500 in 2025. This
extreme increase can be explained by the ongoing violence in Sudan – approximately 62,500 of
the number are fatalities registered in Sudan. Most of these – approximately 60,000 – are related
to the siege and massacre in El Fasher, the capital of North Darfur. The El Fasher massacre was
a large-scale, ethnically targeted killing of civilians following the RSF takeover of North Darfur’s
last major city in October 2025, after a prolonged siege, and has been described by UN investiga-
tors as bearing the hallmarks of genocide.6

Figure 11 illustrates the number of perpetrators of one-sided violence per year by actor type – gov-
ernment or non-state actor (left) – alongside the number of resulting fatalities (right). As well as the
massive killings in Sudan in 2025, we also saw high levels of one-sided violence by non-state actors
in the DRC, including IS-related violence. Another notable episode of violence was the post-election
violence in Tanzania in November and December 2025, with more than 1,000 fatalities resulting
from the Tanzanian security forces’ crackdown on protests. This is significant, as the UCDP data
has never recorded this level of violence in Tanzania before in any of the three categories of conflict.

Since the 2000s, the number of actors perpetrating one-sided violence has steadily increased,
remaining above 40 since 2019. In 2025, 55 actors were recorded – 17 government and 38 non-
state actors. This is the highest number recorded since 1989.

6. Conclusion

In this PRIO Paper, we have provided an overview of global trends in state-based conflicts, non-
state conflicts and one-sided violence from 1946 to 2025. By disaggregating the data to regional
and country levels, we have aimed to acquire a better understanding of contemporary conflict
patterns.

What does the data tell us about state-based conflicts in 2025? The level of political violence has
continued to escalate, and we saw a staggering 245,000 killed. The main share of this relates to
the war between Russia and Ukraine, the bombing of Gaza, as well as the one-sided violence in
Sudan. But we also saw an increasing number of conflicts reach the threshold of war (i.e. more
than 1,000 battle-related deaths). The level of violence remains high by historical standards, mak-
ing 2025 the third most violent year since the end of the Cold War.

A total of 65 state-based conflicts were recorded in 35 conflict-affected countries, a new record,
with 6 more conflicts than in 2024. The main share of these came from civil conflict, although
we also saw a worrying trend of increased international conflicts. In 2025, eight international
conflicts were recorded, a doubling from 2024 and the highest since 1946. These can broadly be
divided into invasions, border conflicts and regional escalations in the Middle East.

The number of non-state conflicts declined from 79 in 2023 to 75 in 2025. Battle-related deaths in
these conflicts reached 14,500 in 2025, a decrease from 2024 that can be ascribed to a reduction
in lethal violence between drug cartels in Mexico. Africa and the Americas remained the regions
with the highest number of non-state conflicts, although the nature of these conflicts differs sig-
nificantly. In the Americas, they are often driven by highly organized groups such as gangs and
drug cartels. In contrast, non-state conflicts in Africa are more frequently communal in nature.

In 2025, almost 76,500 fatalities were attributed to one-sided violence, most relating to the vio-
lence in Sudan, and the highest recorded number since the genocide in Rwanda in 1994. While
non-state actors were responsible for the majority of these fatalities, 17 governments also perpe-
trated one-sided violence. This type of violence often occurs within the context of state-based con-
flicts, as seen in Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and the conflict in the DRC. However, similarly to
non-state conflicts, there is an increase in one-sided violence beyond active war zones – particu-
larly in the Americas. Countries like Brazil and Mexico, which are not experiencing state-based
conflicts, have nonetheless seen a substantial amount of this type of violence.

This PRIO Paper confirms that the level of violence is increasing, that this now is part of a longer
trend and not just a peak, and that a new, higher baseline of global violence has emerged. 2025
was the third most deadly year since the end of the Cold War, and we see that the period post-
2013 has overall been more violent than the period prior to 2013 during the Cold War era. The
growing number of international conflicts reflects an increasing global tension. Growing com-
plexity poses serious challenges for international and humanitarian actors, such as in Sudan,

making it more and more difficult to navigate and operate within the conflict landscape. But we
also see new conflicts emerging, for example in Haiti, where the relatively new state-based con-
flict caused more than 1,000 battle deaths, and in the relatively stable and historically peaceful
country of Tanzania, which saw more than 1,000 people killed by security forces in post-election
protests.
