Abstract

This article examines global trends in organized violence from 1989 to 2025 using new data from the Uppsala Conflict Data Program (UCDP). In 2025, fatalities increased sharply to 244,600, up from approximately 187,000 in 2024. This marked a significant rise, with one-sided violence seeing a fivefold increase, largely driven by large-scale violence against civilians in Sudan, resulting in the highest fatality levels since the Rwandan genocide in 1994. The number of state-based conflicts reached a record high of 65, with interstate conflicts doubling for the second consecutive year, highlighting an increasing trend in international tensions. State-based fatalities, though slightly lower than in previous years, remained high at 153,600, reflecting the intensity of ongoing armed conflicts, particularly in Ukraine, Israel, and Sudan. Non-state violence, on the other hand, continued its decline, with fatalities dropping to their lowest level since 2013. These developments take place in a broader context of mass mobilization, where some events escalate into violence or are met with repression, sometimes overlapping with patterns of organized violence. To capture this dynamic, the article also presents updated data on violent political protests, an established but intermittently published UCDP category, extended here to 2025.

Issue Section: Special Data Feature
Organized violence 1989–2025

In 2025, fatalities in organized violence increased sharply, from about 187,000 in 2024 to 244,600 in 2025.1 As depicted in Figure 1, fatalities were the second highest recorded since the Rwandan genocide in 1994, surpassed only by 2022. This increase was driven by a fivefold increase in one-sided violence, from 14,300 in 2024 to 76,500 in 2025, with violence in Sudan pushing fatalities to their highest level since 1994. The number of state-based conflicts, where at least one of the parties is the government of a state, rose significantly, reaching a record high of 65. Within this category, interstate conflicts rose particularly sharply, doubling for the second consecutive year to eight in 2025. State-based fatalities decreased marginally, but with 153,600 fatalities recorded, they remained at extremely elevated levels, with only 2021, 2022, and 2024 being more violent in the post-1989 period. Non-state violence decreased in both conflicts and fatalities, continuing a downward trajectory since its latest peak in 2021. Non-state violence reached its lowest level since 2013, yet remains deadlier than at any time prior to then, other than 1993.

Figure 1 demonstrates that violence in all three categories of organized violence has increased substantially over the past 15 years. In 2025, Uppsala Conflict Data Program (UCDP) recorded seven times as many fatalities from organized violence as in 2010, despite a sharp decrease from the peak in 2022. Most notably, the number of state-based conflicts has more than doubled since 2010, from 31 to 65 in 2025, and fatalities have increased from 21,700 to 153,600.

Figure 2 shows the geographical distribution of fatalities in 2025, with violence concentrated in a limited number of locations. The surge in one-sided violence was largely driven by Sudan, while state-based violence was concentrated in a small number of high-intensity conflicts, including Ukraine and Gaza.

This article presents trends in the three types of organized violence with an emphasis on 2025, beginning with a section on trends in state-based conflict, followed by sections on non-state conflict and one-sided violence. The final section discusses violent political protests, a fourth category of violence collected by UCDP, which has only been intermittently published (e.g., Svensson et al., 2022).

State-based armed conflict

Uppsala Conflict Data Program recorded 153,600 fatalities in 2025, similar to the 155,000 recorded in 2024.2 Fatalities remain among the highest levels in the post-1989 period, with only 2021, 2022, and 2024 being more violent. Prior to 2021, only 2014 and 2015 exceeded 100,000 fatalities. In 2025, fatalities rose sharply in all regions except for Europe, which saw a slight decline, and the Middle East, where fatalities decreased significantly.

The number of conflicts documented by UCDP increased in 2025 to 65, the highest number since data collection started in 1946, and up significantly from its previous peak of 59 in 2024 and 2023.3 The number of conflicts began rising in 2014, closely tied to the eruption of conflicts around the Arab Spring and the rise of Islamic State (IS). The number of IS-related conflicts peaked at 18 in 2017 and 2019 and has since declined to 13 in 2025. The latest increase was instead driven by a near-global escalation, with the Middle East, Asia, and Africa at or near their peaks in the number of conflicts.

Figure 3 illustrates that Africa, with 29 conflicts, witnessed the most conflicts since 2020, and the second highest number on record. Asia had the most conflicts since 1994 and, at 19, just one fewer than its peak in 1990. With 14 state-based conflicts, the Middle East recorded its highest level ever, largely driven by several interstate conflicts connected to Israel's war in Gaza. Although the number of conflicts in the Americas remained stable, the region witnessed its first war since 2005, after the conflict over government in Haiti escalated dramatically in 2025. The only region to see fewer conflicts was Europe, which however continued to suffer the highest overall level of state-based violence due to the war in Ukraine. Uppsala Conflict Data Program recorded 13 wars in 2025, 2 more than in 2024. This equals the peak in 2013, and there have not been more wars since 1992.

For the second consecutive year, the number of interstate conflicts doubled, reaching eight in 2025, up from four in 2024 and two in 2023. Figure 4 shows that this is the highest number recorded in the post-World War II period by a fair margin, with the previous high of five recorded in 1987. This reflects a trend in which interstate conflicts have become increasingly common since 2016. The first few months of 2026 suggest that this trend is likely to continue. The first decade of the 2000s saw 8 interstate conflicts, while the 2010s saw 12. The 2020s have thus far seen 22. With the exception of 2023, each year since 2020 has witnessed at least one new or reactivated territorial dispute between states, with two such cases recorded in 2024 and three in 2025.

A widening array of states has engaged in interstate conflicts, as well as militarily supported non-state actors fighting against governments, challenging the norms of the international rules-based order upheld by U.S. hegemony. Now the United States itself is turning against the world order it built, as expressed in its 2025 National Security Strategy (The White House, 2025). The extremely high number of conflicts and wars recorded in 2025, particularly the record number of interstate conflicts, lends credence to a growing number of voices arguing that we are witnessing the end of Pax Americana and the liberal world order (Ilves, 2026; Kagan, 2026). Half of all interstate conflicts in 2025 took place in the Middle East, including three involving Israel. A further two involved Pakistan, indicating that while there has been a sharp increase in interstate conflict, it remains concentrated in relatively few countries.

All four interstate conflicts active in 2024 continued in 2025, with four new conflicts erupting during the year. Of these, the India–Pakistan conflict has been intermittently active for several decades, most recently in 2020. Two others, the Israel–Syria and Cambodia–Thailand conflicts, are long latent territorial disputes, last active in 1973 and 2011, respectively. With the January 2026 attack by the United States on Venezuela, Africa is the only region with no active or recent interstate conflicts, having last experienced an active interstate conflict in 2016. Recurring territorial disputes in the Horn of Africa show that interstate conflict is a distinct possibility in Africa as well.

The Israel–Yemen conflict was active for the first time in 2025, having spiraled out of Yemen's Houthi-regime's support for Hamas. In late 2024, Israel announced its intent to dismantle the Houthi regime and in May and August launched campaigns of airstrikes which killed its prime minister. These attacks followed immediately upon an intensive U.S. air campaign, with limited British support, which had killed nearly 800 people in Yemen since mid-March.

For the first time since 1987, two interstate conflicts, those between Russia–Ukraine and Iran–Israel, surpassed the threshold for war. The Iran–Israel conflict has been active since 2018 and largely entailed Israeli airstrikes on Iranian military installations in Syria. It escalated sharply in 2025, with over 1,200 deaths, most resulting from Israel's 12-day bombing campaign of Iran.

The interstate war between Russia and Ukraine remained the deadliest conflict in the world for the third consecutive year. Uppsala Conflict Data Program recorded at least 94,700 fatalities, representing around 62% of global battle-related deaths, down from 102,100 in 2024. As has been the case throughout the war, except for 2022, combatant fatalities dominated. Each year, confirmed Russian battlefield losses have increased, from 42,000 in 2022 to 77,700 in 2025, despite delays in reporting. In contrast, confirmed Ukrainian losses have remained relatively stable at between 21,000 and 24,000 deaths per year, though they declined to 14,000 in 2025. Large numbers of soldiers have been reported missing instead of dead. Combined with delays in reporting from a high-intensity conflict environment, all these numbers, particularly the most recent, can be expected to rise significantly as more information becomes available.

The war between Israel and Hamas remained the second deadliest state-based conflict during the year, as it has each year since 2023. In 2025, 14,400 fatalities were recorded, down by a third from 21,600 in 2024. Two ceasefires were agreed to during the year, the first in January, which collapsed amid a renewed Israeli offensive in March, and a second in October, which resulted in violence decreasing sharply. The conflict had severe spillover effects in the broader region, with Israel fighting one further intrastate conflict against Hezbollah in Lebanon, and three interstate conflicts against Syria, the Sanaa-based Houthi government of Yemen, and Iran.

The third deadliest conflict was the war in Sudan with the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), since February 2025 part of a broader coalition called Sudan Founding Alliance (SFA), which killed around 12,200 people in battle-related violence in 2025, up from at least 4,700 in 2024. Tens of thousands were also killed in intense one-sided violence (discussed below). A further six wars, including the four next deadliest conflicts, took place in Africa, contributing to making Africa the region with the second highest number of state-based fatalities. With 32,500 deaths recorded in 2025, an 87% increase from 2024, Africa suffered the third highest number of deaths since 2000, surpassed only by 2021 and 2022 during the Tigray war.

Violence in both Asia and the Americas increased significantly, though from relatively low levels. In 2025, fatalities in Asia rose by 35%, to 6,600, yet remained far below its peak of nearly 38,000 in 2021. This latest increase in Asia was largely driven by escalating violence in Pakistan, where both the Taleban Movement of Pakistan (TTP) and a Baluch insurgency have increased their attacks. The Americas, while struggling with intense non-state violence between cartels, have been relatively spared from state-based violence during the past 20 years. Yet, state-based violence rose sharply from 400 deaths in 2024 to 1,500 in 2025, with the conflict in Haiti, fought against a coalition of organized crime groups intent on overthrowing the government, becoming the region's first war since 2005.

Non-state conflict

For the fourth consecutive year, fatalities from non-state conflicts declined, from 17,700 in 2024 to 14,500 in 2025. Figure 5 shows that this is the lowest number of deaths recorded since 2013, representing a 35% decrease from the peak in 2021. The number of active non-state conflicts also fell, from 79 in 2024 to 75 in 2025, the lowest level since 2019.

Two regions drove this downward trend: Asia and the Americas. In Asia, UCDP registered unusually high levels of non-state violence in 2024, following clashes between supporters of Prime Minister Hasina and her opponents in Bangladesh, as well as intensified sectarian clashes in Pakistan. In 2025, violence returned to the relatively low levels that have characterized the region since 2018.

In the Americas, UCDP has recorded a clear downward trend since 2021. In both Brazil and Mexico, violence decreased substantially in 2025, contributing to the lowest number of fatalities in the region since 2016. Nonetheless, almost 7,800 deaths were recorded in Mexico alone, accounting for over half of all non-state conflict fatalities globally. The internal power struggle within the Sinaloa Cartel continued, with clashes between Los Chapitos and Los Mayos resulting in over 1,000 deaths in 2025.

The Jalisco New Generation Cartel (CJNG), which has dominated drug-related violence in Mexico in recent years, remains one of the deadliest organized crime groups globally. CJNG was active in 7 separate non-state conflicts in 2025, resulting in nearly 4,700 fatalities. This nevertheless represents a decrease from 2024, when violence involving CJNG caused nearly 7,500 deaths. The group has consolidated territorial control in previously contested areas, contributing to reduced levels of violence.

In February 2026, CJNG leader Nemesio Oseguera Cervantes “El Mencho” was killed during a military raid in Mexico. This triggered a wave of retaliatory attacks, with CJNG stepping up its attacks against state forces. The effect of El Mencho's death on non-state conflict remains uncertain. However, previous cases of arrests or eliminations of cartel leaders have often led to increased violence as successors and internal factions compete to fill the power vacuum.

The decline in non-state conflict fatalities in Mexico corresponds with an overall decrease in recorded homicides. This trend may reflect more targeted security efforts, although their specific impact remains difficult to isolate. At the same time, national-level declines mask significant geographic variation, with violence increasingly concentrated in a limited number of states. Moreover, rising levels of disappearances suggest a possible displacement or undercounting of lethal violence.

In Brazil, fighting between the country's largest gangs, Comando Vermelho and Primeiro Comando da Capital (PCC), and their local affiliates continued. Despite this, organized crime violence declined in 2025, partly attributed to a truce between the two groups from February to April, alongside a broader reduction in homicide levels. As in Mexico, high numbers of disappearances may indicate high levels of unreported fatalities.

This overall decline was not universal. Two regions, Africa and the Middle East, experienced increases in non-state conflict in 2025. In Africa, fighting between Puntland state forces in Somalia and IS resulted in more than 770 deaths, following an operation aimed at pushing IS from its territorial strongholds in the Bari Mountains. Violence also escalated in DR Congo, where the Rwanda-backed Congo River Alliance (French: Alliance Fleuve Congo; AFC) clashed with pro-government patriotic defense volunteers (VDP), closely linked to the ongoing state-based conflict in North Kivu.

The Middle East dominated the non-state category for many years during the Syrian civil war, being the most violent region between 2014 and 2017. Although violence decreased substantially after 2018, a slight increase has been observed over the past 2 years, driven primarily by developments in Syria following the collapse of the Assad regime in December 2024. The breakdown of the centralized state authority allowed long-standing local grievances to surface. In Suwayda province, tensions escalated between the Suwayda Military Council, representing the Druze minority, and local Bedouin tribes organized under the Southern Tribes Gathering. In addition, clashes between the Turkey-backed Syria National Army (SNA) and the Kurdish-led alliance Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF) continued in 2025. Following the formal integration of SNA into the transitional government under President Ahmed al-Sharaa on January 29, 2025, such clashes are classified as state-based rather than non-state conflict.

One-sided violence

In 2025, UCDP reported the highest number of fatalities from one-sided violence since the Rwandan genocide in 1994, with nearly 76,500 deaths recorded across 32 countries. This represents an increase of 438% compared to the 14,200 fatalities registered in 2024. The number of actors involved also increased slightly, from 54 in 2024 to 55 in 2025, the highest number ever recorded by UCDP.

The increase in fatalities was driven primarily by events in Sudan, linked to the civil war between the government and the paramilitary coalition SFA. Both warring parties have systematically targeted civilians since the war began in 2023, executing those accused of supporting the opposing side.

The besieged city of Al Fashir, the last stronghold of the Sudanese government in Darfur, fell to SFA in October 2025, leaving around 250,000 civilians trapped. In the weeks that followed, SFA carried out extensive massacres and has been accused of committing genocide. Although access to the area remains restricted, analyses based on satellite imagery and survivor interviews estimate that at least 60,000 civilians had been killed by mid-December.

Figure 6 shows that the massacres in Al Fashir pushed one-sided violence in Africa to its highest levels since the Rwandan genocide in 1994. Even excluding these events, however, Africa dominated the trend in one-sided violence. In DR Congo, AFC intensified attacks against civilians accused of supporting the Congolese government and its allies. Almost 1,400 civilians were executed by AFC in 2025, many of them farmers killed while working their land.

A different pattern emerged in Tanzania, where protests surrounding the October 2025 elections led to a violent crackdown by government forces. Although largely peaceful, the protests were met with violence from the security forces, and in some locations, protesters also took up arms. As a conservative estimate, at least 1,000 civilians were killed by the government in one-sided violence, while an additional 360 people died in violent political protests, as recorded in the violent political protest (VPP) category of UCDP violence discussed below. Fatality estimates remain highly uncertain, partly due to the internet shutdown imposed by the government.

Another region that experienced increasing levels of one-sided violence was the Middle East. As shown in Figure 6, UCDP recorded at least 2,100 deaths in 2025, the highest number since 2014. The majority of these fatalities occurred in Syria, particularly in Latakia province in the west of the country. Following the fall of the Assad regime, the transitional government struggled to control local militias and manage rising ethnic tensions in the country.

In March 2025, militants loyal to the ousted president Bashar al-Assad launched attacks in coastal areas with large Alawite populations. The interim government responded with widespread executions of Alawite civilians, resulting in nearly 700 deaths within a few days.

In July 2025, tensions escalated in Suwayda province in the south between the Druze minority and local Bedouin tribes. Government intervention led to a complex escalation involving multiple forms of violence: non-state conflict (see above), clashes between government forces and militias aligned under the Suwayda Military Council (which has not stated an incompatibility and is therefore not included in the state-based conflict category), interstate conflict between Syria and Israel, which intervened on behalf of the Druze minority, and extensive one-sided violence carried out by forces linked to the Syrian government.

For the past 10 years, IS has been the deadliest actor in one-sided violence. In 2025, however, this pattern shifted, and SFA replaced IS as the actor responsible for most one-sided killings. Islamic State significantly deescalated its use of one-sided violence in 2025. Uppsala Conflict Data Program registered a 37% decrease from at least 3,800 killings in 2024 to 2,400 in 2025. The IS affiliate in DR Congo, continued to carry out attacks in Ituri and North Kivu provinces, albeit at lower levels than the previous year. The sectarian dimension of this violence continued, with Christians disproportionately targeted.

Over the past 13 years, non-state actors have consistently caused more fatalities from one-sided violence than state actors, and in 2025, they were responsible for 92% of such deaths. However, state actors also escalated their use of violence against civilians, reaching the highest fatality levels since 2004. The most notable cases include the governments of Syria and Tanzania, discussed above, which together accounted for at least 2,700 of the nearly 5,900 fatalities attributed to state actors in 2025.

Trends in UCDP VPP data, 2011–2025

Over the past 15 years, large-scale protest movements have been a recurrent feature in many countries. In many cases, such mobilization has escalated into violence resulting in more than 25 fatalities within a year.

These violent protest episodes do not meet the organizational threshold required for inclusion as intrastate armed conflict but nonetheless constitute serious challenges to state authority and regime control and can generate significant numbers of fatalities. They are also excluded from the one-sided violence dataset, as civilians use armed violence against the state. However, they often occur in contexts where other forms of organized violence are present and may overlap temporally or geographically with state-based, non-state, or one-sided violence.

In this section, we present data extending the UCDP VPP dataset (Svensson et al., 2022) to 2025, together with a new geo-referenced event dataset (GED) covering 2011–2025. A VPP is defined as “… a contested incompatibility that concerns government and/or territory where the use of armed force in civil protest between two parties, of which one is the government of a state and the other an informally organized protest group, results in at least 25 deaths in one calendar year” (Svensson et al., 2022, p.1709). In line with UCDP GED practice, the VPP GED registers the dates and locations of fatal events in violent political protests, allowing for more fine-grained analysis of when and where such events occur.

Figure 7 shows a clear temporal pattern in VPP fatalities, with peaks in 2011, 2013, 2019, 2022, and 2024. The peak in 2011 is driven by the Arab Spring uprisings, which generated fatalities in several countries, including Tunisia, Libya, Egypt, Yemen, and Syria, as well as in the Israeli-occupied territories of Gaza and the West Bank.

The deadliest VPP episode in the time series occurred in Egypt in 2013, with nearly 1,500 fatalities. Protests began in January 2013 against President Mohammad Morsi and the Muslim Brotherhood and eventually led to the military overthrowing the Morsi government on July 3. Protests by Morsi supporters soon erupted against the new government and escalated into clashes with government forces. Violence peaked on August 14 at Rab'a Square in Cairo, where more than 800 people were killed. The harsh government crackdown drove many in the Islamist opposition to organize into formal fighting forces, contributing to escalation in the intrastate conflict in Sinai.

The 2019 peak is more geographically dispersed with deadly protests in Iran, Iraq, Sudan, Haiti, Venezuela, Israel, and Ethiopia. Figure 8 shows this broader geographical distribution and suggests that VPP is not a regional phenomenon but rather a global occurrence. The deadliest episodes were the Bloody Aban protests in Iran with around 700 fatalities, and the Iraqi protests from October to December generating more than 400 fatalities.

The peaks in 2022 and 2024 are driven primarily by events in Asia. In 2022, Kazakhstan, Iran, and Chad experienced major anti-government protests followed by state repression. The 2024 peak is largely driven by the Gen Z Revolution in Bangladesh, which escalated into nationwide violence and political change.

Most instances of VPPs in the dataset concern government power, typically protesting governance issues, corruption, or elections. As shown in Figure 9, protests are a largely urban phenomenon and often concentrated in major urban centers. Territorial incompatibilities are less common but appear in cases such as Kashmir and the Israeli-occupied territories. A notable pattern in the data is recurrence. Several countries experience multiple VPP episodes over time, including DR Congo, Guinea, Iran, Israel, Egypt, and Haiti. Israel stands out with protests in the occupied territories claiming fatalities every year since 2011.

Violent political protest events often occur alongside other forms of organized violence recorded by the UCDP. The case of Bangladesh in 2024 illustrates this clearly. That year, UCDP registered around 1,200 deaths across three different types of violence. In some areas, Bangladeshi state forces used violence against peaceful protests, as recorded in the one-sided violence dataset. In other incidents, supporters of Prime Minister Hasina and her opponents clashed, as recorded in the non-state conflict dataset. Finally, state forces clashed with protesters, commonly armed with sticks, stones, and metal rods, as recorded in the VPP dataset. This highlights the value of disaggregating different types of violence within the same context, to be able to study their different dynamics and patterns.

Similar patterns are visible in Nepal and Tanzania in 2025, with Tanzania becoming the deadliest case. Following the October general election, a wave of largely youth-driven demonstrations erupted and was met by lethal force and mass arrests by the government. The protests in Tanzania marked a significant change in the country's political stability and illustrate how elections can trigger different types of UCDP organized violence.

The year ended with large-scale and widespread antiregime protests in Iran, starting on December 28 and escalating further in 2026. As in previous episodes, they built on earlier waves of protests. In 2019, nationwide demonstrations triggered by fuel price increases evolved into broader antiregime protests. The unrest spread to over 100 cities and led to a severe government crackdown resulting in almost 700 fatalities recorded in the VPP dataset, in addition to several hundred in the one-sided violence category. The protests became known as the Bloody Aban and subsided in early 2020. Another wave emerged in 2022, triggered by the death of Mahsa Amini after her arrest by the morality police. Hundreds of civilians were killed before the protests subsided in early 2023, both in one-sided violence and in violent political protests.

Concluding remarks

In 2025, fatalities from organized violence increased significantly to 244,600, up from approximately 187,000 in 2024, largely driven by one-sided violence in Sudan, which pushed fatality numbers to their highest levels since the Rwandan genocide in 1994. The number of state-based conflicts reached a record high of 65, with interstate conflicts doubling for the second consecutive year. The number of wars increased to 13, matching the previous peak recorded in 2013. The last time the figure was higher was in 1992. Taken together, the past decade has been characterized by an increase in both internationalized and intrastate armed conflict. In 2025, for the first time since 1987, two interstate conflicts, those between Russia–Ukraine and Iran–Israel, surpassed the threshold for war. Non-state violence, on the other hand, continued its decline, with fatalities dropping to their lowest level since 2013. These trends take place in a broader context of mass mobilization. While most protests remain nonviolent, some escalate into violence or are met with repression, and in some cases overlap temporally or geographically with state-based, non-state, or one-sided violence. Updated data on VPPs highlight that deadly protests can coexist with other forms of UCDP organized violence.
