Might I Interest You in an Extended Warranty?

Hyeong-Tak Lee and Sriram Venkataraman, Kenan-Flagler Business School, University of North Carolina - Chapel Hill. July 1, 2016. (Presented at an FTC public event / economics conference.)

Abstract
Retailers routinely market extended warranties to durable-goods buyers. Extended warranties are optional and need be purchased at an additional cost. However, manufacturer-backed base warranties come bundled with the product at no additional cost. The question of whether and how base warranties affect buyers' purchase of extended warranties remains central to how and to whom extended warranties get marketed. We test the impact of base warranties on the purchase-incidence rate of extended warranties in the used-vehicles market. In this market, two otherwise identical vehicles may only differ (marginally) in the amount of their residual base warranties. Additionally, the expiry terms of the base warranties are a deterministic function of the vehicle exceeding certain pre-determined cutoffs (mileage cutoffs). We employ a regression discontinuity design by comparing extended-warranty purchase-incidence rates just below and above the expiry of manufacturer-backed warranties. We find that used-vehicle buyers do indeed adjust their likelihood of purchase of extended warranties in a statistically and economically significant way in response to the stock of residual base warranty still available in the vehicle purchased. We quantify the window of opportunity wherein auto dealers have the highest likelihood of success selling extended warranties and also quantify how this window of opportunity varies by country of origin of the automaker.

1 Introduction
The automobile industry contributes approximately 3.6%, or $500 billion, to U.S. GDP output (Bureau of Labor Statistics 2009). This study investigates the aggressive marketing of extended warranties by auto dealers. Extended warranties are marketed by dealers after the buyer commits to the purchase of the vehicle (new and used). An extended warranty is an agreement between an administrator and a vehicle owner, wherein the administrator agrees to pay for the replacement or repair, for a specific coverage period, of vehicle parts in the event of a mechanical breakdown. Unlike base warranties, which are provided by manufacturers and come bundled with the product at no additional cost to the buyer, extended warranties are optional and can be purchased by the buyer separately at an additional cost. Extended warranties are purchased by buyers so as to insure themselves against the risk of product failure (after the base warranty expires), and supplement manufacturers' original warranties, but do not usually cover routine maintenance or repairs due to excessive use.

Footnote: Prices charged by the dealers for extended warranties are never advertised, which severely limits the ability of the buyer to engage in price comparisons. Extended warranties can be underwritten by either the manufacturer or independent third parties. Coverage of the extended warranty kicks in after the vehicle's bumper-to-bumper base warranty expires.

In 2012 alone, consumers spent $14.7 billion on extended service contracts (Source: Warranty Week). Very little empirical research exists on this topic prior to this study. In the U.S. auto industry, over the past fifteen years, the purchase rates for extended warranties has risen steadily from 20% to 42% (Warranty Week 2010), despite concerns raised in the popular press that extended warranties are not worth buying. Extended warranties have continued to experience a healthy compound annual growth rate of 13.5% since 2009. A recent study by Polk reports that the average age of vehicles in America climbed to an all-time high of 11.4 years in 2013, up from 9 years in 2006 -- owners are holding on to vehicles longer, well past the base warranty.

Key findings:
1. The purchase rate for extended warranties rises gradually leading up to the expiry of the basic (bumper-to-bumper) warranty.
2. Purchase rates drop by approximately 3 percent at the point when the basic warranty expires.
3. Thereafter, the purchase rate remains constant, at a rate much higher than the pre-basic-warranty-expiry level.

Tradeoffs buyers make between power-train and extended warranties differ from the basic-warranty pattern:
1. Purchase rates remain constant leading up to expiry of the power-train warranty.
2. Purchase rates rise sharply by about 10.2 percent at the point when the power-train warranty expires.
3. Purchase rates then fall steadily to a rate well below the pre-power-train-expiry level.

These findings suggest the most opportune time to market an extended warranty is shortly before the basic warranty expires, and then again at or just after the power-train warranty expires; dealers can harvest many more sales opportunities within this window than outside of it. Net effects also differ by country of origin of the automaker: the drop in purchase rates when the basic warranty expires is more precipitous for domestic vehicles (6.4% versus 3% across all vehicles), while purchase-incidence rates do not change for domestic brands around power-train-warranty expiry. These effects are reversed for foreign automakers: purchase-incidence rates rise by as much as 15% for foreign automakers at the point their power-train warranties expire, but remain unchanged at basic-warranty expiry.

3 Data
The data are transaction-level records for every vehicle purchased at 50 randomly selected dealerships across Georgia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, and Virginia between July 2009 and July 2014, from a major auto-industry market-research firm. For each transaction the data record the VIN, date of purchase, buyer age and ZIP code, odometer mileage, transaction price, trade-in details, and the price paid for the extended warranty (if any).

The full dataset contains 135,813 transactions spanning new and used vehicles. The study limits attention to used-vehicle transactions by individual buyers, and excludes vehicles that already included an extended warranty at purchase (previously purchased or certified pre-owned). The most commonly occurring basic warranties are 3-years/36,000 miles (41.46% of vehicles), 4-years/50,000 miles (39.02%), and 5-years/60,000 miles (7.31%). Power-train warranties range from two to ten years; the most common terms are 4-years/50,000 miles (24.39%) and 5-years/60,000 or 5-years/100,000 miles (21.95%). The most widely offered combination of basic and power-train warranties is 3-years/36,000 miles paired with 5-years/60,000 miles (35.29% of vehicles).

Extended warranties can be sold by the manufacturer, the dealer, or a third party; well-established third-party warranty companies such as GE Capital, Lubrico, Global, and Pafco underwrite extended-warranty contracts sold by dealers. The final estimation sample, after limiting to the top 15 brands by cumulative sales (84.75% of used-vehicle sales in the sample) and applying screening criteria, consists of 20,817 observations spanning 41 dealers, 15 automakers, and 2,216 unique make-model-dealer combinations.

6 Results
The study estimates a local linear regression (regression discontinuity design) around the basic- and power-train-warranty mileage cutoffs (36,000 and 60,000/100,000 miles).

The main finding is a discontinuous change in the probability of purchasing an extended warranty at the expiry of the basic warranty: the purchase rate rises gradually up to the cutoff, then drops discontinuously by 3% at the cutoff, then remains constant afterward at a level higher than its pre-expiry level (settling at the pre-expiry level seen 800 miles before expiry). Buyers of used vehicles that are within 800 miles of the basic warranty's expiry are the most likely to purchase extended warranties of any segment; the next-most-attractive segment for dealers is buyers of vehicles with already-expired basic warranties.

To the left of the power-train-warranty mark (60,000 miles), the attachment rate for extended warranties remains constant. As soon as the power-train warranty expires, the purchase probability jumps discontinuously by 10%, then steadily decreases with mileage; the region from 60,000 to 63,700 miles (3,700 miles post-expiry) is the "sweet spot" for marketing extended warranties on power-train grounds.

The net effect for basic warranties is negative (insurance and self-sorting motives dominate near basic-warranty expiry); the net effect for power-train warranties is positive (signaling motives dominate near power-train-warranty expiry). Given the high profit margins dealerships realize on extended-warranty sales, these effects can have an economically significant impact on dealer revenue from extended-warranty marketing.

Country-of-origin breakdown: compared to the average 3% drop in attachment rate at basic-warranty expiry, domestic brands (Ford, GMC, Chevrolet, Jeep, Dodge, Pontiac, Chrysler, Mercury) show a 6% drop, consistent with domestic brands ranking lower than Japanese rivals on reliability (Consumer Reports 2014). Imported brands (Honda, Toyota, Nissan, Mazda, Volkswagen, Subaru, Scion) show a 15% jump in purchase probability at power-train-warranty expiry, versus the pooled average of 10%, consistent with more expensive and less available aftermarket repair parts for imported vehicles.

Buyers of more expensive used vehicles are more likely to purchase extended warranties, consistent with risk-averse buyers both choosing pricier vehicles and being more likely to insure against failure via an extended warranty.

Robustness: a placebo test running the same regression at other 10,000-mile marks (not tied to warranty expiry) finds no discontinuity in nine of ten cases; a test for confounding by product availability (inventory levels) finds no systematic bias; a test of whether buyers near the cutoffs select different (cheaper or shorter) extended-warranty terms finds no discontinuity in extended-warranty price at the 36,000- or 60,000-mile marks, though at the 100,000-mile mark buyers post-power-train-expiry buy cheaper or less comprehensive extended warranties.

7 Conclusion
The study's "net-effect" findings show the net effect of insurance, signaling, and sorting motives for basic warranties is negative (insurance and sorting motives dominate near the expiry of the basic warranty), while the net effect for power-train warranties is positive (signaling motives dominate near power-train-warranty expiry). As the basic warranty expires there is a discontinuous 3% drop in extended-warranty attachment rate; as the power-train warranty (60,000 miles) expires there is a discontinuous 10% jump. Managerial implication: dealers should target extended-warranty marketing most aggressively at buyers of vehicles within 800 miles of basic-warranty expiry (the single most attractive segment), with buyers of already-expired-basic-warranty vehicles the next-most-attractive segment; for power-train warranties, the 3,700-mile window just after the 60,000-mile power-train-warranty expiry is the most profitable segment to target.

Limitations: this is a descriptive, causal-inference study, not a structural model, so it cannot predict how a change in factory-warranty terms or extended-warranty pricing would change adoption; the analysis covers only used vehicles (new vehicles have too little variation in residual base warranty to apply this design), and excludes a few niche automakers with unusually different base-warranty mileage cutoffs.

DERIVED COMPARISONS
- Extended-warranty purchase (attachment) rate, U.S. auto industry, over roughly 1995-2010: rose from about 20% to about 42% of buyers (compound annual growth of about 13.5% since 2009).
- Discontinuous change in extended-warranty purchase probability at basic (bumper-to-bumper) warranty expiry: -3 percentage points overall; -6 percentage points (about double) for domestic-brand vehicles versus no significant change for imported brands at that same cutoff.
- Discontinuous change in extended-warranty purchase probability at power-train-warranty expiry (60,000 miles): +10.2 percentage points overall; +15 percentage points for imported-brand vehicles versus no significant change for domestic brands at that same cutoff.
- U.S. consumer spending on extended service contracts, 2012: $14.7 billion (Warranty Week), against total U.S. auto-industry GDP contribution of about $500 billion (3.6% of GDP).
- Average vehicle age in the U.S. vehicle fleet: 9 years in 2006 versus 11.4 years in 2013 (an increase consistent with owners holding vehicles well past the factory warranty period, which the paper cites as a driver of extended-warranty demand).
