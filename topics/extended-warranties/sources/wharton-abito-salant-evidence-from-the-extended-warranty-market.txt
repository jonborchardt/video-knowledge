The Effect of Product Misperception on Economic Outcomes: Evidence from the Extended Warranty Market

Jose Miguel Abito (University of Pennsylvania, Wharton School) and Yuval Salant (Northwestern University, Kellogg School of Management). June 15, 2018. Peer-reviewed economics working paper.

Abstract
Panel and experimental data are used to analyze the economic outcomes in the extended warranty market. We establish that the strong demand and high profits in this market are driven by consumers distorting the failure probability of the insured product, rather than standard risk aversion or sellers' market power. Providing information to consumers about failure probabilities significantly reduces their willingness to pay for warranties, indicating the important role of information, or lack of, in driving consumers' purchase behavior. Such information provision is shown to be more effective in enhancing consumer welfare than additional market competition.

1 Introduction
An extended warranty is an insurance contract that protects against the failure of a durable good such as a consumer electronic. The extended warranty market is highly profitable and has caught the attention of consumer protection and competition authorities in different countries. The UK Competition Commission (2003) estimated the top five UK consumer-electronics retailers earned 116 to 152 million pounds annually on extended-warranty sales in the early 2000s; analysts estimated extended warranties accounted for almost half of BestBuy's operating income in 2003, with profit margins on warranties ranging from 50% to 60% ("The Warranty Windfall," Business Week, December 19, 2004).

Sellers have market power on extended warranties because the warranty is an add-on product usually offered only after the consumer finalizes the decision to buy the insured product, at a point where switching to another seller is costly (an add-on pricing model, following Ellison 2005). On the demand side: about one in four TV buyers in the panel data purchase an extended warranty, paying $90 or more on average to insure against a loss of at most $400 with a 7% probability.

The paper's three takeaways: (1) strong demand and high profits are driven by consumers distorting (overestimating) the insured product's failure probability, not by standard risk aversion or sellers' market power; (2) providing consumers information about objective failure probabilities significantly reduces their willingness to pay for warranties; (3) such information provision improves consumer welfare more than adding market competition would.

2 Data
The data is the INFORMS Society of Marketing Science Durables Dataset 1, transactions from a major US electronics retailer: about 140,000 product-level transactions by almost 20,000 households across 1,176 outlets and the online store, December 1998-November 2004 (prices are essentially identical in-store and online). About 117,000 transactions are non-warranty product purchases, about 15,000 are extended-warranty purchases, about 5,000 are non-warranty returns, and about 1,000 are extended-warranty returns.

2.1 Attachment rates, prices, and approximate profit margins
Across product categories, the extended-warranty attachment rate (share of buyers who purchase one) ranges from about 20% (VCRs, music CDs, video games) to about 40% (car stereos/speakers). Warranties are priced on average at 24% of the insured product's price (standard deviation 11%); there is no significant correlation between a product's price and its warranty's price.

For TVs specifically, attachment rates range from 15% to 35% (higher for more expensive TV subcategories), the average warranty-to-TV-price ratio is about 22% (standard deviation 8%), and TV failure rates (from Consumer Reports' annual survey of TV owners, used because the paper's structural model needs an objective per-category failure rate) are about 6-7%. Using TV price times failure rate as the expected cost of servicing a claim, the paper computes a "back of the envelope" profit margin on TV warranties (margin = (EW price - fail rate x TV price) / EW price) ranging from 62% to 73% across TV subcategories -- close to figures cited in the popular press. The retailer in the dataset likely has a lower realized margin because of revenue sharing with warranty providers and sales commissions.

2.4 Warranty returns
1,239 extended-warranty return transactions: about 67% accompany a return of the insured product itself; 33% are warranty-only returns (the insured product is kept). No buyer or household characteristic strongly predicts warranty-only-return behavior.

5 Results
5.1 The relevance of probability distortions. Consumers substantially overestimate failure probabilities, and more so for already-low failure probabilities. For example, products with an actual average failure probability of 5% are perceived, on average, as having a 13.5% failure probability (see DERIVED COMPARISONS). The degree of overestimation shrinks as the true failure probability rises.

5.2 The irrelevance of risk aversion. The estimated risk-aversion parameter is statistically indistinguishable from zero across every specification tested; probability distortion, not risk aversion, explains buyers' willingness to pay (WTP) for a warranty. Comparing a model with both probability distortion and risk aversion to one with risk aversion only (distortion turned off) shows WTP in the distortion-only model tracks the data's response to changing repair costs and failure probabilities much better than a risk-aversion-only model would.

5.4 Retailer's cost. Using a quadratic fit of the estimated non-parametric cost-to-warranty-price ratio, the paper estimates the retailer's cost of selling and servicing a warranty (including sales commissions and revenue sharing with warranty providers) is about 54% of the warranty's price, implying a retailer profit margin of about 46%.

6 What drives probability distortions? (M-Turk experiments)
A pretest (504 participants) found a self-reported average TV 3-year failure rate of 4.9% (range 4.1%-5.8% across purchase years), closely matching Consumer Reports' own survey of more than 100,000 TV owners (mean/median brand failure rate of 5%; 17 of 23 brands between 2% and 5%, 5 brands 6%-7%, one brand at 10%). A follow-up experiment found uninformed participants (given no information on the actual failure rate) substantially overestimated the failure probability and were willing to pay far more for a warranty than informed participants who were told the objective failure rate; a second experiment found informed participants still showed a mild residual distortion, consistent with both (a) misperception of an unknown probability and (b) some overweighting of a known small probability (prospect theory) jointly driving the effect.

7 The effect of probability distortions on economic outcomes
Counterfactual scenarios comparing today's market (market power + biased/distorting consumers) against three alternatives (market power with unbiased consumers; competitive pricing with unbiased consumers; competitive pricing with biased consumers) at a TV price of $400 and failure rates of 4%-15%:
- At the average 7% failure rate, the warranty-to-TV-price ratio in the status quo is about 20% higher than in a fully competitive market; about three-quarters of that gap is due to market power (pricing above cost) and the rest to probability distortion.
- Attachment rates in the status quo (market power + biased consumers) are 30% or higher, versus about 5% if consumers were unbiased but the retailer kept the same market power -- a five-fold-or-larger increase in sales volume attributable to the distortion, holding market structure fixed.
- More than 90% of retailers' extended-warranty profit is attributable to probability distortion rather than to market power/pricing power.
- Consumer welfare is negative in the status quo market (consumers would be better off, on net, if the extended-warranty market for TVs did not exist); at a 7% failure rate, eliminating the probability distortion (while leaving market power in place) would increase consumer welfare by about $140 million/year, and more than 95% of that gain comes from removing the distortion rather than from removing market power.
- Eliminating market power alone (via a competitive-pricing policy) while leaving the distortion in place would more than double purchase volume but would reduce both consumer and total welfare, because more consumers with a true willingness-to-pay below the fair price would still buy warranties due to the distortion.

8 Conclusion
Probability distortion -- not risk aversion and not, primarily, sellers' market power -- is the dominant driver of both strong demand for extended warranties and retailers' high profit margins on them. Because consumers who receive objective failure-probability information show much less distortion in their stated willingness to pay, the authors conclude the distortion reflects a decision-making mistake driven by lack of information, not a stable preference. Policies that make retailers disclose the warrantied product's objective failure probability next to its price are predicted to raise consumer welfare more than policies that simply increase price competition among warranty sellers (e.g., forcing warranty prices to be posted and compared) would.

DERIVED COMPARISONS
- Extended-warranty attachment (purchase) rate by product category: about 20% (VCRs, music, video games) up to about 40% (car stereos/speakers); TVs specifically 15%-35%.
- Average extended-warranty price as a share of the insured product's price: 24% overall (products in the dataset); about 22% for TVs specifically.
- "Back of the envelope" profit margin on TV extended warranties (price paid minus expected repair cost, as a share of price paid): 62%-73% across TV subcategories.
- Structurally estimated retailer profit margin on extended warranties (accounting for sales commissions and revenue sharing with the warranty provider): about 46% (cost to the retailer is about 54% of the warranty price).
- Perceived vs. actual product failure probability: a product with an actual ~5% three-year failure rate is perceived, on average, as having a ~13.5% failure rate -- roughly 2.7x the true rate; the degree of overestimation (in percentage points of distortion per percentage point of true failure rate) shrinks as the true failure rate rises.
- Self-reported TV failure rate in a 504-person survey: 4.9% average (range 4.1%-5.8% by purchase year), versus Consumer Reports' own survey-based brand failure rates (mean/median 5%, range 2%-10% across 23 brands).
- Share of today's market's extended-warranty profit attributable to probability distortion (consumer overestimation of failure risk) versus to sellers' market power/pricing power: more than 90% distortion, with market power accounting for the remainder.
- Effect of removing probability distortion alone (information provision), holding market power fixed: attachment rate falls from 30%+ to about 5% (five-fold-or-larger drop in volume); consumer welfare rises by about $140 million/year at a 7% failure rate, of which more than 95% is attributable to removing the distortion rather than to any change in market power.
