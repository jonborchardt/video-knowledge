Investigating the Demand for New Vehicle Service Contracts

Evan C. Moore, Auburn University Montgomery. Southwestern Economic Review. Peer-reviewed economics journal article; survey of new-vehicle buyers at the point of purchase.

ABSTRACT
Extended warranties in the automotive industry, known as service contracts, have an average price of almost $1,200 with an average markup of 100 percent. Given these conditions, why do consumers decide to purchase these offerings? Using survey data collected from new vehicle buyers, the paper investigates the roles of consumer experience and risk attitudes, financing and vehicle usage, ability of the salesperson, and vehicle characteristics on the service contract purchase decision, plus socioeconomic and demographic features.

INTRODUCTION
Service contracts typically provide coverage after the base warranty expires, for up to 7 years or 100,000 miles, but do not usually cover routine maintenance or repairs due to excessive use, and the markup on new-vehicle service contracts can reach upwards of 100%. Consumers' Checkbook (2007): "service contracts for automobiles produce big profit... On a contract for which you pay $1,000, the average payout for claims might be less than $250." In 2001, service contracts had an average retail price of $1,178 and provided dealers with an average profit of $548 (J.D. Power, 2001). Used-vehicle service contracts produced nearly $1.9 billion in profit for franchised dealers in 1997, averaging $455 gross profit per contract (Automotive News, 1998).

Despite this markup, roughly 30% of new and used vehicle buyers purchase a service contract (penetration of 34.1% in 2004, per NADA 2005) -- notable given that the large majority of consumer-advocate guides (AAA, Consumer Reports, Ultimate Car Book) and press coverage say service contracts are not worth buying. One analyst (Ursula Moran, Sanford C. Bernstein & Company) described two typical buyer types: people living paycheck to paycheck who want to avoid unplanned expenses, and people buying for convenience.

The study's goal is to identify which factors distinguish service-contract purchasers from non-purchasers, using point-of-sale survey data from new-vehicle buyers (chosen to minimize measurement error, since dealership staff verify vehicle and price details, and to avoid the selection bias of post-sale surveys, where more frustrated customers may be overrepresented among respondents).

KEY FINDINGS
- First-time vehicle buyers are much more likely to purchase a service contract than repeat buyers, across all loan durations tested (marginal effect of the first-time-buyer variable ranging from 0.225 to 0.459 depending on loan length).
- Consumers who rated themselves more knowledgeable about likely repair frequency and cost were significantly less likely to buy a service contract.
- Buyers who had purchased a service contract on a previous vehicle were significantly more likely to purchase one again on the current vehicle.
- Having a driving-age (16+) child in the household increased the likelihood of purchase; frequency of changing one's own oil was negatively associated with purchase (less frequent self-maintenance, lower purchase likelihood).
- None of the individual finance/business-manager identities had a statistically significant effect on whether a given customer bought a service contract (lowest p-value among manager variables was 0.562) -- no evidence any specific salesperson is more effective than another at selling the product.
- Higher household income was significantly associated with a lower likelihood of purchase, consistent with higher-income buyers choosing to self-insure instead.
- Being female and being married were both significantly, positively associated with purchase likelihood (controlling for risk aversion and vehicle knowledge). Combined, likelihood of purchase from least to most likely ran: single male, married female, married male, single female. The marginal effect of being female ranged from 0.093 (no loan) to 0.338 (5-year loan, the most common loan duration).
- Increasing loan duration had a positive, significant effect on the likelihood of a service contract purchase.

MANAGERIAL IMPLICATIONS (as stated by the paper, describing dealer incentives, not an endorsement)
The paper notes dealers could use these findings to target first-time buyers, lower-income buyers, single women, and buyers with driving-age children for service-contract sales, and to steer buyers toward longer loan terms (which both increases interest revenue and increases the odds of a service-contract sale, since a longer loan makes the added monthly payment look smaller).

CONCLUSION
Prior research found marital status, risk attitudes, vehicle price, and business-use status affect service-contract purchase; this study replicates the role of risk aversion and marital status and finds additional significant factors: longer loan duration increases purchase likelihood; greater buyer knowledge of repair probabilities and costs, and more vehicle-buying experience, decrease purchase likelihood; and having bought a service contract previously increases the likelihood of buying one again.

DERIVED COMPARISONS
- Average new-vehicle service-contract markup: about 100%, with an average retail price of about $1,178-$1,200 and an average dealer profit of about $548 (2001, J.D. Power).
- Claims payout on a service contract, per Consumers' Checkbook: on a $1,000 contract, the average claims payout is less than $250 -- i.e., the dealer/provider keeps the large majority of the purchase price even before accounting for overhead.
- Used-vehicle service-contract profit, franchised dealers, 1997: about $1.9 billion total, averaging $455 gross profit per contract (Automotive News, 1998).
- Service-contract purchase (penetration) rate among new- and used-vehicle buyers: roughly 30% generally, 34.1% in 2004 (NADA 2005).
- Likelihood of buying a service contract, by buyer type (holding other factors equal, from least to most likely): single male < married female < married male < single female.
