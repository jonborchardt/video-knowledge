AUTO ADD-ONS ADD UP: HOW DEALER DISCRETION DRIVES EXCESSIVE, ARBITRARY, AND DISCRIMINATORY PRICING

John W. Van Alst, Carolyn Carter, Marina Levy, Yael Shavit. National Consumer Law Center (NCLC), October 2017. Legal-aid / consumer-advocacy research report, based on a national dataset of one large add-on-product provider's sales in the U.S.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Add-on products sold by car dealers, such as service contracts, Guaranteed Asset Protection (GAP) insurance, and window etching, make up a large share of dealers' profits and significantly increase car buyers' costs. Pricing of these products is not transparent: generally only the dealer, the finance company, and the third-party add-on provider know what other consumers are paying. This NCLC analysis of a large dataset found: add-on products are sold at prices far higher than dealer cost, marked up by a far higher percentage than dealers mark up the cars themselves; dealers are inconsistent in add-on pricing, with the same dealer charging some consumers many times more than others for the identical product at the identical dealer cost; this inconsistent pricing produces discrimination, with Hispanics charged higher markups than non-Hispanics; and auto finance companies play an important role in enabling excessive and discriminatory add-on markups. The resulting inflated prices raise loan-to-value (LTV) ratios (since add-ons add to the financed amount without adding real vehicle value), which increases negative equity and is associated with higher loan default rates.

INTRODUCTION
At many dealers the largest source of profit from a car sale is not the sale of the vehicle ("the metal") but financing and the sale of add-ons: service contracts, GAP insurance, and window etching. It is common for dealers to mark up window etching by over 300%, with some dealers marking it up by over 1,000%; many dealers also mark up other add-ons, including service contracts, by 300% or more, often with no set price -- charging whatever the F&I (finance and insurance) manager thinks the consumer can be induced to pay, which creates potential for pricing discrimination by race or national origin.

PRICING OF ADD-ON PRODUCTS
Comparing add-on markups to markups for other retail products
Looking collectively at service contracts, GAP products, and etch products in the dataset, the combined average markup was 170%. For comparison: big-box office-supply or sporting-goods stores mark up roughly 40-50%; general big-box stores (Costco, Target) 10-50%; clothing retailers 50-100% (tempered by frequent sales); jewelry stores 25-125%; furniture stores about 80%; grocery staples only 2-8%; Amazon averages about 15%. Car dealers' own markup on the vehicle itself is far lower: the National Automobile Dealers Association (NADA) found a 3.4% markup on new-car sales and an 8.6% markup on used-car sales in 2015 (historically dealers marked up new cars 5-10% over invoice price). Soft add-ons (the majority of add-ons sold) require no inventory, shipping, storage, insurance, financing, or reconditioning for the dealer, which should allow a profit at a low markup percentage -- yet the data show add-on markups are typically far higher than markups on the cars themselves.

Comparing add-on markups with insurance products
Add-on products such as service contracts, GAP, and etch have insurance-like qualities (etching a VIN costs only a few dollars; most of the price is for the promised benefit). Unlike add-ons, insurance pricing is typically regulated: independent insurance agents do not set their own markup, they receive a fixed commission (typically 10-15% of the first year's premium, equivalent to an 11-18% markup), and most property/casualty insurance products have a loss ratio (claims paid divided by premiums collected) of 50-65%; state minimum loss ratios for credit insurance, among the lowest-regulated insurance products, are as low as 40%.

Applying the same loss-ratio logic to add-ons (treating the dealer's cost -- the amount passed to the third-party provider -- as the maximum possible amount available to pay claims, since the provider cannot pay out more than it collects and stay in business) finds loss ratios "shockingly low": in the dataset reviewed, the average dealer markup for Etch products in 2012 was 325% (average markup of $189 over an average dealer cost of $58), implying an upper-bound loss ratio below 25% even if every dollar paid to the third party was paid out in claims. A 2011 trade advertisement for Safeguard Products International (describing itself as the top-volume F&I add-on provider) stated it had paid $600 million in claims and generated $5 billion in profits for dealer clients; even assuming Safeguard kept nothing beyond paying claims, that implies a loss ratio of only 10.7% ($600 million paid on $5.6 billion in premiums) -- lower than even the 40% regulatory floor set for credit insurance.

In 2012, average dealer markups in the dataset were: Etch 325% (markup of $189 over average dealer cost of $58); GAP 151% (markup of $378 over average dealer cost of $251); service contracts 83% (markup of $859 over average dealer cost of $1,032). There is enormous dealer-to-dealer and state-to-state variation: 13 dealers marked up Etch products by an average of over 1,000% (one dealer sold over 1,000 Etch products at a dealer cost of $16 and consumer charge of $189, a 1,081% markup); 38 dealers marked up GAP by an average of 300% or more; 38 dealers marked up service contracts by an average of more than 300%. By comparison, non-dealer GAP sellers that do not treat add-ons as a special profit center price far lower: North Carolina State Employees Credit Union offered GAP at a flat $275 regardless of vehicle value (up to $100,000), versus the dataset's dealer-sold GAP averaging $629 in 2012 and $655 in 2013.

Pricing trends in service contracts
Reviewing the pricing structure of the top 20 service-contract-selling dealers by 2011 volume: some dealers consistently add a fixed dollar markup (e.g., a constant $1,500 markup regardless of dealer cost) in addition to other varied prices; others charge many customers the identical price point (e.g., $1,999, $2,495, or $2,999) regardless of what the contract actually cost the dealer; some dealers combine both patterns.

DISCRIMINATION IN PRICING
Because F&I managers have discretion to price each add-on individually and are typically compensated more as F&I profit per vehicle rises (e.g., a 10% commission on F&I profit rising to 12% once a per-car profit goal is hit), and because add-on pricing (unlike interest-rate markups, which are not supposed to be based on protected characteristics under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, though studies have found Black and Hispanic buyers charged higher dealer interest-rate markups than white buyers with the same credit risk) is effectively unregulated and unmonitored, the same discretion creates room for discrimination. Because no U.S. auto sales or finance dataset records buyer race (Regulation B bars lenders from asking), NCLC could not directly code race, but found that Hispanic buyers paid higher average dollar and percentage service-contract markups than non-Hispanic buyers in 12 of 14 states studied, even after controlling for dealer cost by comparing only contracts with a similar dealer cost to the dealer (markups were roughly equal in Kentucky and lower for Hispanics in Florida) -- so the disparity could not be explained by Hispanic buyers simply being sold cheaper or costlier contracts.

DERIVED COMPARISONS
- Combined average dealer markup across service contracts, GAP, and etch products (dataset, 2012): 170%, versus dealer markup on the vehicle itself: 3.4% (new cars) to 8.6% (used cars), NADA 2015.
- Average dealer markup by add-on product (2012): Etch 325% ($189 markup on $58 average dealer cost); GAP 151% ($378 markup on $251 average dealer cost); service contracts 83% ($859 markup on $1,032 average dealer cost).
- Implied upper-bound "loss ratio" (claims paid as a share of what consumers paid) for add-ons sold through dealers: below 25% for Etch (2012 data) and about 10.7% for one major national provider's own marketing claim (Safeguard: $600 million in claims paid against $5.6 billion in profits generated), versus a regulated minimum loss ratio of 40% for credit insurance and a typical 50-65% loss ratio for property/casualty insurance generally.
- Independent insurance agent's typical commission on a policy: 10-15% of premium (equivalent to an 11-18% markup), versus a dealer's typical 83% markup on a service contract and 151% markup on GAP.
- Dealer-sold GAP average price: $629 (2012) and $655 (2013), versus a flat $275 charged directly by a credit union (North Carolina State Employees Credit Union) regardless of vehicle value up to $100,000.
- Extreme-case dealer markups found in the dataset: 13 dealers averaged over 1,000% markup on Etch (one dealer: $16 cost, $189 charge, 1,081% markup); 38 dealers averaged 300%+ markup on GAP; 38 dealers averaged 300%+ markup on service contracts.
- Average service-contract markup, Hispanic versus non-Hispanic buyers, holding dealer cost roughly constant ($910-$1,010 cost range): higher dollar and percentage markup for Hispanic buyers in 12 of 14 states studied.
