CONSUMER FINANCIAL PROTECTION BUREAU | OCTOBER 2024
Supervisory Highlights: Special Edition Auto Finance, Issue 35 (Fall 2024)

Government supervisory report summarizing findings from CFPB examinations of auto lenders and servicers.

1. Introduction
As of the second quarter of 2024, Americans owe $1.616 trillion in auto loan debt -- more household debt than any category except home mortgages. This issue highlights a trend of significant violations related to the handling of add-on products (also called optional or ancillary products). Consumers generally finance add-on products at loan origination, with the product premium paid upfront and then included in the amount financed; auto-finance companies profit from the original cost, finance charges on that cost over the life of the loan, and, in some cases, from failing to ensure refunds when consumers can no longer use the product. Examiners identified unfair, deceptive, and abusive acts or practices throughout the lifecycle of add-on products: from add-on products included without consumer consent, to servicers failing to allow cancellation during the initial cancellation period, failing to provide the product's benefit, or failing to ensure refunds when the loan terminates early.

2.4 Add-on products
Add-on products fall into two categories. Credit products assist with the remaining loan balance under certain circumstances and cease to provide any benefit once the loan terminates -- "guaranteed asset protection" (GAP) products are a credit product, helping pay off the loan if the car is totaled or stolen. Vehicle products relate to the vehicle itself and may continue to provide benefit after the loan terminates -- a vehicle service contract (paying for the cost of certain repairs) is a vehicle product. Dealers and finance companies typically charge the full add-on cost as a lump sum at origination, folded into the financed amount, with the consumer paying it off (plus interest) over the loan term; these products generally allow early cancellation for a pro-rata refund of the unused ("unearned") portion.

When a loan terminates early (through payoff, default, or total loss), credit products like GAP stop providing any possible benefit, because their coverage is tied to the loan itself; absent a refund, the consumer ends up having paid for a product they can no longer use. Vehicle-product coverage such as a service contract terminates upon default, when the borrower no longer has the vehicle.

2.4.1 Collecting and retaining amounts for add-on products consumers did not agree to purchase
Examiners found subprime auto-finance companies committed abusive acts or practices by collecting and retaining payments for add-on products (an extended service contract or other vehicle protection product, and an optional GAP waiver) that consumers did not agree to purchase. The companies' contracted service providers, who prepared refinance documents, were required by contract to include a minimum number of "tangible benefits" (i.e., add-on products) in refinanced loans. Recorded calls showed the service providers walking borrowers through electronically signing the refinance agreement without disclosing or explaining the add-on products that had been included and financed. The finance companies had not monitored the service providers' compliance. This took unreasonable advantage of consumers' inability to protect their interests, since consumers who did not know about or consent to the charges could not object to them. Remedy: entities were directed to stop collecting for unauthorized add-ons, hire external consultants to find and remediate all affected consumers, rewrite service-provider contracts, and record and audit all such sales calls going forward.

2.4.2 Financing of void add-on products on salvage vehicles
GAP products are void (worthless to the consumer) if the vehicle has a salvage title (a recorded loss event such as a major accident). Examiners found servicers suspended their own title-check procedures for certain preferred originating dealers, then financed GAP products on those loans anyway -- meaning the loans included GAP coverage that delivered no benefit but still increased the amount financed and the monthly payment, while the servicers saved the cost of the title check. This took unreasonable advantage of consumers' lack of understanding of the GAP product's material conditions. Remedy: entities were directed to implement title-history checks before financing add-on products to confirm eligibility.

2.4.3 Failure to identify payee of add-on products
Examiners found violations of Regulation Z (Section 1026.18(c)(1)(iii)), which requires the financing disclosure to separately itemize and identify the payee of amounts paid to other parties, including for add-on products. Entities had not identified (or had bought contracts that did not identify) the add-on-product payee in the itemization of amount financed.

2.4.4 Onerous requirements to cancel add-on products
Examiners found servicers abusively required consumers to make two separate in-person dealership visits to cancel a cancellable, pro-rata-refundable add-on product -- one visit to meet with the dealership's general manager to initiate cancellation, a second to pick up the refund check -- a requirement not disclosed in the contract. This unreasonably exploited consumers' inability to protect their interests, since the excessive time and effort needed functioned to deter consumers from exercising a cancellation right the contract actually granted them.

2.4.5 Failure to honor contractual cancellation rights
Examiners found servicers denied consumers' cancellation requests even though the add-on contract explicitly allowed cancellation with a pro-rata refund within the first year -- an abusive practice, since servicers controlled the process and simply refused to honor contractual cancellation rights, leaving the consumer with no alternative way to get the refund they were contractually owed.

2.4.6 Failure to ensure refunds of unearned premiums
Examiners found servicers committed unfair practices by failing, across every state (including states with no state-law mandate to do so), to ensure refunds of the unearned (unused) portion of add-on-product premiums when a loan terminated early via payoff, repossession, or total loss. Because the add-on product (GAP, or a service contract once the vehicle was repossessed or totaled) had no further value to the consumer after termination, consumers ended up paying for coverage they could no longer use, and unrefunded amounts inflated payoff and deficiency balances. Consumers could not reasonably protect themselves because servicers controlled the refund process and most consumers did not understand they had become eligible for a refund.

2.4.7 Inaccurate add-on product refund amounts
Examiners found servicers miscalculated add-on refund amounts after early termination -- for example, using the date of a deficiency notice instead of the actual repossession date as the pro-rata cutoff, or relying on a third party's calculation that did not match the contract's own refund formula -- causing consumers to receive less than they were owed, with no ability on the consumer's part to check or control the calculation.

2.4.8 Delays in applying add-on product refunds
Examiners found servicers took unfairly long to apply refunds of unearned add-on premiums to borrower accounts. In one matter, refunds were applied an average of 84 days after the post-repossession sale of the vehicle, with at least one refund delayed 423 days; in another matter, delays ranged from 150 to 664 days. Even where a refund is eventually granted in full, the delay itself denies the consumer use of money they are owed.

2.4.9 Continuing to collect payments when consumers are covered by a GAP product, and miscalculating refunds
GAP waiver agreements generally waive the amount still owed on the loan as of a total-loss date, less unpaid charges and less the vehicle's actual cash value at the time of loss, so the consumer does not keep owing on a totaled car. Examiners found servicers continued collecting monthly loan payments for months after a total-loss event despite knowing the consumer's GAP waiver would cover the outstanding balance, then -- when the GAP waiver claim was finally processed -- miscalculated the refund owed back to the consumer, resulting in underpayment. This caused two separate harms: consumers lost use of their money for the months they kept paying on a totaled vehicle (sometimes while also paying for a replacement vehicle), and then received an inaccurate, insufficient refund once the claim was finally resolved. Consumers could not avoid this because they had no control over the servicer's process, and stopping payment on their own would have triggered negative credit reporting.

DERIVED COMPARISONS
- Total outstanding U.S. auto loan debt, Q2 2024: $1.616 trillion -- the largest category of household debt after home mortgages.
- Observed delay between vehicle disposition and an owed add-on-product refund being applied, across two CFPB-examined servicers: average 84 days (one case up to 423 days) in one matter; a range of 150 to 664 days in another matter.
- Both GAP (a credit add-on, tied to the loan) and vehicle service contracts (a vehicle add-on) stop providing any benefit once a loan terminates early (through payoff, default, or total loss) -- GAP always, a service contract once the vehicle itself is repossessed or totaled -- meaning the portion of the premium covering the remaining term becomes a straightforward refund due back to the consumer if not consumed first by continued, often-unnoticed monthly billing.
