## A mortgage bill drawn on a secret account [heading:hook fp=2f488ea7b7]

In Virginia, a borrower tried to clear her home loan by sending Washington Mutual a $244,663.79 bill of exchange, drawn on what she called a contract trust account administered through the IRS. [s01 ok fp=28a8e243d2] Her complaint reasoned that because the bank had received the bill, "this loan is paid off". [s02 ok fp=2c6cee5d2b]

Figure decision-money-debt-and-taxes/sc01: The bill of exchange the borrower sent her lender, as her complaint described it. [decision-money-debt-and-taxes/sc01 fp=b0ffdd0222]

This article follows the story her bill relied on, step by step, from the name on a birth certificate to the account it is meant to fund, and then the older idea underneath it, that leaving gold made debts impossible to pay. [s03 fp=3ca9658e38] Each step is set against what the Treasury, the IRS and the courts have said about it. [s04 fp=50cae93cd8]

## Is there really a secret Treasury account in my name that can pay off my debts? [heading:question-1 fp=adc1282170]

Believers say the account belongs to a second, legal version of you; in the words of jonhoyle.com, "The all capitals spelling of the name creates a corporate fiction". [s05 ok fp=f0dd9b27ed] The birth certificate is said to give that fiction its value, with the site Understand Contract Law claiming "These birth certificate bonds have a value and are being traded". [s06 ok fp=2a782f289d] The proceeds are supposed to be reachable: jonhoyle.com describes "an alphanumeric account number in your Strawman name that is your private account that can be drawn from", and a YouTube tutorial calls a stamped Social Security number on a bill "what's going to give you access into your treasury account". [s07 ok fp=128f6307fb]

On the account, the Treasury is direct: such accounts "are fictitious and do not exist in the Treasury system". [s08 ok fp=caf70ba435] The IRS, citing the Sixth Circuit in United States v. Anderson, adds that the Treasury "does not maintain depository accounts against which an individual can draw a check, draft, or any other financial instrument", adding that "The notion of secret accounts assigned to each citizen is pure fantasy". [s09 ok fp=ae59025eb1] The Treasury also notes that some websites sell videos, webinars and coaching on these methods, and that "No one has profited from the Treasury Department by using these tactics". [s10 ok fp=e76774577d]

Nor is the birth certificate an asset: according to the Treasury, "There is no monetary value to a birth certificate", and it cannot be used to make purchases. [s11 ok fp=a052d8388b] When a claimant in Gravatt v. United States argued that his birth certificate was a contract with the government, the Court of Federal Claims called the claim frivolous, holding that such documents do not "recognize or impose contractual rights, obligations, or duties". [s12 ok fp=5c7de07814] The same court doubted the Treasury trust fund he said he was owed from, writing that it "strains all logic and reason to believe that the trust fund alleged by plaintiff actually exists". [s13 ok fp=d8c795fd9f]

The split person at the start of the chain fares no better: a legal history of these movements in Canada says the strawman "has no basis in law in either the US or Canada", and the Supreme Court of Queensland found it "does not find any reflection in any provision of our law". [s14 ok fp=9553678b75]

**unresolved (sovereign-citizens/government-creates-separate-corporate-entity-strawman):** The case against the strawman here rests on legal commentary and individual rulings from the United States, Canada and Australia, not on a survey of every court that has heard it. [s15 ok fp=9a54cde173] The strongest of it is Canadian: a study of applications to the Supreme Court of Canada concludes that the strawman is incompatible with Canadian law and that "Any human being is a legal person". [s16 ok fp=796b2a8d86] Every source here rejects the idea, but the record grades these sources short of the level it treats as settled, so the claim is listed as studied rather than closed. [s17 fp=0032cfd166]

That leaves the instrument itself, and the Treasury's verdict is that "All these Bills of Exchange drawn on the U.S. Treasury are worthless", adding that drawing such drafts on it "is fraudulent and a violation of federal law". [s18 ok fp=062b52c1b1] Courts outside the US have reached the same view: in Bosse v. Farm Credit Canada, a family claimed to have settled debts on wood lots with setoff bonds drawn on secret US Treasury accounts, and the New Brunswick Court of Appeal found the strategy frivolous and illogical, adding that the family knew it. [s19 ok fp=5142fc35cc] The Virginia borrower fared the same way: her bank called the bill an unacceptable form of payment and foreclosed, and when she sued, the court held that she "did not tender payment, but rather a worthless piece of paper", though it accepted that her belief in the process appeared genuine. [s20 ok fp=6ec5f51ca5]

Passing these bills can also lead to a criminal case: Barton Buhtz, the promoter whose redemption advice the borrower had relied on, was convicted in Oregon with several co-defendants of fraud charges over passing bills of exchange. [s21 ok fp=f31a9f0869] And in United States v. Heath, the Sixth Circuit upheld the conviction of a man who sent the IRS a so-called "Registered Bill of Exchange", made to look like a certified check with no actual account behind it. [s22 ok fp=fac60efe8c]

**qualification (sovereign-citizens/sovereign-citizen-debt-discharge-instruments-worthless):** What these sources record are convictions of a promoter and of people who made or passed the instruments; none of them counts how often an ordinary person who sends a bill to a lender is charged. [s23 ok fp=7798699f2d]

So the answer is no: the Treasury says the account is nonexistent, and every court in this evidence that weighed a bill drawn on it found it worthless or frivolous. [s27 ok fp=375f813a26]

## But since the US left gold in 1933, isn't money just credit, so a debt can only be discharged, never paid? [heading:question-2 fp=5314c10ee2]

Underneath the account sits an older claim about money, which Educated in Law sums up as "As a result of HJR 192, the people can no longer pay their debts." [s28 ok fp=de9a096010] A YouTube video, Take Back Your Strawman Birth Certificate Bonds, says the resolution "eliminated our ability to pay our debts allowing only for their discharge". [s29 ok fp=2cd7e93a19]

House Joint Resolution 192 is a real law of 1933, and the court in the Virginia mortgage case read it: its heading is "To assure uniform value to the coins and currencies of the United States". [s30 ok fp=95ca2d8850] In the court's summary, the resolution voids contract terms that demand payment in gold and makes US currency "legal tender for all debts", the reverse of what the claim needs. [s31 ok fp=ccfb7506db] The Supreme Court case the borrower also cited, Guaranty Trust Co. v. Henwood, said the resolution ties a debt to the "actual number of dollars promised, dollar for dollar". [s32 ok fp=7ca0f97bac] The judge concluded that both authorities concern "nothing more than the U.S. monetary shift away from the gold standard" and say nothing of secret trust accounts or bills of exchange. [s33 ok fp=fd3c9c173f]

Paper money settles debts as well: the Eighth Circuit, in United States v. Rifen, held that "Congress has declared federal reserve notes legal tender". [s34 ok fp=bd730671b9] The Ninth Circuit, in United States v. Condo, called "frivolous" the argument that Federal Reserve Notes "are not valid currency, cannot be taxed, and are merely" debts. [s35 ok fp=59d69377c8]

**unresolved (sovereign-citizens/since-1933-gold-suspension-money-credit):** The reading of the resolution itself in this evidence comes from the court in Bryant, a suit over a single mortgage; the appeals rulings here are about Federal Reserve Notes as legal tender in tax cases, not about the resolution. [s36 ok fp=5d97d1c984] Both point the same way, but the record grades them short of the level it treats as settled, so the claim stays listed as studied rather than closed. [s37 fp=87140489e8]

On what is here, the answer is no: the move off gold changed what stands behind a dollar, while the law kept dollars as payment for any debt. [s38 ok fp=b698f53506]

## Before you stamp a bill [heading:close fp=b4e59eb3cc]

The Treasury's own account of the scheme begins with the move off gold, and the judge who read that law found it gives the redemption claim "absolutely no support". [s39 ok fp=587bc26643] A payment method that only works if the Treasury is secretly holding your money is, to a lender, no payment at all. [s40 fp=58e79336d4] Whoever sells the method should be able to name a court that accepted it; the rulings above did not. [s41 fp=288e2b0d74]
