## Hook

A homeowner sent the bank holding her mortgage a bill of exchange for $244,663.79, drawn, she said, on a trust account run through the IRS. [s01 ok] She argued that once the bank received it, "this loan is paid off". [s02 ok] Is there really a secret Treasury account in your name that can pay off your debts? The story behind her bill points at real things, your birth certificate, the Treasury, the commercial code, so every step sounds like something you could check.

## Is there a secret Treasury account in your name?

It starts with your name in capital letters. The site jonhoyle.com says "The all capitals spelling of the name creates a corporate fiction", which believers call the strawman. [s05 ok] Next comes your birth certificate, which the site Understand Contract Law treats as an investment: "These birth certificate bonds have a value and are being traded". [s06 ok] And the value is supposed to sit in an account you can reach: jonhoyle.com says "there is an alphanumeric account number in your Strawman name that is your private account that can be drawn from". [s07 ok] The way in is a stamp: in one YouTube how-to video, a red stamp with your Social Security number on a bill is "what's going to give you access into your treasury account". [s08 ok] The kit is for sale: the Treasury notes that some websites sell videos, webinars and coaching on how to do this, and that "No one has profited from the Treasury Department by using these tactics". [s09 ok] So start with the account itself. Does it exist? The Treasury says these accounts "are fictitious and do not exist in the Treasury system". [s11 ok] A federal appeals court, the Sixth Circuit, said the same: the Treasury "does not maintain depository accounts against which an individual can draw a check, draft, or any other financial instrument". [s12 ok] The IRS puts it more bluntly: "The notion of secret accounts assigned to each citizen is pure fantasy". [s13 ok] What about the birth certificate that is meant to fill it? The Treasury's answer: "There is no monetary value to a birth certificate", and a birth certificate cannot be used to buy anything. [s15 ok] In Gravatt v. United States, a man told the Court of Federal Claims that his birth certificate was a contract with the United States, and the court called that frivolous: birth certificates, it said, do not "recognize or impose contractual rights, obligations, or duties". [s16 ok] He also claimed to be owed money from a trust fund held in the Treasury, and the court wrote that it "strains all logic and reason to believe that the trust fund alleged by plaintiff actually exists". [s17 ok] And the strawman itself? A history of these movements in Canada says it "has no basis in law in either the US or Canada", and in Australia the Supreme Court of Queensland found that the idea "does not find any reflection in any provision of our law". [s18 ok] That leaves the stamped bill. The Treasury says "All these Bills of Exchange drawn on the U.S. Treasury are worthless", and that drawing such drafts on it "is fraudulent and a violation of federal law". [s20 ok] In Canada, a family whose wood lots were financed by Farm Credit Canada said they had paid with bonds drawn on secret accounts run by the US Treasury; the New Brunswick Court of Appeal found the strategy frivolous and illogical, and said the family knew this. [s21 ok] As for the homeowner, the bank told her the bill was an unacceptable form of payment, and went on to foreclose on her home. [s22 ok] When she sued, the federal judge found that she "did not tender payment, but rather a worthless piece of paper", while accepting that her belief in the process seemed genuine. [s23 ok] The man whose redemption advice she had followed, Barton Buhtz, had just been convicted in Oregon, with several co-defendants, of fraud charges for passing bills of exchange. [s24 ok] Sending one can be a crime by itself: in United States v. Heath, the Sixth Circuit upheld a conviction for presenting a fictitious financial instrument, a "Registered Bill of Exchange" sent to the IRS with no actual account behind it. [s25 ok] How often an ordinary user ends up charged, none of these sources counts; what they record are the convictions of a promoter and of people who passed the bills. [s26 ok] So the answer to the first question is no: the Treasury says the account does not exist, and the courts in this evidence that looked at these bills treated them as worthless. [s27 ok] With the account gone, the promoters fall back on the idea underneath it: that ever since the country left gold, no debt can truly be paid at all.

## Since 1933, is money just credit that can never pay a debt?

The site Educated in Law states it in one line: "As a result of HJR 192, the people can no longer pay their debts." [s29 ok] A video called Take Back Your Strawman Birth Certificate Bonds says the change "eliminated our ability to pay our debts allowing only for their discharge". [s30 ok] So what does that resolution actually say? The judge in that mortgage case read it, and it is a real law from 1933, headed "To assure uniform value to the coins and currencies of the United States". [s32 ok] It made contract clauses demanding payment in gold against public policy, and it declared US currency "legal tender for all debts". [s33 ok] A 1939 ruling the homeowner also cited, Guaranty Trust Co. v. Henwood, explained what Congress wanted: a debt in dollars is measured by the "actual number of dollars promised, dollar for dollar". [s34 ok] Nothing in it mentions secret trust accounts or bills of exchange, the judge wrote; it addresses "nothing more than the U.S. monetary shift away from the gold standard". [s35 ok] The notes in your wallet count too: as one federal appeals court put it, "Congress has declared federal reserve notes legal tender". [s36 ok] In a tax case, the Ninth Circuit rejected as "frivolous" the argument that Federal Reserve Notes "are not valid currency, cannot be taxed, and are merely" debts. [s37 ok] So the answer to the second question is no: leaving gold changed what backs a dollar, not whether a dollar pays a debt. [s38 ok]

## Close

Both answers meet in the same place: the Treasury says the scheme's story starts with 1933, and the 1933 law says the opposite of what that story needs. [s39 ok] So if a way to pay needs you to believe the Treasury is hiding your money, expect your lender to treat it as no payment at all. And before you stamp anything, ask whoever is selling the method for one court that accepted it.
