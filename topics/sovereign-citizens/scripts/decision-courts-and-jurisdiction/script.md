## Hook

A man on trial in Ohio told the court he reserved his rights "per UCC 1-308 without prejudice", and then added, "I give the Court no consent". [s01 ok] He believed those words, said in the right place, would leave the judge with no power over him. Is there anything you can say, sign or point to that takes you out of a court's jurisdiction? The people who sell this idea offer a whole kit: a code to write under your signature, a fringe on the courtroom flag, an old act of Congress, and the theory that every law is just a contract. Each one leans on something real, so we can look at what it rests on, and then read what courts did when someone tried it.

## Can you sign, say or point your way out of a court's power?

The kit starts with consent. A channel called Out of Babylon puts it plainly: "there are various jurisdictions that do not have jurisdiction over you unless you grant them that jurisdiction". [s06 ok] And the way to withhold that consent, according to The Grown Academy, is to "write UCC 1-308 next to your signature All Rights Reserved". [s07 ok] So what does that code section actually say? It is part of the Uniform Commercial Code, and it says a party that "performs, promises performance, or assents to performance" while explicitly reserving its rights does not lose them; words like "without prejudice" or "under protest" are enough. [s09 ok] The official comment gives its purpose: the continuation of performance "along the lines contemplated by the contract despite a pending dispute". [s10 ok] Picture a shop that keeps accepting a supplier's deliveries while it argues about the bill, and doesn't want accepting them to count as dropping the argument. That is the whole job of the section: it is about performing a commercial contract, and it says nothing about courts, police or criminal charges. [s12 ok] And the man in Ohio? He was found guilty of all four charges, and the appeals court noted that "Any arguments related to the UCC are unavailing". [s13 ok] Withholding consent out loud fares no better than writing it down. In United States v. Benabe, a federal appeals court wrote that whatever status a person claims, "that person is not beyond the jurisdiction of the courts". [s15 ok] It added that such theories "should be rejected summarily, however they are presented". [s16 ok] Courts in New Zealand heard the same argument from people who said a court's authority depended on their consent, and all three lost; the researchers who reviewed those cases put the problem simply: "no one needs to explicitly and affirmatively consent to the authority of a jurisdiction to be subject to it". [s17 ok] Then there is the flag. A letter in the Mountain Democrat newspaper states the belief in one line: "The fringe denotes Admiralty law's jurisdiction". [s19 ok] A federal court, noting that other courts had already called the argument frivolous, decided to examine the law of the flag "in the interest of killing this argument for good". [s20 ok] It found that the Army's own Adjutant General had written that a fringe "is not considered to be a part of the flag, and it is without heraldic significance". [s21 ok] Then it said what decides the question: "Jurisdiction is a matter of law, statute, and constitution, not a child's game wherein one's power is magnified or diminished by the display of some magic talisman". [s22 ok] The next trick goes bigger: it says the whole country was quietly turned into a company. The channel SYDLIS says "the act of 1871 that is exactly what changed america from a constitutional republic to a corporation". [s24 ok] The act of 1871 is real, and the Supreme Court later described what it did: it made the District of Columbia "a body corporate for municipal purposes", a local government that could make contracts, sue and be sued, and look after its streets. [s25 ok] The Court called the District "a mere municipal corporation", and said the sovereign power over it was lodged "in the government of the United States". [s26 ok] And the government that the 1871 act set up did not even last: an act of 1874 abolished it. [s27 ok] The last item in the kit is the broadest. Americans In Action says that "these codes and statutes are only the bylaws of the corporation", rules that, it says, do not bind state nationals. [s29 ok] In Bey v. Indiana, a man argued that Indiana could not tax him without a contract between them, and the federal appeals court answered: "We have repeatedly rejected such claims". [s30 ok] In New Zealand, a self-described freeman said he had never consented to the Land Transport Act and so was not bound by it; the court struck out his claim as an abuse of process. [s31 ok] The reason is simple: "Everyone who operates a vehicle on public roads must comply with legislation regulating the operation of vehicles". [s32 ok] So the answer to the first question is no: courts in the United States, Canada and New Zealand have rejected the idea that their power depends on your consent. [s33 ok] Researchers who followed these arguments to the Supreme Court of Canada found that it has never granted one of them leave to appeal, and their verdict is blunt: "These ideas are pseudolaw, and devoid of merit". [s34 ok] A review of cases in Australia and New Zealand reached the same place: "No courts accept these arguments, and no courts absolve the claimant (or defendant) from their legal obligations and responsibilities". [s35 ok] For the United States, the evidence here has no count like that; what it has is a run of rulings, and the Ohio appeals court summed them up by saying the claims "continue to make their way to court and fail". [s36 ok] If nothing you say, sign or point to removes a court's power, the promoters' next offer is to change who you are.

## Can declaring a new status put you outside the law?

A channel called Sovereign National describes ordinary citizenship as the thing "which forces you into their statutes and codes and bylaws and we are not part of that". [s38 ok] Some sellers promise that the paperwork works at the roadside too: SPC University tells viewers that "when the officer runs your id he or she will see your status and has to let you go". [s39 ok] So what is a national, in the law itself? Federal statute defines a national of the United States as "a citizen of the United States", or a person who is not a citizen but owes the country permanent allegiance. [s41 ok] So every citizen is already a national, and the non-citizen kind comes from the circumstances of birth, such as being born in an outlying possession of the United States, not from a form you file. [s42 ok] Federal appeals courts, in tax cases, have also rejected the related claim that being a citizen of a state rather than of the United States puts a person beyond the federal courts; one called it "barely worth a footnote". [s43 ok] The Moorish version leans on old treaties instead, and the Institute for Strategic Dialogue explains how: "Moorish sovereigns cite that treaty to claim that Moors in the US have sovereign status, meaning they would be exempt from normal laws". [s44 ok] In Bey v. Indiana, the man who argued that being a Moor made him a sovereign citizen wanted an $11.5 billion refund from the State of Indiana. [s45 ok] The court said the treaty argument is made "without any basis in fact", and that Bey "is a U.S. citizen and therefore unlike foreign diplomats has no immunity from U.S. law". [s46 ok] The oldest-sounding version reaches back to the common law, and the National Liberty Alliance teaches that "the un-codified common law is the superior law of the people and the codified civil law is the special or inferior law of the government". [s47 ok] As for Magna Carta, it had been repealed by 1969 except for three provisions. [s48 ok] As early as 1905, the High Court of Australia said the claim that a law is invalid because it does not conform to Magna Carta "is not one for serious refutation". [s49 ok] And the Supreme Court of Canada has refused to hear appeals built on Magna Carta and historical English common law, which the researchers read as a quiet rejection of both. [s50 ok] On the papers that are meant to stop police, the evidence is thin: one FBI bulletin reports that three people in Kansas City were convicted for selling "sovereign status" ID cards, at $450 to $2,000 each, that promised immunity from stops and arrests, and it records the sellers' convictions, not what happened to buyers at a stop. [s51 ok] So the answer to the second question is no as well: no court in this evidence has accepted a declared status, whether state national, Moor or free man under the common law. [s52 ok] The parts settled less directly are narrow: the federal rulings on state citizenship come from tax cases, so a state court's reach is not what they tested, and the stop question rests on the bulletin above. [s53 ok]

## Close

Both answers come down to the same thing: a court gets its power from the law, not from what you write under your name, what hangs on its wall, or what you declare yourself to be. [s54 ok] So if someone offers you a form, a code to write by your signature, or a card for the glovebox, ask them for one ruling where it worked. The rulings where it was tried are public, and they are the place to look before you sign anything.
