8 U.S. Code § 1408 - Nationals but not citizens of the United States at birth
Unless otherwise provided in section 1401 of this title, the following shall be nationals, but not citizens, of the United States at birth:
(1)A person born in an outlying possession of the United States on or after the date of formal acquisition of such possession;
(2)A person born outside the United States and its outlying possessions of parents both of whom are nationals, but not citizens, of the United States, and have had a residence in the United States, or one of its outlying possessions prior to the birth of such person;
(3)A person of unknown parentage found in an outlying possession of the United States while under the age of five years, until shown, prior to his attaining the age of twenty-one years, not to have been born in such outlying possession; and
(4)A person born outside the United States and its outlying possessions of parents one of whom is an alien, and the other a national, but not a citizen, of the United States who, prior to the birth of such person, was physically present in the United States or its outlying possessions for a period or periods totaling not less than seven years in any continuous period of ten years—
(A)during which the national parent was not outside the United States or its outlying possessions for a continuous period of more than one year, and
(B)at least five years of which were after attaining the age of fourteen years.
The proviso of section 1401(g) of this title shall apply to the national parent under this paragraph in the same manner as it applies to the citizen parent under that section.

Chapter 2 - Born in the United States and Becoming a U.S. Naturalized Citizen

Given the injunction currently in place in Casa Inc. v. Trump, No. 8:25-cv-00201 (D. Md. Sep. 2, 2026), USCIS will not take actions to implement this guidance specifically to any member of the certified class, or otherwise conflict with the preliminary injunction, unless and until the government obtains relief from the injunction. If the government obtains timely relief from the injunction, USCIS will implement this guidance accordingly.

A. Born in the United States

A person may derive or acquire U.S. citizenship at birth. Persons who are born in the United States[1] and subject to the jurisdiction of the United States are citizens at birth. The Fourteenth Amendment grants United States citizenship at birth to “all persons born… in the United States, and subject to the jurisdiction thereof.” The Supreme Court has interpreted the words “subject to the jurisdiction thereof” to create certain exceptions to birthright citizenship, notably “children of members of the Indian tribes…children born of alien enemies in hostile occupation, and children of diplomatic representatives of a foreign state” with rights of extraterritoriality.[2]

The United States means the continental United States, Alaska, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, Guam, the Virgin Islands of the United States, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands.[3] For certain regions there are different dates for the start of U.S. citizenship from birth including:[4]

Puerto Rico on or after April 11, 1899;[5]
Canal Zone or the Republic of Panama on or after February 26, 1904;[6]
Virgin Islands on or after January 17, 1917;[7]
Guam born after April 11, 1899;[8] or
Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands (CNMI) on or after November 4, 1986.[9]

U.S. citizens may obtain a U.S. passport from the Department of State (DOS).[10] A passport determination by the DOS is separate from a USCIS adjudication, but a valid, unexpired U.S. passport is conclusive proof of U.S. citizenship.[11]

B. U.S. Nationals

Persons born in American Samoa and Swains Island (the “outlying possessions” of the United States), are generally considered U.S. nationals at birth under INA 308 and are not citizens of the United States. Persons born in the outlying possessions must apply for naturalization to become a U.S. citizen.[12]

The following people are also considered U.S. nationals, but not citizens, of the United States:

A person born outside the United States and its outlying possessions to two U.S. national parents who have had a residence in the United States or one of its outlying possessions prior to the child’s birth.[13] 
A person under the age of 5 found in an outlying possession of the United States, unless shown not to have been born in the outlying possession before attaining the age of 21[14] 
A person born outside the United States and its outlying possessions to an alien and a U.S. national, where prior to the birth, the U.S. national parent was:
Physically present in the United States or its outlying possessions for a period totaling at least 7 years within a 10-year continuum;  
At least 5 of these years occurred after the U.S. national parent attained the age of 14; and
During this 10-year continuum the U.S. national parent was not outside the United States or its outlying possessions for a continuous period of more than 1 year.[15] 

U.S. nationals may apply for naturalization under the special provisions located at INA 325. Further, the child of a U.S. national and a foreign government employee as described below, who is born in the United States, is not a U.S. citizen at birth unless at least one parent is a U.S. citizen. Such child would need to register as a permanent resident and subsequently apply for naturalization if otherwise eligible. 

C. Subject to the Jurisdiction of the United States

Generally, under the Fourteenth Amendment, a child born in the United States [16] is subject to the jurisdiction of the United States and is a citizen at birth (“birthright citizenship”), with limited exceptions.

The Supreme Court has interpreted the phrase “subject to the jurisdiction thereof” to exclude from birthright citizenship “children of members of the Indian tribes…children born of alien enemies in hostile occupation, and children of diplomatic representatives of a foreign state.”[17] 

An officer determines whether a person born in the United States to a foreign diplomatic officer or a foreign government employee (as applicable) was subject to the jurisdiction of the United States, and therefore whether he or she acquired citizenship at birth, by referring to the applicable regulatory provisions and conditions that existed at the time of the person’s birth.[18] 

For persons born in the United States before September 4, 2026, the officer evaluates whether either of the person’s parents was a “foreign diplomatic officer” and whether the person had a U.S. citizen parent.[19] For persons born in the United States on or after September 4, 2026, the officer evaluates whether either of the person’s parents was a “foreign government employee” and whether the person had a U.S. citizen parent.[20] 

If an officer determines that either parent was a “foreign diplomatic officer” or a “foreign government employee” (as relevant) at the time of the person’s birth in the United States, and neither of the person’s parents was a U.S. citizen,[21] then the person was not subject to the jurisdiction of the United States and he or she did not acquire U.S. citizenship at birth under the Fourteenth Amendment. 

Children who are not considered U.S. citizens at the time of birth in the United States must first register as lawful permanent residents[22] and then apply for naturalization if otherwise qualified.

1. Persons Born in the United States Before September 4, 2026

A person born in the United States before September 4, 2026, was not subject to the jurisdiction of the United States, and therefore was not a U.S. citizen at birth, if the person’s parent was a “foreign diplomatic officer” at the time of the person’s birth.[23] USCIS defined a foreign diplomatic officer as a specific subset of the individuals in the United States who enjoyed certain diplomatic immunities, including:

A person listed in the State Department Diplomatic List (“Blue List”);
Ambassadors, ministers, chargés d’affaires, counselors, secretaries, and attachés of embassies and legations;
Members of the Delegation of the Commission of the European Communities;
Individuals with comparable diplomatic status and immunities who are accredited to the United Nations or to the Organization of American States; and
Other individuals who are also accorded comparable diplomatic status.[24]

A foreign diplomatic officer did not include the following:

Employees of foreign diplomatic missions whose names appeared in the State Department list entitled “Employees of Diplomatic Missions Not Printed in the Diplomatic List” (White List);
Employees of foreign diplomatic missions accredited to the United Nations or the Organization of American States;
Foreign diplomats accredited to other foreign states; and
Foreign government employees with limited or no diplomatic immunity, such as consular officials named on the State Department list entitled “Foreign Consular Officers in the United States” and their staff.[25]
2. Persons Born in the United States On or After September 4, 2026 

A person born in the United States on or after September 4, 2026, is not subject to the jurisdiction of the United States for purposes of birthright citizenship, and therefore not a U.S. citizen at birth when neither parent was a U.S. citizen, if a parent was a “foreign government employee” at the time of the person’s birth.[26] A foreign government employee means:

A foreign diplomatic officer accredited to the United States;[27] 
A person employed by a foreign embassy or consulate who is a national of the foreign country;[28] 
A person employed by a foreign government in an official capacity;[29] and
A person employed by an international organization that possesses international-organization immunity.[30] 

A foreign government employee generally does not include the following, unless the foreign government employee meets the definition of a foreign diplomatic officer accredited to the United States: 

Personal employees or attendants of foreign government or international organization officials who are not employed by the foreign government, including personal assistants, chauffeurs, or housecleaners;[31] 
Employees of state-owned enterprises in a status other than those in A or G nonimmigrant visa classification;[32] 
Third-country nationals working for a foreign government, including at a foreign embassy or consulate;[33] 
Employees of international organizations beyond those international organizations designated by executive order as enjoying immunity in the United States under the International Organizations Immunities Act;[34] 
Contractors of foreign missions, international organizations, or governments in a status other than those in A or G nonimmigrant visa classification;[35] and
Foreign government employees visiting the United States in a personal rather than an official capacity.[36] 
D. Birth Outside the United States

In addition, persons who are born outside of the United States may be U.S. citizens at birth if one or both parents were U.S. citizens at their time of birth. Persons who are not U.S. citizens at birth may become U.S. citizens through naturalization. Naturalization is the conferring of U.S. citizenship after birth by any means whatsoever, including acquisition of citizenship. Persons may acquire citizenship after birth, but before the age of 18, through their U.S. citizen parents.

In general, an applicant files a naturalization application and then USCIS grants citizenship after adjudicating the application. In some cases, a person may be naturalized by operation of law. This is often referred to as deriving citizenship. In either instance, the applicant must fulfill all of the requirements established by Congress. In most cases, a person may not be naturalized unless he or she has been lawfully admitted to the United States for permanent residence.

Deciding to become a U.S. citizen is one of the most important decisions an immigrant can make. Naturalized U.S. citizens share equally in the rights and privileges of U.S. citizenship. U.S. citizenship offers immigrants the ability to:

Vote in federal elections;
​Travel with a U.S. passport;
Run for elective office where citizenship is required;
Participate on a jury;
Become eligible for federal and certain law enforcement jobs;
Obtain certain state and federal benefits not available to aliens;
Obtain citizenship for minor children born abroad; and
Expand and expedite their ability to bring family members to the United States.
