## Hook

At a rally in Montana this October, President Trump told the crowd that "tariffs don't cause inflation". [s01 ok] If he is right, a tariff costs shoppers nothing extra. If he is wrong, somebody here is paying it.

## Setup

In his State of the Union, Trump said who he thinks pays: countries that were ripping us off "are now paying us hundreds of billions of dollars". [s03 ok] On his channel, Patrick Boyle puts it the other way round: "A tariff is just a tax paid by Americans, not by Canadians." [s04 ok] They cannot both be right. So follow the cost, starting at the border.

## Investigation

If foreign sellers were paying, they would have cut their prices to make room for the tariff, so economists at the New York Fed checked their prices with the tariff taken out. [s06 ok] In the first eight months of 2025, a 10 percent tariff cut those prices by only 0.6 percentage points, which by the Fed's estimate left 94 percent of the cost with American importers. [s07 ok] Foreign sellers did start giving more ground late in the year, yet in November importers still carried 86 percent. [s08 ok] But an importer paying at the border is not yet a shopper paying at the till. How much of it reaches the shelf? A New York Fed staff report on the 2025 tariffs estimates that consumer prices rise by about 26 percent of the tariff increase, roughly a quarter of the tariff rate, measured against goods less exposed to the tariffs. [s10 ok] And not all of that comes from imports: 36 percent of it comes from goods made at home, because American producers pay more for imported parts, and with foreign rivals pricier, they raise their markups. [s11 ok] Nor does it arrive the day a tariff is announced. In the New York Fed's estimates, about half of the direct effect on imported goods is in shop prices after three months, and all of it after six, as retailers take a few months to pass on higher costs. [s13 ok] The part that runs through American-made goods is slower still, taking nine to twelve months to work through supply chains. [s14 ok] Firms in the New York Fed's business surveys gave their reasons for spreading increases out: some sell under fixed-price contracts they cannot change until they expire, and some raise prices a little at a time to avoid shocking customers. [s15 ok] So is a tariff a one-time bump in prices, or lasting inflation? The New York Fed estimates the tariffs' effect on the level of consumer goods prices peaked near 3 percent in February 2026, and forecasts it to ease to about 2 percent by August after a Supreme Court ruling cut some tariffs, then edge up again with new tariffs on Canadian goods. [s17 ok] Its contribution to the yearly rise in consumer goods prices also peaked that February, at 2.9 percentage points, and is forecast to fall to around zero by August. [s18 ok] But the Fed's survey researchers warn that "one-time" can be "a drawn-out affair, especially when the tariffs change frequently", with many firms spreading price increases across extended periods. [s19 ok]

## Answer

So who pays? By the Fed's estimates, mostly not foreign sellers: American importers carried the bulk of the 2025 tariffs, and part of that cost reached consumer prices, including for goods made at home. [s20 ok] What these estimates cannot give you is the economy-wide inflation rate: they compare goods more and less exposed to tariffs, and leave out effects through the exchange rate and the Fed's monetary-policy response. [s21 ok] Within those limits, the tariffs look more like a step up in prices than lasting inflation, though a step that firms are taking slowly. [s22 ok]

## Takeaway

Next time someone says another country pays a tariff, ask one question: did that country's sellers cut their prices? And when a tariff changes, don't judge it by next week's receipts; the goods made at home take the better part of a year to catch up. [s24 ok]
