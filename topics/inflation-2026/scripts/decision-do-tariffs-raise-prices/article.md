## Who pays for a tariff? [heading:hook fp=d4d15768cb]

At a rally in Kalispell, Montana, this October, President Trump told supporters that "tariffs don't cause inflation". [s01 ok fp=32accc2cc0] If that holds, a tariff costs American shoppers nothing; if it does not, someone in the United States is paying it. [s02 fp=f5fff51f62] Research at the New York Fed on the 2025 tariffs points the other way: most of the cost stayed in America, and part of it reached the prices shoppers pay. [s03 ok fp=7e71fb98de]

## Foreign sellers or American buyers: what each side says [heading:setup fp=7c170072b8]

In his State of the Union address, Trump described the countries he had tariffed as "now paying us hundreds of billions of dollars". [s04 ok fp=39323a1558] Interviewed on BlazeTV, Jim Rickards argued the same way, concluding that "tariffs don't cause inflation". [s05 ok fp=f07ba1bf37] On PBS's Firing Line, Scott Lincicome answered the opposite: "in most cases, it is actually the American consumer, companies or individuals, paying it." [s06 ok fp=289804417a] These views make different predictions about something measurable: the prices foreign sellers charge before the tariff is added. [s07 fp=8a600d4a88]

## Where the cost of the 2025 tariffs landed [heading:investigation fp=5ccdeace48]

New York Fed economists tested exactly that, relating each product's import price with the tariff taken out, which is the foreign seller's price, to the change in its tariff, using monthly trade data by product and country. [s08 ok fp=02e2de80fb] For the first eight months of 2025 they put 6 percent of the tariff's incidence on foreign sellers and 94 percent on the United States: a 10 percent tariff lowered foreign export prices by only 0.6 percentage points. [s09 ok fp=e555e83f7b] Foreign sellers gave more ground late in the year: in November a 10 percent tariff came with a 1.4 percent fall in their prices, which still left 86 percent of the incidence in the United States. [s10 ok fp=872296790b]

| | Foreign exporters | United States |
|---|---|---|
| January to August 2025 | 6 percent | 94 percent |
| September to October 2025 | 8 percent | 92 percent |
| November 2025 | 14 percent | 86 percent |

The authors' conclusion is that American firms and consumers "continue to bear the bulk of the economic burden" of the tariffs. [s11 ok fp=f19a5c12db] A bill paid at the border, though, is not yet a higher price on a shop shelf. [s12 fp=c962e6b728] A New York Fed staff report follows the cost that far: for the 2025 tariffs it estimates that about 26 percent of a tariff increase passes through to consumer prices, measured against goods less exposed to the tariffs. [s13 ok fp=76e17e4234] Of that rise, 64 percent comes directly from imported goods costing more and 36 percent from goods made in the United States, whose producers pay more for imported inputs and raise their markups when competing imports get dearer. [s14 ok fp=8ef5d4e0ce]

Figure decision-do-tariffs-raise-prices/sc11: Where the consumer price rise from the tariffs comes from: imported goods directly, and goods made at home indirectly. [decision-do-tariffs-raise-prices/sc11 fp=889baff768]

These parts move at different speeds: tariffs lift import prices almost at once, but the part that runs through domestic producers takes nine to twelve months to work through supply chains. [s15 ok fp=b9488ad21f] Even the direct part does not reach shoppers the day a tariff is announced: by the Fed's estimates about half of it is in consumer prices after three months and all of it after six, as retailers take a few months to pass on higher costs. [s16 ok fp=6e33806871] Firms in the New York Fed's business surveys described why: some sell under fixed-price contracts they cannot raise until the contracts expire, and some take a trickle-up approach, raising prices gradually so as not to shock customers. [s17 ok fp=f82563103f] That leaves the question of how long the higher prices last. [s18 fp=444c0d7db4] A later New York Fed analysis estimates that the tariffs' effect on the consumer goods price level peaked near 3 percent in February 2026, and forecasts it easing to about 2 percent by August 2026, after a Supreme Court ruling replaced the emergency-power tariffs with a lower 10 percent surcharge, then edging up again, mainly because of tariffs on Canadian goods. [s19 ok fp=1f9a4cde78] The tariffs' contribution to the twelve-month change in consumer goods prices also peaked that February, at 2.9 percentage points, and is forecast to fall to around zero by August 2026, then below zero as the 2025 increases drop out of the comparison. [s20 ok fp=22f4ca7790]

|---|---|

In the authors' words, tariffs "have left consumer goods price levels higher, while their effect on inflation fades." [s21 ok fp=eea26008ba]

**unresolved (inflation-2026/tariffs-push-prices-once-raising-price):** Whether tariffs amount to a single step in prices is not settled by this evidence: the Fed's survey researchers write that a one-time price-level adjustment "may be a drawn-out affair, especially when the tariffs change frequently". [s23 ok fp=0012d31183] Their surveys find many firms spreading price increases across extended periods, so the price pressure from tariffs may last for some time. [s24 ok fp=1c452be032]

## So who pays, and do tariffs raise prices? [heading:answer fp=be4c72727b]

By these estimates, foreign sellers absorbed only a small share of the 2025 tariffs; American importers paid most of the cost, and part of it reached consumer prices, including the prices of goods made at home. [s25 ok fp=d2e5f2761a] The claim that tariffs do not raise consumer prices does not survive this evidence: the staff report concludes that the tariffs had "economically and statistically significant effects on consumer prices". [s26 ok fp=5cc05bf99b] What it cannot say is how much tariffs added to inflation across the whole economy: it compares goods by how exposed they were to tariffs, and leaves out effects through the exchange rate, the monetary-policy response and other economy-wide forces. [s27 ok fp=47872acab8] Within those limits, the tariffs look more like a raised level of prices than lasting inflation, though firms are taking that step slowly. [s28 ok fp=d28e24d223]

## How to read the next claim about tariffs [heading:takeaway fp=ec6f11fbc5]

When someone says another country pays a tariff, ask whether that country's sellers cut their prices. [s29 fp=150a928505] And judge a tariff's effect on prices over the months that follow it, not the week after: the goods made at home are the last to catch up. [s30 ok fp=b87f7d3eb2]
