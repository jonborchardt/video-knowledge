## Veteran or newcomer: who gets more from an AI coding assistant? [heading:hook fp=0f85bba7a2]

Imagine a veteran engineer and a recent hire who start using the same coding assistant this week. [s01 fp=ce89149c37] Popular commentary expects the veteran to pull away; this article checks that expectation against the trials that split their results by experience, and then asks why expertise can make the tool slower rather than faster. [s02 fp=65c1ca8b8a]

## Does AI help junior or senior developers more? [heading:question-1 fp=d6fa83c4a4]

The case for experts is usually made by practitioners: on The Pragmatic Engineer, in a conversation about Shopify, the guest suggested that "the AI agents are going to help the best engineers more than the mediocre engineer". [s03 ok fp=bddb4562a4] Simon Willison, on the same podcast, expected his experience combined with the tools to let him "run circles around somebody who's never written a code line of code in their life". [s04 ok fp=97558646cc] Willison's comparison is with someone who has never programmed, which none of the trials below makes; what they can check is whether experienced developers gained more than less experienced ones. [s05 fp=9e9d86fc9b]

A field experiment at Microsoft that gave developers Copilot in their jobs points the opposite way: the output gain for short-tenure developers was 27 to 39 percent, against 8 to 13 percent for long-tenure ones. [s06 ok fp=330e988733] Those differences fell short of conventional statistical significance, yet all three main output measures showed the same ordering. [s07 ok fp=edb694374d] The authors caution that longer-tenure developers who dropped the tool after trying it may pull their estimate down, but juniors and seniors used it equally once they had adopted it, and juniors still gained more. [s08 ok fp=2d44c2ab7c]

Table t1: Output gain with Copilot in the Microsoft field experiment, by tenure and by seniority; ranges span the main output measures, and the estimates are noisy and not statistically significant [t1 fp=296ad69633]

| | Output gain |
|---|---|
| Short-tenure developers | 27% to 39% |
| Long-tenure developers | 8% to 13% |
| Junior developers | 21% to 40% |
| Senior developers | 7% to 16% |

A separate experiment's moderation analysis found that developers with fewer years of experience gained speed from generative AI with no change in the quality of their code. [s09 ok fp=6388001ed4] In a Copilot lab trial, programmers with less experience benefited more, a result its authors describe as promising for people moving into software development, provided further studies confirm it. [s10 ok fp=4915a989a9] A study spanning bachelor's students to working professionals found that every group completed significantly more of its task with AI, and the authors judged the differences between groups unrelated to experience. [s11 ok fp=96a8752135] Professionals did gain more than bachelor's students there, but the groups were given different tasks matched to their level, with the most complex going to professionals and researchers. [s12 ok fp=06d5bd6337]

METR's trial went to the other extreme: open-source maintainers averaging 5 years and 1,500 commits in the very repositories they worked on. [s13 ok fp=5c96d8bddb]

Figure decision-who-benefits/sc11: How long, on average, the METR trial's developers had worked in their own repositories. [decision-who-benefits/sc11 fp=29730467ca]

Given AI, these maintainers were slowed rather than sped up, with moderate evidence of a larger slowdown on issues they had high prior exposure to, and they themselves reported AI helping most on unfamiliar issues and least on familiar ones. [s14 ok fp=f77bbd2039] One developer wrote: "if I am the dedicated maintainer of a very specialized part of the codebase, there is no way agent mode can do better than me." [s15 ok fp=d7b8135671]

**unresolved (ai-coding/coding-tools-amplify-experienced-engineers-far):** Google's trial, on a complex task, pointed the other way: more senior engineers were if anything faster with AI than junior ones, but the interaction between AI and seniority was not statistically significant. [s16 ok fp=3a8f976847] Its authors think AI may not yet be able to close a skill gap on tasks as complex as the one they set. [s17 ok fp=eee58a851c]

A softer version of the expert claim holds that using AI well is a skill in itself, so practised users should pull ahead. [s18 fp=646131306f] In METR's trial, more Cursor experience did not broadly shrink the slowdown up to 50 hours of use, a count that includes hours the developers reported from before the study. [s19 ok fp=5dc0616248] The lone developer beyond 50 hours did show a speedup, but after another participant corrected their reported experience, the estimate for that group dropped from roughly 25 percent to roughly 0 percent. [s20 ok fp=eed434e0ca]

Figure decision-who-benefits/sc16: METR's estimate for developers with the most Cursor experience, before and after a participant corrected their reported hours. [decision-who-benefits/sc16 fp=bcc2945883]

Google's trial found no significant link between how often engineers already used AI tools and their speed with AI; the authors' candidate explanations include too little statistical power, a low usability floor, and prompting skills that may not yet be universal. [s21 ok fp=ad90ca3a68] The strongest support for tool skill comes from the Echoes of AI maintainability trial: the more practised with AI the original developers were, the more their use of AI raised the CodeHealth of the code someone else then built on it, though the original developer's Java proficiency mattered more. [s22 ok fp=ca31827ef1]

Taken together, no trial shows AI amplifying experienced engineers far more than novices: several found larger gains for less experienced developers, with noisy or provisional estimates, and the one result favouring seniors was not significant. [s23 ok fp=27f8c9750e] The common thread is context: gains were larger for developers newer to the company in the Microsoft field experiment and on unfamiliar issues in METR's trial, and smaller for maintainers on tasks they knew well. [s24 ok fp=5c840e5fe9]

## Why can AI assistance cost expert programmers more time than it saves? [heading:question-2 fp=c413d90f36]

The METR maintainers kept fewer than 44 percent of Cursor's generations. [s25 ok fp=2b01dff6e2] Rejected output was not free: developers re-prompted or gave up, and the researchers observed significant time spent reviewing, testing or modifying code that was then thrown away. [s26 ok fp=e1e7ff2a9e] Accepted output had a cost too, by the developers' own account: 75 percent read every line of it, and 56 percent often had to make major changes to clean it up. [s27 ok fp=7beceb887d]

Table t2: What METR's experienced developers did with AI-generated code; the review shares are the developers' own reports [t2 fp=ad9682e76e]

| | Share |
|---|---|
| Cursor generations accepted | <44% |
| Developers who read every line of AI code | 75% |
| Developers who often make major changes to clean it up | 56% |
| Developers who report needing to modify AI code | 100% |

METR counts that unreliability, and the review time it wastes, as one of the factors that held back AI for its developers. [s28 ok fp=4ab148b185] Engineers at Anthropic weigh a related cost when choosing what to delegate: whether prompting would take longer than doing the job. Of a job expected to take less than 10 minutes, one said, "I'm probably not going to bother using Claude." [s29 ok fp=f0fb3fe31d] Another pointed to knowledge about the team's code that Claude lacks by default and, rather than refine a prompt, concluded: "I'm just going to go and do it myself." [s30 ok fp=18e70e8b1a]

**unresolved (gap 0):** These are interview accounts of what engineers choose to delegate, not a measurement of how much time checking costs. [s31 ok fp=cc2b6cbfae]

Review capacity limited a much larger job as well: in an AI-assisted migration of 5,359 files at one company, engineers' review speed was the bottleneck, and the team throttled how many changes it generated each week. [s32 ok fp=eea06f68f3] A review of the vibe-coding literature puts a verification gap at the centre of its findings: AI-assisted workflows generate code faster than developers and their processes can check it for correctness, security and provenance. [s33 ok fp=a369cf66c5] The same review suggests that the skills of verifying, specifying constraints and reviewing may count for as much as the skill of writing code. [s34 ok fp=6812433948]

**unresolved (gap 1):** The review's link between speed and checking is indirect: it notes that sources often discuss defects alongside productivity gains and suggests that faster development may add verification work, without measuring the time that work takes. [s35 ok fp=2e759cb763]

Anthropic's study names the bind a "paradox of supervision": getting value from Claude means supervising it, and supervising it takes the coding skills that heavy use may erode. [s36 ok fp=170271af81]

## What to do with this [heading:close fp=46adb3881a]

Before handing a task to an assistant, weigh how much of it you already know against how long it would take to check what comes back. [s37 fp=9bb052ed90] Expect the most from it on code that is new to you, be wary of promises that it multiplies experts, and if most of its output ends up deleted, keep the work you know for yourself. [s38 fp=68710cab61]
