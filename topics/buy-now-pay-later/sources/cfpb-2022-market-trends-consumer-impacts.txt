CONSUMER FINANCIAL PROTECTION BUREAU | SEPTEMBER 2022
Buy Now, Pay Later: Market trends and consumer impacts

1. Executive Summary

Consumers have long used credit instruments to purchase goods and services. In recent years, a
fast-growing alternative to credit cards has emerged in a number of developed economies
around the world, including in the United States. This alternative is marketed as "Buy Now, Pay
Later" (BNPL).

BNPL is a form of credit that allows a consumer to split a retail transaction into smaller,
interest-free installments and repay over time. The typical BNPL structure divides a $50 to
$1,000 purchase into four equal installments, with the first installment paid as a down payment
due at checkout, and the next three due in two-week intervals over six weeks. When a borrower
does not make these payments, many BNPL lenders charge late fees, often around $7 per missed
payment on an average loan size of $135.

In December 2021, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) issued market
monitoring orders to five lenders (Affirm, Afterpay, Klarna, PayPal, and Zip) to provide data on
their BNPL loans. This market report summarizes that data, individual and organizational
submissions to the CFPB, and publicly available sources to provide a review of BNPL's
marketplace importance and consumer impacts in the United States.

Important metrics from the report include:

    The BNPL industry is in the midst of rapid growth. From 2019 to 2021, the number of
        BNPL loans originated in the U.S. by the five lenders surveyed grew by 970 percent, from
        16.8 to 180 million, while the dollar volume of those originations (Gross Merchandise
        Volume, or GMV) grew by 1,092 percent, from $2 billion to $24.2 billion.

    73 percent of applicants were approved for credit in 2021, up from 69 percent in 2020.

    The average individual order value (average purchase amount financed by a BNPL loan)
        in 2021 was $135, up from $121 in 2020.

    10.5 percent of borrowers were charged at least one late fee in 2021, up from 7.8 percent
        in 2020.

    13.7 percent of individual loans in 2021 had at least some portion of the order that was
        returned, up from 12.2 percent in 2020.

    3.8 percent of borrowers had a loan that was charged off in 2021, up from 2.9 percent in
        2020.

The report identifies several competitive benefits of BNPL loans over legacy credit products.
These benefits are both financial (no interest and sometimes no late fees) and operational
(ubiquitous, easy to access, simple repayment structure).

The report also identifies several potential consumer risks, which fit into three broad areas of
concern:

    Discrete consumer harms. The BNPL product is often structured in ways that may
        present borrowers with undesirable operational hurdles, including the lack of clear
        disclosures of loan terms, challenges in filing and resolving disputes, and a requirement
        to use autopay for all loan payments.

    Data harvesting. Similar to many other large tech platforms, BNPL lenders often
        collect consumer data -- and deploy models, product features, and marketing campaigns
        based on that data -- to increase the likelihood of incremental sales and maximize the
        lifetime value it can extract from each current, past, or potential borrower. These
        practices may compromise consumers' privacy and autonomy and contribute to the
        overextension risks described below.

    Overextension. The BNPL business model may encourage overextension, and in doing
        so present a pair of risks: loan stacking, which can cause borrowers to take out several
        loans within a short time frame at simultaneous lenders; and sustained usage, in which
        frequent BNPL consumption over a period of months and years may affect consumers'
        ability to meet non-BNPL obligations.

3.3.1 Compulsory autopay

All five lenders surveyed allow borrowers to change their payment method for the second
through fourth installments. BNPL lenders have different operational policies concerning
payment method removal (removing autopay without adding a new payment method). One
lender allows borrowers to do so on its self-service online portal. A second and third lender
allow it only via customer service. Two other lenders generally prohibit the practice, meaning
that borrowers cannot turn autopay "off."

By implementing these policies, lenders can benefit from increased payment "stickiness" to keep
credit losses below a desired threshold. However, from the borrower's perspective, forced
autopay may have the effect of depriving those borrowers of a degree of agency. A borrower
facing multiple concurrent debts and bills may prefer to prioritize other obligations over their
BNPL loan, and policies that limit that ability can be harmful to the borrower's financial
well-being. Likewise, not allowing borrowers to easily remove their payment method could
inadvertently lead to overdraft.

3.3.2 Late fees

Three of the five lenders surveyed charge late fees for missed payments, typically the minimum
of a flat fee or a percentage of the missed payment. One of the lenders that currently charges
late fees does not limit late fee amounts on either a per-installment or per-loan basis, except in
two states where a limit is required by law.

Of the three lenders who charge late fees, two have a policy to charge a late fee at 10 days past
due, while the other lender may charge the fee after one day past due. In the past, several lenders
have reserved the ability to charge multiple late fees for a single missed installment, and one
lender outside the scope of the inquiry may currently charge two late fees after a missed
payment.

In 2021, 10.5 percent of borrowers were charged at least one late fee, up from 7.9 percent in
2020. Lenders' share of revenues from consumer fees (late fees and other fees) was 13.4 percent
in 2021, up from 11.7 percent in 2020. Late fees specifically accounted for 6.9 percent of
consumer-fee revenue growth from 2020 to 2021. Late fees in the BNPL should be considered
in the context of a product for which virtually all borrowers are on autopay: as a result, the
assessment of a late fee suggests that a borrower's primary form of payment either had
insufficient funds or credit, or was no longer valid.

3.3.3 Dispute resolution

Dispute resolution is the top-ranking BNPL-related complaint category in the CFPB's Consumer
Complaint Database (a review of consumer complaints received between Jan. 1, 2021 and Mar.
31, 2022). Other common complaint topics included issues with debt collection: consumers
complained that BNPL credit was applied for fraudulently by someone else, that their accounts
were sent to collection for items they had returned, or that they were contacted by collection
agencies about debts they had already paid.

One lender surveyed requires continued payments pending a dispute, two lenders allow
borrowers to defer payment for a short time following a reported dispute, and two lenders stop
payment indefinitely during a reported dispute. Though not all lenders provided the
information, most require that borrowers submit disputes within 60 or 120 days.

3.3.4 Other fees

In addition to late fees, two lenders either currently charge or recently charged other consumer
fees. One lender charged a non-sufficient funds (NSF) fee of $25 (later lowered to $10 or the
installment amount, whichever was lower) for ACH payments that failed to clear, resulting in a
chargeback; the cumulative amount of NSF fees collected across the three years covered by the
CFPB's market monitoring orders was approximately $640,000, and in 2021 the amount of NSF
fees charged amounted to less than one percent of that year's late fees. The lender stopped
charging NSF fees in Q4 2021. Another lender charges a fee on virtual-card-facilitated loans
made with non-partnered merchants, amounting to $1 per installment payment.

3.3.5 Failed payments, re-presentments, and charge-offs

In 2021, 4.1 percent of all loans experienced one or more declined or failed payment, and 7.5
percent of all borrowers experienced a failed or declined payment, up from 2019 and 2020. All
five BNPL lenders re-present (attempt to reauthorize) failed payments, in some instances up to
eight times for a single installment. Consumers may experience harm from multiple attempted
presentments in the form of fees charged by the consumer's bank associated with the payment
method, along with the downstream impacts to consumers when those funds are unavailable to
pay other obligations.

3.3.6 Collections

All five of the lenders surveyed have, at some point between 2019 and 2021, used third-party
debt collectors to collect delinquent loan balances. Two of the lenders only used debt collectors
for specific, short intervals, while the other three use debt collectors as part of their general
collections strategy. One common industry practice is to refer loans to debt collectors after the
loans have reached charged-off status. Debt collectors collecting BNPL loans rely more heavily
on digital communications channels (text and email) than on traditional channels such as phone
calls and paper mail. Throughout the three years surveyed, all of the agreements between BNPL
lenders and debt collectors were on a contingency placement basis (the debt collector earns a
commission on debts collected, but the debts remain on the BNPL lender's balance sheet) rather
than sold to a third-party debt buyer.

5.1 Introduction

At their core, the benefits and risks that BNPL provides borrowers are two sides of the same
coin, one that defines much of today's digital and mobile experiences: free and seamless. Like
their "free-and-seamless" tech peers (Google, Facebook, Pinterest, Robinhood), BNPL lenders
offer an easy-to-access, interest-free product, increasingly packaged in an app-driven format
that enriches the user experience and the behavioral-data-driven feedback loop. However, these
same features that make BNPL popular may present risks to borrowers' short- and long-term
financial health.

5.2 Benefits

By contrast with credit cards (whose total cost of credit the CFPB separately found to be 17.7
percent for revolving cardholders, and which cost Americans roughly $120 billion per year in
interest and fees), BNPL is typically a no-interest product. Borrowers who miss BNPL payments
may be prohibited from future use until they repay or face late fees, but those fees are relatively
low in absolute terms and do not compound as does credit card interest. Consequently, BNPL
can be a low-cost alternative to other credit products.

In summary: a review of the claims that BNPL lenders make about financial benefits reveals one
indisputable fact and one unproven hypothesis. The fact is that BNPL imposes significantly
lower direct financial costs on consumers than legacy credit products. The hypothesis is that the
nature of the product structure and underwriting strategy minimizes the likelihood of BNPL
inducing overspending and debt cycles. Section 5.3 analyzes this hypothesis.

Compared to legacy credit predecessors, BNPL offers an easy-to-use, accessible product: a short
list of required user inputs, an instantaneous credit decision, a high credit approval rate, a
straightforward repayment structure, and (in the app-driven model) a discovery engine that lets
users instantly browse dozens of products and brands, tailored to the individual user's interests.
However, the same factors driving these operational and UX benefits may also lead to consumer
risk via two distinct forms of overextension.

5.3 Overextension Risks

BNPL lenders promote their value proposition to retailers with the phrase "incremental sales" --
retail revenue generated because of their platforms that would not have occurred without them
-- citing metrics such as "85% higher merchant AOV compared to other payment methods," a
"41% increase in average order value," and "30% increase in conversion." These data points
suggest that many BNPL consumers may not simply be shifting their existing purchases to a new
payment platform; they may be spending (and borrowing) more than they otherwise would.

Repeat usage is a critical feature of the BNPL product, not a coincidence: lenders emphasize it
both to merchants and to investors ("81% of our transactions were from repeat...users," per one
lender's earnings call). For many borrowers, this incremental consumption may not result in
negative financial ramifications. However, a subset of the BNPL borrower base is at risk of
overextension, in two distinct forms: loan stacking and sustained usage.

5.3.1 Loan Stacking

Loan stacking is the risk that a borrower takes out concurrent BNPL loans at different lenders
and is unable to repay some or all of them. Because most BNPL lenders only make "soft" credit
bureau inquiries, BNPL lenders have no visibility into an applicant's borrowing activity on other
BNPL platforms. Even if the credit extended by any single lender does not exceed a few hundred
dollars, the ease with which an applicant could borrow that much from multiple BNPL lenders
within a short time frame could quickly affect their financial health. Consumer advocates have
voiced worries about this risk, particularly since BNPL lenders historically have not furnished
loan performance data to the credit bureaus: "even with credit checking, lenders (BNPL or
other lenders) have no lens into how much BNPL debt borrowers are carrying."

5.3.2 Sustained Usage

Sustained usage is the risk that frequent BNPL usage may threaten borrowers' ability to meet
non-BNPL financial obligations, such as rent, utilities, mortgages, auto loans, and student loans.
Unlike loan stacking, which can occur almost instantly, sustained usage may take months or even
years to fully appear, and it is borne not by those who fail to repay their BNPL debts (who are
usually cut off from future use) but by the habitual borrower who keeps repaying and so retains
continual access to an often-increasing amount of BNPL credit.

A 2017 study by a nationwide credit reporting company found that consumers with at least one
active personal loan, auto loan, mortgage, and credit card prioritized the personal loan payments
above the other three -- a result the study's authors found surprising, since personal loans are
unsecured and (unlike credit cards) offer no future credit utility. Two hypotheses were offered:
personal loans' fixed installments make autopay especially sticky, and personal loans' smaller
average size makes full payment feel more attainable than partial payment on a bigger debt.

The structure of BNPL is closely aligned to personal loans in these same two ways: autopay is a
de facto requirement for most BNPL lenders (one surveyed lender found only 0.1 percent of
active loans lacked a card on file), and BNPL's average order value of $135 breaks into four
installments of under $35 each -- small enough that a consumer struggling with several
concurrent debts may still find the funds to cover a BNPL installment ahead of a larger bill.
BNPL also adds attributes legacy personal loans lack: rewards programs, in-app discovery
engines, and dark-pattern- and individual-behavior-enhanced app features designed to maximize
conversion and consumption, all of which increase the product's likelihood of habitual reuse.

An Australian regulator's study of BNPL users found that, in the prior 12 months, to make BNPL
payments on time, 20 percent of consumers surveyed cut back on or went without essentials
(e.g., meals), and 15 percent took out an additional loan. A separate US consumer survey found
that 32 percent of respondents had to "delay or skip paying an essential bill due to the payments
on [their] Buy Now Pay Later plans." US consumer surveys more broadly have found that
between 20 and 50 percent of BNPL consumers regret their purchases, though the surveys'
differing methodologies and the ambiguity of what counts as "BNPL" in each limit how much
weight any single figure can bear.

6. Takeaways and Risks

6.1 Takeaways

    User experience gaps exist for important account management features. Some
        BNPL lenders' policies make it difficult for borrowers to directly initiate a product return
        or dispute, instead requiring borrowers to first contact the merchant. Lenders also often
        require autopay enrollment as a condition for taking out a BNPL loan, and some make
        removing autopay difficult or even impossible.

    There are divergent company approaches on late fees and consumer fees as a
        whole. Across the five lenders surveyed, the share of borrowers charged at least one late
        fee increased from 7.9 percent in 2020 to 10.5 percent in 2021, but individual lenders'
        2021 rates ranged from 0 percent to 29.7 percent, and the share of 2021 revenue derived
        from consumer fees ranged from 0 percent to 52.8 percent across the five.

    Proprietary apps broaden and deepen the BNPL lender-borrower relationship.
        By driving more traffic through their proprietary apps, lenders are able to own and
        control virtually every aspect of the borrower relationship, leading to higher repeat usage.
        This app-driven repeat usage -- often aided by harvested consumer data and
        behavioral-science-guided "dark patterns" -- may exacerbate the risks of overextension.

6.2.1 Discrete Consumer Harms

The CFPB's analysis of typical BNPL product features demonstrates that some market
participants' offerings appear to be structured to evade certain federal consumer lending
requirements. These requirements are designed to protect consumers and create a level playing
field that fosters competition and consumer choice. Conversely, the absence of these
requirements may lead to a competitive imbalance both within the BNPL industry and against
similar products, as well as cause harm to consumers.

Lack of standardized disclosures. Transparency is a bedrock principle in U.S. credit
regulation. The Truth in Lending Act (TILA), as implemented by Regulation Z, requires that
lenders subject to its provisions provide consumers with a uniform set of clear and conspicuous
cost-of-credit disclosures -- including the amount financed, total number of payments, finance
charge, annual percentage rate, and potential late fee disclosures -- before consumers agree to
take out a loan. However, most BNPL lenders do not currently provide the standard cost-of-
credit disclosures required by Regulation Z or periodic statements, though at least one lender
currently does. The lack of clear, standardized disclosure language may obscure the true nature
of the product as credit and make important information about loan terms less accessible.

Dispute resolution challenges. Regulation Z requires creditors of open-end credit and credit
card issuers to provide billing dispute and error resolution rights, including the right to withhold
payment while a billing dispute is being resolved. Most BNPL lenders surveyed are currently not
following Regulation Z's credit dispute resolution provisions, and consumers sometimes are
required to pay loan installment amounts in dispute pending resolution.

Compulsory use of autopay. The Electronic Funds Transfer Act, as implemented by
Regulation E, prohibits a creditor from requiring a consumer to repay a loan through automatic
withdrawal from their deposit account (autopay). Most BNPL lenders nonetheless require that
borrowers use autopay, and some make removing autopay challenging or impossible. Autopay
increases the "stickiness" of the payment method provided at checkout, reducing collections
costs and credit losses for the lender, but may adversely limit consumer choice and flexibility to
skip a BNPL payment to satisfy other financial obligations.

Multiple payment re-presentments. All five BNPL lenders re-present failed payments, in
some instances up to eight times for a single installment, exposing consumers to repeated bank
fees and to the downstream impact of funds being unavailable for other obligations.

Late fees. At least one BNPL lender's policy permitted it to impose multiple late fees on the
same missed payment. Regulation Z prohibits credit card issuers from assessing multiple late
fees for the same missed payment, and the CARD Act of 2009 requires that late fees on an
open-end credit card account be "reasonable and proportional" -- requirements that do not
apply to BNPL.

6.2.2 Data Harvesting

The practice of harvesting and monetizing consumer data across the payments and lending
ecosystems may threaten consumers' privacy, security, and autonomy. It also may lead to a
consolidation of market power in the hands of a few large tech platforms, reduce long-term
innovation, choice, and price competition, and risks harvested data being used to offer targeted
discounts to some customers but not others -- meaning different groups of consumers could pay
different prices for the same goods at the same retailer.

BNPL lenders often collect a consumer's data -- and deploy models, product features, and
marketing campaigns based on that data -- to increase the likelihood of incremental sales and
maximize the lifetime value they can extract from the consumer. The use cases fall into two
categories: individual consumer demographic, psychographic, and behavioral data used to
optimize which products and brands are promoted to that consumer; and aggregated data used
to modify the general product experience (font, color scheme, word choice, user-flow order) to
drive consumer behavior in subtle ways toward a desired outcome. Both use cases confer
financial benefits to BNPL lenders, primarily through incremental merchant-discount-fee
revenue and increased affiliate-fee and interchange revenue.

None of the five lenders surveyed directly sells data to third parties. However, the fact that
BNPL lenders usually restrict their data usage to "first party" scenarios does not eliminate
potential consumer risks.
