## Hook

One finance channel, The Plain Bagel, tells its viewers that nearly 90 percent of e-commerce stores "fail within the first four months". [s01 ok] Iman Gadzhi, ranking side hustles for beginners, says of print-on-demand shops that "most stores die in the first few weeks or months". [s02 ok] You have probably heard some version of this at a party, on a podcast, or from a relative who wanted you to keep your day job. Most new businesses fail, and they fail fast. It sounds like hard-won wisdom. But is it a measurement, or a slogan? There is a way to check, because the US Bureau of Labor Statistics keeps a table that follows each year's newly opened private-sector establishments and counts how many are still operating, every year after they open. [s06 ok] So let's put the slogan next to the table and see where they part ways.

## Setup

First, it helps to notice that the slogan comes in more than one size. Barry's Economics, a channel that pushes back on hustle podcasts, gives the most specific version: "About 25% of businesses fail in their first year. 40% after 3 years, and half of businesses fail after 5 years." [s09 ok] Listen to how different that is from the first version. One says your shop is likely gone before the season changes. The other says starting a business is closer to a coin toss, spread over years. And Shopify, the e-commerce platform, writes on its own blog that "Only 34.7% of all US private-sector business establishments started in March 2013 were still operating a decade later." [s12 ok] So which is it: a few months, a few years, or a decade? And does any of it say something about an online store in particular, which is the business most of these videos are actually talking about? To answer that, we need one group of businesses, followed from the day they open.

## Investigation

In the year that ended in March 2015, 677,876 new private-sector establishments opened in the United States, according to the BLS table. [s16 ok] Think of them as a class photo, retaken every March to see who is still in the picture. One year later, in March 2016, 539,701 of them were still operating: 79.6 percent of the class. [s18 ok] Turn that around and 20.4 percent had closed within their first year. [s19 ok] That is a real loss. But it is not most. By March 2018, three years in, 61.4 percent of the class was still open. [s21 ok] So the first year accounts for a little over half of the closures in the first three years: 20.4 points of the 38.6 points lost. [s22 ok] And the share of last year's survivors that make it through another year climbs, from 79.6 percent in the first year to 86.8 percent in the second and 88.9 percent in the third. [s23 ok] From March 2021 on, it never drops below 92.4 percent. [s24 ok] In other words, the danger is front-loaded. A business that makes it through its first couple of years is a much safer bet than one that has just opened. Now the five-year mark, the one Barry's Economics used: by March 2020, 50.2 percent of the class was still operating. [s26 ok] That is the coin toss, almost exactly. And ten years on, in March 2025, 235,071 were left out of the original 677,876: 34.7 percent. [s28 ok] Put the other way, 65.3 percent were gone within the decade. [s29 ok]

## Findings

So let's go back to the versions of the slogan we started with. The specific one, from Barry's Economics, is close on the shape: the table has about half closed within five years, though the first-year loss in this cohort is 20.4 percent rather than 25. [s32 ok] Shopify's figure is for establishments started in 2013, and the class of 2015 we have been following also ends its decade at 34.7 percent. [s33 ok] And the dramatic version, nearly 90 percent gone within months, is nowhere in this table, though the table covers all new establishments, not online stores: after a full year, 79.6 percent of them were still open. [s34 ok] So most of the new establishments in this table do close eventually, but most of them are still standing after a few years. And an establishment, in the BLS definition, is typically a single location, like a store or a factory, while a firm may consist of one or more of them. [s35 ok] A second set of numbers, quoted second-hand, agrees that most close eventually. [s36 ok] Jon Taylor, in a book-length critique of multi-level marketing called The Case against MLM, cites the Small Business Administration as finding that 44 percent of small businesses survive at least four years and 31 percent at least seven. [s37 ok] He also cites a survey by the National Federation of Independent Business in which 64.2 percent of businesses had failed over a 10-year period. [s38 ok] Different agencies, different years, different definitions, and the passage does not tell us exactly how either one counted. And the SBA figures Taylor cites show lower survival: 44 and 31 percent at four and seven years, against 55.4 and 43.6 percent for the class of 2015. [s39 ok]

## Qualifications

Before you take that curve home, there are limits to what the table can say. The first is what a closure means. In the BLS definition, a closing is an establishment that reported employment at the end of one quarter and none in the next. [s41 ok] That records only that the jobs at one location stopped, not why. And because survival is measured location by location, a chain that shuts one branch has a closing in this count, even if the company carries on. [s58 ok] A sale is different. When an establishment changes owners, the BLS links the old record to the new one, so a sale is meant to show up as the same establishment carrying on, not as a closing. [s59 ok] So reading every closure as a failure goes further than these definitions allow. And one question stays open: nothing in the evidence here says whether a shop that moves to a new address counts as a closing. [s42 ok] The second is the part of the slogan most of these videos care about: the table has no breakdown for online stores at all. [s43 ok] Taylor's figures are general small-business numbers too, with nothing on online stores. [s44 ok] Whether online stores close faster than a bakery or a plumbing firm is a reasonable guess, but it is a guess. The evidence here cannot tell you how long the typical online store lasts, so the claims about e-commerce stores dying within weeks or months remain unchecked, not confirmed. [s46 ok]

## Implications

So what should you do with this, if you are thinking about starting something? First, the honest base rate for a new establishment is not almost certain failure; in this cohort it was a roughly even chance of still operating after five years. [s48 ok] That is sobering, but it is not a reason to dismiss every idea out of hand. Second, the riskiest single year is the first, when roughly a fifth of these establishments closed; planning to get through it is not a detail. [s50 ok] Say a friend tells you almost every online store dies within months, so why bother. You can ask them where that number comes from, and what it counts. And say a course seller tells you the same thing, then offers to put you among the lucky few. That is the same unchecked number, now doing a sales job. In both cases, a more useful question is: what does the survival curve look like for businesses like mine, and how will I know early if mine is on the wrong side of it?

## Conclusion

So, do most new businesses really fail within a few years? Not in the government's count of new establishments: about half of the class of 2015 was still operating after five years, and a clear majority, 65.3 percent, had gone by ten. [s55 ok] Most, eventually: in the government's establishment count, that part is right. Most within a few years: in that count it is not, because most were still open after three years, though the Small Business Administration figures Taylor cites, with 56 percent gone within four years, come closer to the slogan. And most online stores within months is a claim this evidence cannot check. [s56 ok] The next time someone quotes the failure statistic at you, to scare you off or to sell you something, ask for the curve, not the slogan.
