You make a little, not much. Not enough to pay the bills that are wrecking up. You make money from signing people up.' Oliver's own next words are the Glengarry Glen Ross joke. Attributing the sentence to Oliver misattributes a critic's one-liner to the host.
- s10 (s10.a) unsupported (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): 'The best-known trainer in the field' is a factual claim about the speaker's prominence that no assertion or passage covers; the quote itself is fine. The writer's note already offers to cut it.
- s37 (s37.a) overgeneralization (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): The FTC passage establishes that commissions can be paid for recruits' purchases (inducing others to purchase, recruiting participants who make such purchases). It does not say the 'customer' is 'often' the recruit; it quantifies nothing, and s35 and s53 say in the script's own words that the split between downline and retail income is not measured. 'Often' is a frequency claim the evidence does not establish and the script elsewhere disclaims.
- s49 (s49.b) causation (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): 'Which is exactly why' asserts that the pay plans routing money upward causes the vast majority to sit in the lowest ranks. The FTC passage reports the rank distribution and separately describes the pay structure; it draws no causal link, and footnote 34 says ranks are MLM-defined designations that do not necessarily correspond to income. s54 then says 'the rank tables show where people end up, not why', contradicting s49. The viewer hears a causal finding the evidence does not contain.
- s62 (s62.a) overgeneralization (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): 'The more than 90 percent below them' takes the 92.37% bottom row of one excerpted table and states it as a general fact about MLMs. The FTC's general finding is 'the vast majority' in the two or three lowest ranks of nearly every rank-based disclosure, not 'over 90 percent'. 'Paid for by' is also a money-flow claim: Taylor says most payout goes to the top levels in the plans he read, and the FTC describes commissions on recruits' purchases, but no source measures that the bottom cohort funds the top (s53 says the split is unmeasured).
- s09 (s09.b) selective (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): In the transcript the subject of the quote is the person you recruit, not you: 'the person who you bring in might reorder ... they might recruit 10 people themselves and have an organization of a thousand people but you've just brought one person in'. The narration's 'you "might recruit 10 people themselves"' shifts the pronoun (and reads oddly). The pitch point survives, but the quote's context should be stated correctly.
- s04 (s04.a) missing-qualification (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): 'In 2018, AARP asked' misdates the fieldwork: the survey ran from 9 September to 3 October 2017 (Appendix A) and the report was published in August 2018. The figures are right; the year attaches to the publication, not the asking.
- s27 (s27.a) missing-qualification (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): 'AARP did, in 2018' has the same date slip as s04: fieldwork was September to October 2017, publication August 2018. The sampling description (GfK KnowledgePanel, probability-based, generalisable to the US population) is established by the methods section at L105-L109.
- s15 (s15.a) omission (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): 'Many of them publish an income disclosure statement' leaves out what the report says about prevalence: staff reviewed the websites of the more than six hundred MLMs known to them and only 79 made one publicly available (footnote 15), and staff say the data 'may not be representative of data from all MLMs'. The 70 are the minority that publish, which also bears on the advice in s59 to ask for the statement. s51's 'not the whole industry' is true but does not convey the proportion.
- s16 (s16.a) missing-qualification (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): 'Read as published, those statements say most participants received $1,000 or less' misdescribes where the figure comes from. The statements do not say it; FTC staff derived it by working through fine print and 'careful application of algebra' for the 33 disclosures with enough data (Figure 14), and the report's conclusion limits it to 'those MLMs where the data is sufficient to calculate it'. The blog's shorter wording drops that limit. The number ('vast majority', under $84 a month) is right.
- s16 (s16.a) double-counting (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): The deterministic sheet counts the FTC blog summary and the FTC staff report as '2 supporting (2 independent)' here and on s08, s15, s17, s46, s51 and s60, although the package marks the blog sameStudyAs the report. The narration itself never presents them as two sources (it says 'three kinds of evidence'), so no change to the script; the independence count is one.
- s21 (s21.a) missing-qualification (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): '20 of them do not mention expenses at all' is stronger than the passage. The report says 20 'have no express statement about whether their figures account for expenses'; footnote 28 says ten of those imply the figures are gross, for example because 'the fine print discloses mandatory fees'. So some of the 20 do mention fees; what they omit is whether the figures are net of them.
- s22 (s22.a) missing-qualification (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): 'Before whatever the participant spent' is true of nearly all 70, but the report's wording is that none accounts for 'all' expenses; footnotes 29 and 59 note that three disclosures include some expenses and at least one nets specific costs. Imprecise at the margin only.
- s25 (s25.b) needs-human (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): 'Half the rows in that table describe half of one percent of the people' repeats the FTC's own rounding ('Half of the information displayed pertains to a mere half a percent'). Summing the table: the top five of ten rows cover about 0.16% of participants, the top six 0.51%. The sentence errs in the conservative direction (it makes the top look larger), and it is the FTC's characterisation, so it can stand; an editor may prefer 'as the FTC puts it' or 'the top five rows cover less than a fifth of one percent'.
- s33 (s33.a) missing-qualification (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): The FTC says MLMs 'often' offer commissions for benchmarks 'such as' purchasing, inducing others to purchase, or recruiting participants who purchase; the narration drops 'often' and 'such as' and presents the examples as the definition of how the plans work. 'In some cases' for retail income is quoted fairly: it is the FTC's own phrase, in the same paragraph, about the same plans.
- s42 (s42.a) omission (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): The sentence names only the spending assumption. Taylor's table also rests on assumed retention rates (10% to 15%, footnote 46), on counting dropouts as losers (footnote 48), and on assuming reported 'active' participants are at most half of all participants (footnote 45). The 99.6% and 'one in 238' depend on those as much as on expenses, and Taylor says so himself; the script's qualification is incomplete in a way that makes the figure sound more robust than he claims.
- s44 (s44.b) overgeneralization (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): The cited passage's subject is the classic 8-ball no-product pyramid scheme: 'In a classic 8-ball (1-2-4-8) no-product pyramid scheme ... The inevitable result of such pyramid schemes is that eventually recruitment will dry up as the market becomes saturated ... the vast majority of participants are guaranteed be in a losing position at the bottom.' Taylor treats MLMs as product-based pyramid schemes in the same column and applies the logic to them, but the sentence as cited is about the 8-ball example, and the narration presents it as a statement about MLM recruitment.
- s46 (s46.a) overgeneralization (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): 'And that is with the zero-earners left out of most of the tables' implies the $1,000 figure excludes the zero-earners. Staff's Figure 14 calculations used the fine print to put them back where possible (many entries' lower bound is '% receiving $0'), omitted MLMs where the missing group would substantively alter the result (footnote 58), and flag the remainder as possible underestimates. The exclusion count is also stretched: 29 disclosures exclude no-commission participants and 24 exclude 'inactive' ones, which 'in at least some cases' means zero-earners; 29 alone is not 'most' of 70. The direction (the true share at $1,000 or less is at least this high) is right.
- s60 (s60.a) overclaim (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 2): 'In the one nationally representative survey' can be heard as 'the only one in existence', which nothing in the corpus establishes; the sources establish that AARP's sample is nationally representative, not that it is unique. The figures are right and the self-report limit is stated in s50.
- s43 (s43.a) needs-human (raised against revision 1, addressed in revision 3): The attribution is accurate: Oliver himself says 'Let's say five. Then you let the five do their five ... you could only do that 13 cycles and you would exceed the population of the earth', so 'a handful' is fair. Note for the editor: with five per person the cumulative total after 13 cycles is about 1.5 billion, not the world's population; the script does not endorse the figure and s54 calls it an untested thought experiment, which covers it.
