## Hook

Somewhere in your messages there is probably a pitch like this. A friend you have not heard from in years has started a business, and she would love to have you on her team: you earn from what you sell, and from everyone you bring in after you. The recruiters have a word for that second part: one trainer promises to show you how to "finally create the leverage that you need inside of your network marketing business". [s03 ok] In a study published in 2018, AARP asked 601 Americans who had actually done this how it went, and 47 percent said they lost money. [s04 ok] Only 25 percent said they made any profit at all. [s05 ok] So where does the money go, and why does leverage that sounds so good on paper show up in almost nobody's bank account?

## Setup

This video is about one question: how much money do people in multi-level marketing actually make? To answer it we have three kinds of evidence: 70 of the companies' own income disclosure statements, read closely by Federal Trade Commission staff; a nationally representative survey of 601 participants by AARP; and an analysis of 30 companies by Jon Taylor, an independent analyst who argues against the industry. [s08 ok] First, the promise, in the recruiters' own words: one coach tells his audience that once the team grows, "it starts to compound", like a snowball; another says the person you bring in "might recruit 10 people themselves and have an organization of a thousand people". [s09 ok] Another trainer puts the whole idea in one line: it would be "better to have a smaller percentage of 100 people than 100 of yourself". [s10 ok] That is the pitch: income from the efforts of other people. The critics have their own one-liner; in a clip John Oliver's show played, a former Herbalife distributor puts it this way: "You make money from signing people up." [s12 ok] Even the FTC's own consumer page says, flatly, that "Most people who join legitimate MLMs make little or no money." [s13 ok] Two confident stories, so let us see which one the numbers tell.

## Investigation

Start with the companies, because some of them publish an income disclosure statement, and FTC staff collected 70 of them, from household names to small firms. [s15 ok] Once FTC staff worked through the fine print, the statements with enough data to tell showed most participants received $1,000 or less in a year: under $84 a month. [s16 ok] In at least 17 of the 70 companies, most participants made nothing at all. [s17 ok] And that is the generous reading, because of three things buried in the fine print. First, who gets counted: of the 70 statements, 29 leave out participants who earned no commission, and another 24 leave out anyone the company has labelled inactive. [s19 ok] What happens to an average when you remove the zeros before you take it? Second, what gets counted: not one of the 70 statements reports income with expenses taken out in full, and 20 of them never say whether expenses were taken out at all. [s21 ok] So the dollar figures are what the company paid out, before most or all of whatever the participant spent to take part. [s22 ok] Third, how it is presented: 44 of the 70 break the numbers out only by rank, and in those tables, nearly without exception, the vast majority of participants sit in the two or three lowest ranks. [s23 ok] One table the FTC excerpted shows the shape: 92.37 percent of participants in the bottom row, with average earnings of $41.69. [s24 ok] The top row averages just over $1,018,350, and holds fewer than 0.01 percent of participants; as the FTC puts it, half the rows in that table describe half of one percent of the people. [s25 ok] Disclosures count what companies paid, not whether anyone came out ahead; for that you have to ask the people. AARP did, in a study published in 2018, using a probability-based national panel, the kind of sample that lets you generalise to the whole country. [s27 ok] Among the 601 current and former participants it reached, 47 percent said they lost money, 27 percent said they broke even, and 25 percent said they made a profit, counting their entire time in the business. [s28 ok] Profit, for most of that quarter, was small: 14 percent of all participants made under $5,000 in total, and only 3 percent made $25,000 or more. [s29 ok] Three people, half of one percent of the sample, reported profits of $100,000 or more; those three had each been in the business more than five years, and one of them had founded the company. [s30 ok] On the other side of the ledger, 41 percent lost less than $5,000, 3 percent lost $5,000 to $9,999, and 1 percent lost more than $10,000. [s31 ok] These are people's own accounts of what they put in and took out, which is a limit; they are also the only figures so far that measure profit or loss rather than payout. [s65 ok] So most people lose or break even, and the few who profit mostly profit a little. The pitch said recruiting would compound, so why doesn't it? The FTC describes how the pay plans typically work: commissions for hitting benchmarks such as buying a set amount of product, getting others to buy it, or recruiting people who then buy it; selling to an outside customer at a markup is mentioned as a source of income "in some cases". [s33 ok] Taylor went further: he read the compensation plans of the 30 companies with published earnings data, and of more than 350 in all, and concluded that each of them pays primarily for recruiting a large downline rather than for retailing product, with most of the payout going to the highest levels. [s34 ok] That is one analyst's reading of the plans; the FTC's report describes the same structure but does not measure how much income actually comes from downlines versus retail customers. [s35 ok] The recruiters reject that framing; the same trainer insists that "you don't get paid for the act of building the team you only get paid when sales happen to a customer". [s36 ok] Both can be true at once, because in the plans the FTC describes, the "customer" can be the recruit, buying a set amount of product to qualify, and the plans pay commissions on those purchases. [s37 ok] Which brings us back to leverage: if income flows up from a downline, somebody has to be the downline. In that rank table, over 90 percent of participants were in a cohort averaging under $100. [s39 ok] Taylor's own summary of the pitch says the quiet part: the appeal is "time freedom or leveraged income", a flow from the efforts of others, and under these pay plans getting it depends on recruiting a downline more than on selling the product. [s40 ok] His own estimate is blunter: across the 30 companies, after subtracting required purchases and modest expenses, he puts the share losing money at 99.6 percent, about one in 238 profiting. [s41 ok] That number depends on his assumptions about what participants had to spend, how many dropped out and how many were ever counted, which he states; it is an estimate built on the companies' tables, not a measurement of anyone's bank account. [s42 ok] Critics like to finish with arithmetic; in John Oliver's version, if each recruit brings in a handful more, "you could only do that 13 cycles and you would exceed the population of the earth." [s43 ok] None of the studies here tests that arithmetic directly; what they show is the shape it predicts. [s44 ok] Taylor writes of pyramid schemes, a class in which he places MLMs, that recruitment eventually dries up as the market saturates, leaving the vast majority at the bottom. [s66 ok] And among the former participants AARP surveyed, 29 percent said they left because the commission structure mostly benefited those at the top, and 25 percent because the market was saturated. [s67 ok]

## Findings

Put the three side by side and they agree on the shape, though they measure different things. The companies' own statements: most participants received $1,000 or less in a year, before expenses, and most of the tables leave some participants out entirely. [s46 ok] The survey: 47 percent lost, 27 percent broke even, 25 percent profited, and of that 25, 14 points made under $5,000. [s47 ok] The analyst: 99.6 percent losing, once required purchases are counted as a cost. [s48 ok] And on the central promise: the plans do route money upward from a downline, and the rank tables show where that leaves most people, because in table after table the FTC looked at, the vast majority sit in the two or three lowest ranks with the smallest amounts. [s49 ok]

## Qualifications

Now the limits, because they matter: the AARP numbers are self-reported, by current and former participants, and nothing checks them against bank statements. [s50 ok] The FTC's figures are company payouts, not profit, and they cover the 70 companies whose statements were publicly available, which is not the whole industry. [s51 ok] Taylor is an independent analyst who argues against the industry, and his 99.6 percent is a calculation under assumptions he states, not a count of real accounts. [s52 ok] The split between recruiting income and retail income has been described in these documents, and argued, but not measured. [s53 ok] And the thirteen-cycles arithmetic is a thought experiment that none of these studies tested; the rank tables show where people end up, not why. [s54 ok]

## Implications

So, back to that message from your friend; suppose you say yes. The survey says the most likely outcome, by a wide margin, is that you lose money or break even: 74 percent of participants did, nearly three in four. [s56 ok] If you do profit, the same survey suggests it will most likely be under $5,000 over your whole time in the business. [s57 ok] And the leverage? It exists, in the plan documents; the rank tables show who it works for. Before you answer, ask for the income disclosure statement, and then ask three questions of it: who is left out of the table, whether the figures are before expenses, and how many people are in the bottom row.

## Conclusion

How much money do MLM participants actually make? For most of them, nothing, or less than nothing: in the nationally representative survey, 47 percent lost money and 27 percent broke even, and in the companies' own statements most participants received $1,000 or less in a year before expenses. [s60 ok] A quarter made some profit, mostly small, and half of one percent made a lot. [s61 ok] The leverage the pitch sells is real for the fraction at the top; in the plans Taylor read, most of the payout goes to the highest levels, and in the FTC's rank tables the vast majority sit at the bottom. [s62 ok] None of that is a verdict on your friend. It is a description of the structure she has joined, written mostly by the companies themselves, and it is worth reading before you join it too.
