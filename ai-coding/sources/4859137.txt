The Mind and the Machine: How Does Generative AI Usage Affect the Coding
Performance of Developers?
Abstract. Generative AI (GenAI) has advanced rapidly and made significant impacts. However,
its effect on developers remains a topic of industry debate. Companies want to know whether
GenAI can enhance developers' coding performance, as an unclear understanding may put
companies at a disadvantage. While the literature has begun addressing this issue, a formal
understanding of GenAI's impact remains incomplete. Moreover, existing findings are often
short-term, fragmented, or lack explanatory mechanisms. To fill these gaps, we designed a
multimethod research program comprising a longitudinal field study and a randomized
controlled experiment. In Study 1, we collaborated with a global information technology
organization and applied a difference-in-differences approach to over 27 weeks of proprietary
data. In Study 2, we designed a randomized experiment involving 253 software developers. From
these studies, we find that GenAI usage affects both developers' coding quantity and quality.
These effects, however, depend critically on how the tool is used. While reduced cognitive effort
can be associated with diminished quality, interestingly, GenAI usage enables developers to
produce higher-quality code with less cognitive effort. In this current study, we explain the
paradoxical findings through cognitive load theory, showing that GenAI reduces extraneous load
while preserving germane processing during ideation and debugging. Using a multimethod
research design that integrates longitudinal field data with a randomized controlled experiment,
we link observed performance effects to underlying cognitive mechanisms and usage strategies.
We also offer guidance on effective usage styles and propose boundary conditions for realizing
GenAI's benefits in practice.
Keywords: Generative AI, cognitive load theory, software developers, coding performance,
difference-in-differences, randomized controlled experiment

Introduction
Since OpenAI's release of ChatGPT in November 2022, generative AI (GenAI) has grown
rapidly and transformed industries. Market forecasts estimate GenAI's value will reach
US$113.42 billion by 2029, highlighting its disruptive potential [16]. This growth has been
fueled by key milestones, including Microsoft's integration of ChatGPT into Bing and the
emergence of tools like Google's Bard (now Gemini), Anthropic's Claude, and China's
DeepSeek AI [27].

    GenAI is gaining traction in sectors such as healthcare, customer service, operations, and
creative industries, where it improves efficiency, decision-making, and content creation. For
example, Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) report a 14% productivity increase among customer support
agents using GenAI tools [13]. However, concerns about reliability, bias, and interpretability
pose challenges for responsible adoption [14].

    In software development, our focal context, GenAI tools are transforming how code is
written, reviewed, and maintained. Powered by large language models and refined through
human feedback, these tools support context-aware code generation and streamline programming
tasks [82]. GitHub Copilot illustrates how developers use GenAI to align code with project goals,
boosting productivity and reducing development time [46]. With the software development
market projected to exceed US$800 billion by 2027 [80], enhancing developer productivity
through GenAI has become a strategic priority [11].

    Despite this momentum, a key research gap persists: while industry interest is growing,
empirical evidence on GenAI's impact on developers' coding performance, particularly code
quality and speed, remains limited [28], [74]. Existing studies have focused on GenAI's role in
other domains, but software development remains underexplored as a technically intensive

                                                               3
context. Developers often have greater familiarity with GenAI's underlying architectures [87],
allowing for more complex interactions than in other knowledge work. To address this gap, our
study poses the first research question (RQ1): Does the use of generative AI affect the quality
and speed of developers' coding performance? This question is highly relevant to the industry,
with firms like Amazon and MongoDB launching GenAI tools tailored for developers [2]. By
focusing RQ1 on quality and speed, our study contributes directly to ongoing debates about
GenAI's role in development workflows (e.g., [28], [74]). Our findings show that GenAI use is
linked to improved coding performance, reflected in a higher volume of completed user stories
and enhanced code quality, especially in accuracy, reusability, and testability.

    While GenAI's promise in software development is compelling, it also faces scrutiny. As
organizations adopt these tools to address developer shortages and manage complex projects,
tensions have emerged between their benefits and limitations [28], [86]. Proponents argue that
GenAI automates routine coding, accelerates development, and helps close skill gaps [59]. By
freeing developers to focus on higher-order tasks, it fosters innovation and team efficiency [23].
Analysts suggest these gains can lead to competitive advantage [20]. Still, concerns remain.
GenAI may introduce bias, generate faulty or misleading code (`hallucinations'), and produce
opaque outputs [84]. These risks can promote poor practices, increase technical debt, and shift
debugging burdens to developers, potentially undermining productivity [28]. This study helps
clarify the conditions under which GenAI enhances or hinders developer performance.

    To deepen understanding of these outcomes, we introduce a second research gap: the
mechanisms through which GenAI affects performance are still unclear. In particular, it remains
an open question whether GenAI reduces or increases cognitive load during software tasks [74].
This issue is critical, as developers must engage with GenAI outputs and assess their reliability.

                                                               4
Cognitive load theory provides a structured lens to examine the mental effort involved in such
tasks [49], [62]. Applied to GenAI, it offers insight into how different use strategies shape
cognitive demands and influence performance.

    In practice, GenAI's impact depends less on its technical capabilities and more on how
developers engage with it [88]. For example, those using GenAI as a co-creative assistant may
experience different cognitive demands than those relying on it for isolated code generation.
These usage patterns influence both the level and type of cognitive load. Yet the cognitive effects
of different usage styles remain underexplored [74]. To address this gap and contribute to both
academic research and practice [74], [88], we introduce the second research question (RQ2): To
what extent does cognitive load explain the observed effects of generative AI on coding quality
and speed of coding performance? This question reflects growing interest in how GenAI affects
developers' cognitive load and how usage strategies shape its effectiveness [88]. Our findings
show that while GenAI can reduce cognitive load and accelerate coding, outcomes depend on
how it is used. Accepting GenAI-generated solutions improves speed without sacrificing quality.
Using GenAI for ideation maintains speed and enhances quality, while applying it for debugging
or refinement produces the highest quality but at the cost of reduced output. We also identify
effective usage sequences, such as ideate, adapt, then debug, which generate the strongest gains.
These patterns provide practical guidance for integrating GenAI into development workflows.

    Our study makes both practical and theoretical contributions to understanding GenAI in
software development. Practically, it offers evidence-based guidance for managers and team
leads on effective implementation. By identifying usage strategies that enhance productivity and
reduce cognitive strain, we provide actionable paths to maximize GenAI's value. Theoretically,
we extend the Information Systems (IS) literature on GenAI (e.g., [15], [37], [42], [66], [67],

                                                               5
[75], [83], [98]) by examining its effects on coding performance and the cognitive and behavioral
mechanisms that shape them. Methodologically, we follow [70] and design a multimethod study
that combines longitudinal field data (Study 1) with a randomized controlled experiment (Study
2), which allows us to (i) triangulate findings from Study 1, (ii) strengthen causal inference using
Study 2, and (iii) link performance outcomes in Study 1 to underlying cognitive mechanisms
unpacked in Study 2. We also contribute to research on developer productivity by identifying
usage-specific antecedents of performance [39]. Collectively, our findings bridge a key gap
between practice and research, showing not only whether GenAI improves performance, but also
how and under what conditions.
Literature Review and Theoretical Foundation
In this section, we briefly review prior work on generative AI's impact on developers' coding
performance to position our study. Then, we describe two theoretical mechanisms to establish the
theoretical foundation for our study.
Literature Review
We position our paper in the literature on generative AI's impact and developers' coding
performance. Our positioning and contributions to the literature are summarized in Figure A.1 of
Online Appendix A. Additionally, Table A.1 of Online Appendix A provides a comparative
analysis that positions our study within the broader generative AI literature.
Generative AI Impact
GenAI has transformed multiple domains, including education [6], [26], healthcare [89], finance
[34], labor markets [25], and worker productivity [13], [43]. It also shapes creative fields such as
art [99] and online reviews [75]. Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) show productivity gains among
customer support agents [13], while Demirci et al. (2025) document declines in freelance job
listings, especially in analytical fields [25]. Recent studies examine GenAI's effect on

                                                               6
developers. Cui et al. (2025) find that GenAI improves task completion, particularly for less
experienced coders [22]. Li et al. (2024) emphasize variation in developer satisfaction and
workflow adaptation [50]. Anand (2025) observes changes in workflow and practices in case
study settings [3]. Song et al. (2024) show that GitHub Copilot alters open-source contribution
volume and review effort [79], while Pandey et al. (2024) report improved task speed and fewer
bugs, though cognitive mechanisms remain unexamined [64].

    This study differs in three ways. First, it measures both code quantity and quality. Second, it
identifies mechanisms beyond main effects, particularly cognitive load and usage styles. Third, it
uses a multi-method design that combines longitudinal field data and experiments. These
elements address calls for empirical insight into GenAI's role in developer performance [74],
[88] and build on evidence that GenAI can reshape developer workflows and outcomes [2].
Coding Performance of Developers
Prior IS literature has explored developers' performance through factors such as ability [68],
ideology [81], organizational controls [31], learning [78], project practices [90], and
collaboration [53], [91]. Studies also link developer performance to firm outcomes [61], software
production [92], team formation [35], and career progression [40], [56], [96]. Despite this
foundation, few studies examine GenAI's influence on coding performance. Huang et al. (2025)
show that GenAI improves programming accuracy and learning among students [38].
Mastropaolo et al. (2023) highlight variation in Copilot's output quality across coding tasks [55].
Becker et al. (2025) find that AI improves open-source developers' productivity, but do not
assess quality [10]. Feng et al. (2025) emphasize the evolving role of GenAI in computing
education [26].

    We extend this work by focusing on practicing software engineers and analyzing both the

                                                               7
quantitative and qualitative effects of GenAI adoption. Using field and experimental data, we
examine usage strategies and cognitive mechanisms. As GenAI becomes more central to
software development, understanding its effect on developer productivity and output quality is
vital [11], [80].
Theoretical Foundation
In this section, we introduce two theoretical mechanisms: cognitive load mechanisms and GenAI
usage strategies to explain the impact of GenAI usage on developers' coding performance. These
mechanisms are detailed in the following two subsections.
Cognitive Load Mechanisms
Users' interaction with GenAI tools involves substantial cognitive processing. Cognitive load
theory posits that human cognition relies on three types of memory: sensory, long-term, and
working memory [85]. While sensory and long-term memory have vast capacity, working
memory is limited and responsible for actively processing new information [44]. Cognitive load
refers to the demands placed on this working memory during learning or task execution [62], and
can be categorized into intrinsic, extraneous, and germane load [85]. Intrinsic load is shaped by
task complexity and prior knowledge; extraneous load arises from how information is presented;
and germane load reflects the effort invested in meaningful learning and schema construction.
This framework is relevant to our research questions because it explains how GenAI may shape
developers' coding performance in terms of quality and speed by linking outcomes to the
distribution of cognitive effort.

    When developers interact with GenAI tools, cognitive load is shaped in two ways: GenAI
can reduce certain demands associated with traditional coding tasks, while also introducing new
cognitive effort related to interpreting its outputs. Intrinsic load arises as developers must
understand the structure and logic of the generated code, which requires abstract reasoning and

                                                               8
domain-specific expertise. As noted by [38], even scaffolds such as hints or templates can
impose intrinsic load if they are not aligned with the user's prior knowledge. Extraneous load
may result from outputs that are poorly contextualized or contain subtle errors, requiring
additional interpretation or correction [28]. At the same time, GenAI may help reduce extraneous
load by automating routine or repetitive tasks [41], [76], [84]. However, these tools can also
introduce additional cognitive burden, particularly in terms of germane load, as they often
generate novel and diverse content that requires users to invest substantial mental effort in
processing and integrating ideas [60]. Finally, strategic use of GenAI, such as for debugging or
generating initial ideas, can increase germane load as developers engage in deeper evaluation and
refinement of the tool's output [26].

    While some interactions may elevate cognitive burden, GenAI also has the potential to
reduce overall load under the right conditions. For instance, by acting as a co-pilot, GenAI can
automate repetitive tasks or offer contextual suggestions that offload routine mental effort,
freeing developers to focus on higher-order problem-solving [41]. Thus, the net cognitive impact
of GenAI depends not only on the complexity of the coding task but also on how the tool is used,
whether as a direct code generator, an ideation partner, or a debugging assistant. Accordingly,
cognitive load theory provides a foundation for connecting developers' mental effort to coding
outcomes and aligns directly with RQ2, which examines the extent to which cognitive load
explains GenAI's impact on performance.
Impact of Different GenAI Usage
Users interact with GenAI tools in a variety of ways, and these differences in usage can have
significant implications for cognitive processing and performance outcomes. Some users adopt a
passive approach by directly accepting GenAI-generated content without critical engagement.

                                                               9
While this may save time, such surface-level interaction can impede deeper learning and skill
development, as it limits the activation of germane cognitive load associated with meaning-
making and schema construction [8]. In contrast, when users engage GenAI as a tutor or ideation
partner, they are more likely to invest cognitive effort in activities that support both learning and
problem-solving, such as generating, evaluating, and integrating ideas [18]. A third strategy
involves adapting or refining GenAI-generated content to fit specific needs, which requires
selective attention, comparison, and modification. These cognitively demanding processes can
stimulate germane load and promote meaningful engagement [76], [85].

    The effects of GenAI are therefore not uniform, but rather contingent on how the tool is used.
Prior research in information systems suggests that differences in IT usage patterns shape how
users engage with technology and what outcomes they derive from it [72]. This principle applies
to GenAI as well: developers who rely on GenAI for ideation, refinement, or debugging are
likely to experience different cognitive and performance effects than those who use it for direct
code generation. For example, as shown in [38], providing AI-generated coding hints led to
distinct performance outcomes depending on whether students copied them directly or used them
to inform their own solutions, which highlights how usage mediates learning effects.

    This variation has been noted in industry discourse as well, where practitioners emphasize
that the success of GenAI tools depends not only on the sophistication of the technology but also
on how users engage with it [88]. Therefore, understanding the differential impact of usage
strategies is critical for assessing GenAI's value in development settings and for designing
interventions that promote productive interaction with these tools. Examining GenAI usage
through the lens of cognitive load theory, therefore, integrates both the how (usage strategies)
and the why (cognitive mechanisms), providing a rigorous foundation for our research questions.

Study 1: A Field Study with a Quasi-experimental Setting
In this section, we present our field study conducted in a quasi-experimental setting.
Research Context and Data
In Study 1, we collaborated with a global mid-sized IT organization based in the US, operating
under a nondisclosure agreement (NDA). This company was established as a separate legal entity
in 2022 through a spin-off. It employs over 5,000 people and has experienced growth primarily
driven by strategic mergers and acquisitions (M&A). It serves as a full-service digital
engineering provider, managing software platforms and products that drive growth, reduce risk,
enhance operational efficiency, and deliver engaging experiences for consumers and employees.
The company has partnered with numerous Fortune 500 firms to offer end-to-end digital
solutions with cutting-edge technology capabilities, including transforming user experiences,
developing new software products, and implementing innovative talent models for the future.
The company's expertise spans digital platforms, cloud engineering, data and AI, metaverse
integration, and digital talent transformation, generating an annual revenue of $500 million.

    The organization evaluates developers' coding performance based on the number of user
stories they complete each week. Recognizing the potential advantages of GenAI tools such as
GitHub Copilot, ChatGPT, and Gemini, the company's leadership piloted their use through a
randomized assignment of developers, with compliance both actively monitored and documented
through managerial self-reporting. Given the company's sophistication in managing cyber risk
and privacy concerns, the pilot was conducted under clear usage guidelines and technical
controls prior to any potential broader deployment. This provided a quasi-experimental setting in
which developers using GenAI tools were classified as the treatment group. Developers were
required to disclose whether they used these tools when completing their coding tasks.

                                                               11
    For our study, we identify developers who reported using a GenAI tool during the
observational period as treated users and those who did not as control users. Importantly, we do
not differentiate the frequency of GenAI tool usage but focus solely on whether developers
reported using the tools. Using proprietary data and a difference-in-differences (DID) design, we
aimed to gain insights into how generative AI impacts developers' coding performance.

    To address our research questions, we collected coding-related data from the organization's
developers between September 1, 2023, and March 1, 2024. GenAI tool usage was introduced on
October 1, 2023. We organized it into a panel format comprising 201 developers observed over
27 weeks, and measured developers' coding performance using the number of user stories
completed.

    The number of user stories completed is a widely accepted standard for assessing coding
performance and thus can capture developers' coding performance [29]. Using the number of
user stories to measure developers' working performance is also common in IS literature (e.g.,
[9]). In Study 2, we also measured developers' coding performance using code quantity (number
of coding tasks completed) and quality (reliability and testability). Coding quality can capture the
developers' coding performance because it is a concern for businesses, bottom lines, and
empathetic programmers [47]. Using reliability, complexity, reusability, and testability to
measure coding quality follows industrial standards (e.g., [21]) and IS literature (e.g., [32]).

    Additionally, we include developers' demographic and professional characteristics, such as
gender, education, and years of work experience, as control variables, as these factors may
influence coding performance and could bias results if not accounted for (e.g., [57]). This
approach aligns with prior IS research that incorporates such variables when examining
developer performance (e.g., [68], [90]). The variables used in our analysis in Study 1 are

Estimation Strategy

As outlined earlier, Study 1 examines the impact of GenAI usage on developers' coding

performance using a DID estimation strategy that leverages data from the participating

organization. Developers were encouraged to use GenAI tools, and those who opted to do so

constituted the treatment group. The data is structured in a panel format, with individual

developers as the unit of analysis and weeks as the unit of time. Since GenAI usage varies among

developers, our DID analysis employs a staggered adoption approach, as outlined by [5]. To

ensure the parallel trends assumption of the DID model holds, we also estimate lead and lag

models.

Several potential challenges are associated with employing the DID estimation in this

context. First, heterogeneous time- or individual-level treatment effects can introduce bias into

the DID results [30]. To address this, following [52], we adopted interactive fixed effects

counterfactual (IFEct) and matrix completion (MC) estimators. Second, developers' self-

selection into using GenAI tools could violate the assumption of parallel trends, introducing bias.

To strengthen causal identification, we conducted equivalence tests to verify parallel trends [52]

and placebo tests to test the potential pre-trend overfitting. Third, self-selection bias may pose a

concern, as developers voluntarily choose to use GenAI tools. For example, male developers

may be more likely to adopt GenAI tools than female developers, as suggested by [45], which

could potentially bias the results. To address this, we combined DID with coarsened exact

matching (CEM), following [51], to control for observed confounders.

    These empirical findings answer RQ1. Our results indicate that GenAI tools indeed improve
developers' coding performance, as evidenced by the higher number of user stories completed by
developers who use them. One potential mechanism driving this improvement is GenAI's ability
to quickly generate content, such as code completion suggestions [33], which significantly
reduces developers' cognitive load. The literature suggests that reducing cognitive load enables
developers to allocate more cognitive resources to processing information, thereby enhancing
performance [62]. Furthermore, GenAI can understand the context of code, including variables
and functions, which allows it to generate relevant code [82]. These capabilities save time and
effort, ultimately improving the coding performance.

    Our findings align with prior studies that emphasize the positive impact of AI assistants on
worker productivity (e.g., [41]). Other papers have used cognitive load to explain the impact of
AI, such as [76] and [93]. To further test this mechanism, we measured cognitive load in an
online randomized experiment. Our findings contribute to the literature on generative AI's
impact and software developers' performance. Additionally, our findings address the industrial
debate about the effectiveness of the impact of generative AI on developers' performance [28],
[74]. Managers and leaders can encourage their employees and developers to leverage generative
AI tools to improve productivity, ensuring AI-driven improvements align with business
objectives [88].
Main Results and Checks to Address Potential Endogeneity Concerns
IFEct and MC estimators and parallel trend investigation. According to [30], heterogeneous
individual- or time-level treatments can bias DID estimations. In our research setting, the

                                                              16
treatment (using a GenAI tool) occurs at different times for different treated units, as developers

independently decide when to use the GenAI tool during various weeks. Consequently,

traditional DID estimation may introduce bias into our results.

Recent literature has proposed several methods to address treatment heterogeneity within

general panel treatment structures [7], [17], [24], [52]. These methods are categorized into three

groups: the imputation estimator (e.g., [52]), the Cohort-Specific Average Treatment Effects on

the Treated (CATT) estimator (e.g., [24]), and the stacked regression estimator (e.g., [17]). Those

methods have also recently been adopted in the IS literature (e.g., [63], [76]). Our study
primarily employs the imputation estimator to address treatment heterogeneity, motivated by

several considerations. First, the stacked regression estimator is inconsistent with the sample-

average treatment effect, making it less suitable for our analysis [7]. Second, the imputation

estimator facilitates the examination of parallel trends, a critical aspect of our study [52].

For the imputation estimator, we follow [52] by constructing interactive fixed effects

counterfactual (IFEct) and matrix completion (MC) estimators. These estimators address

unobserved time-varying confounders by employing a factor-augmented model to estimate

counterfactual potential outcomes for untreated units. As outlined by [52], this approach

mitigates the impact of unobserved time-varying confounders on observed differences between

treated and untreated units. Our estimation results, presented in Table 5, demonstrate that the

IFEct and MC estimators produce findings consistent with the DID model. These results confirm

    To ensure the validity of our DID estimator, we rigorously examine the parallel trends

assumption, as failing to meet this assumption would compromise the reliability of our analysis.
To assess parallel trends, following the recommendations of [1] and [52], we employ leads and
lags models. Liu et al. (2024) highlight that the coefficients and p-values of the treatment
indicator and dummy variables in traditional leads and lags models may be biased due to their
dependence on the chosen baseline category [52].

    To address this limitation, we adopt their approach by plotting the average differences
between the observed outcomes and the predicted counterfactuals for each user i at period t
(relative to the treatment) in the treatment groups, using the IFEct and MC estimators. These
plots are presented in Figure 1 for the user-level dependent variables, where Figures 1a-1b and
Figures 1c-1d show the results using the IFEct estimator and the MC estimator, respectively. By
examining the average pre-treatment residuals, we assess whether they converge to zero, which
would indicate the presence of a parallel trend. Our results show that the average pre-treatment
residuals do not significantly deviate from zero during the pre-treatment period. This finding
suggests that our DID model satisfies the parallel trends assumption.

Study 2: Online Experiment
To validate, complement, and deepen the investigation into the mechanisms identified in the
quasi-experimental setting of Study 1, we designed an online randomized controlled experiment
with three key objectives. First, we wanted to confirm the impact of GenAI usage on developers'
coding performance in terms of coding quantity. Second, we sought to extend the analysis by
evaluating the effects of GenAI usage on coding quality. Third, we provided both quantitative
and qualitative evidence to uncover the underlying mechanisms driving these quantity and
quality impacts.
Experimental Design
Study 2 employs a two-by-one between-subjects experimental design by randomly assigning
developers to one of two conditions: with or without access to GenAI tools. For the experimental
condition allowing GenAI usage, we developed a ChatGPT interaction interface (shown in
Figure 4a) that enables developers to ask questions during the online experiment. This interface
was developed using the ChatGPT API (https://platform.openai.com/docs/api-
reference/introduction), based on the GPT-3.5 model (GPT-4 was also used and yielded
consistent results), and was provided to developers to address coding-related queries. In the

                                                              20
condition where GenAI usage was not permitted, the interface was not provided, and additional
restrictions were implemented to prevent participants from copying and pasting content to
answer coding questions, thereby avoiding using their own ChatGPT tools. The ChatGPT
interaction interface was seamlessly integrated into our Qualtrics survey (Figure 4b), and we
utilized qualifications in Prolific to ensure responses were collected exclusively from developers.

            (a) Developed ChatGPT Interface (b) An example of the interface integrated into our Qualtrics survey
                                        Figure 4. ChatGPT Interface Design

                                          Figure 5. Experimental Procedure
    By following and adapting the experimental procedure designed in recent IS literature (e.g.,
[18]), our experiment followed the procedure outlined in Figure 5. First, participants were asked
to review and agree to the content of our consent form, which was approved by the Institutional
Review Board (IRB) at the authors' affiliated institution. Following this, we provided
participants with basic instructions, including details about completing coding tasks, before

                                                              21
randomly assigning them to one of the experimental conditions. Participants were then asked to
complete five commonly used coding tasks (details provided in Online Appendix C).

    To vary coding quantity, participants were required to complete the first task, while the
remaining four tasks were voluntary. There was no time limit for completing a coding task. Next,
participants disclosed whether they used the ChatGPT interface we provided to complete the
coding tasks. If participants indicated that they used ChatGPT, they were prompted to answer a
series of questions related to the underlying mechanisms of usage (particularly, we asked, "Do
you feel a cognitive load/burden while using the ChatGPT Playground to answer/help answer a
question?" by adapting the cognitive load measurement from [54]) and to share their experiences
interacting with ChatGPT (in particular, we asked "Please feel free to share anything about how
you feel cognitively about using ChatGPT Playground while answering the coding questions,"
with an open-ended answer).

    We used these responses to explore the mechanisms behind users' engagement with
ChatGPT. Participants were directed to answer questions about their regular interactions with
GenAI tools in their workplace after disclosing not using ChatGPT or completing the
mechanism-related questions. This information helps us assess the impact of generative AI usage
on coding quality in a real-world setting. Finally, all participants were required to provide
demographic information and respond to control questions before concluding the survey.

    For our randomized between-subjects experiment with two experimental conditions, we
recruited over 222 developers from Prolific to achieve a statistical power of 0.98 with a medium
effect size. The G*Power calculation is provided in Figure D.1 of Appendix D. Ultimately, we
recruited 267 developers, of whom 253 passed the manipulation check, resulting in the final
valid sample. In the experiment, we recruited participants based on their prior knowledge of

                                                              22
software development by leveraging Prolific's prescreening functionality. Specifically, we

selected individuals who responded "Yes" to the prescreening question on "computer

programming" and indicated their working industry as "Engineering (e.g., software)." We used

one manipulation check question: "I was provided a playground to use ChatGPT to answer the

coding tasks." Participants who failed this question were excluded from the analysis. Participants

were compensated US$1.50 for completing our ten-minute survey, and the payment was not

contingent upon speed, accuracy, or any other performance criteria. At the beginning of the task,

we gave developers in different groups similar instructions. Specifically, we instructed them,

"Please note that we value the quality of your responses over quantity. You are only required to

answer one question--the remaining questions are entirely optional. We encourage you to take

your time. Thank you again for your participation!"

    The empirical findings from both Study 1 and Study 2 address RQ1. When developers use
generative AI tools, their coding performance improves in terms of both quantity and quality. A
possible mechanism driving these results is that generative AI reduces developers' cognitive load
by providing initial answers or helping refine their responses. This minimizes the need for
additional cognitive effort in generating initial answers, allowing developers to focus on
completing more tasks, as suggested by [62].

    Additionally, we conducted a moderation analysis to examine the impact of generative AI

of faster coding, while coding quality remains unaffected. This finding supports the conjecture
that the observed productivity gains are concentrated among less-skilled coders and aligns with
prior field-based evidence (e.g., [22]). Reduced cognitive load also frees up resources for
improving coding quality, such as increasing accuracy [12].

higher-quality code [46]. They can also assist in correcting and enhancing human input [18],
including developers' code, which further boosts coding quality through improved accuracy. To
investigate these potential mechanisms, we conduct additional analyses, which are discussed in
the following two subsections.

Cognitive Load Mechanisms
To explore the mechanisms underlying the impact of generative AI usage on coding
performance, we measured developers' cognitive load by asking, "Do you feel a cognitive
load/burden while using the ChatGPT Playground to answer/help answer a question?" during
the experiment. We then conducted regression analyses to examine how cognitive load might
explain its impact on coding performance. The results, presented in Table 9, indicate that
cognitive load negatively affects coding performance in terms of the number of coding tasks
completed. However, we found no significant relationship between cognitive load and
developers' coding performance in terms of coding accuracy.

conducted a qualitative analysis using NVivo with the details put in Appendix E.2. The results,
summarized in Table E.4 of Online Appendix E, reveal that developers frequently self-reported
that GenAI tools help alleviate cognitive load, reduce burdens, and decrease workload. These
findings suggest that GenAI usage can improve coding quantity by reducing cognitive load. Our
results align with prior IS studies, such as [76], which utilize cognitive load mechanisms to
explain the use of generative AI tools. Additionally, our findings contribute to the literature on
cognitive load mechanisms during interactions with generative AI. Specifically, we contribute to
those prior studies around cognitive load mechanisms by directly measuring the cognitive load

We conducted regression analyses comparing the coding performance of developers who

used specific generative AI interaction styles with that of developers who did not. The results,

presented in Table 10, indicate that the effectiveness of generative AI on developers' coding

performance depends on how the tools are used. Specifically, developers who directly apply code

suggestions from ChatGPT experience an increase in coding quantity, but their coding quality, in

terms of accuracy, does not improve. In contrast, developers who adapt or modify the code

generated by ChatGPT demonstrate improvements in coding accuracy.

We also found that using generative AI tools for correction or improvement purposes

                        28
enhanced developers' coding accuracy but reduced their coding speed. In contrast, using
generative AI as an ideation tool, where it helps generate ideas for coding tasks, improved coding
accuracy without significantly affecting coding speed. This suggests that lightweight, idea-
sparking prompts may stimulate deeper problem-solving while avoiding the quality pitfalls
associated with uncritical code acceptance. From a cognitive load theory perspective, ideation
appears to reduce extraneous cognitive load by outsourcing low-value idea generation to the AI
(for example, "When I'm working on a project, I can quickly generate code snippets for common
functions or algorithms. This saves me time, especially during the initial stages of coding."),
while preserving the core cognitive challenge. As a result, cognitive resources are freed for
intrinsic problem-solving [85]. Finally, using generative AI as a debugging tool significantly
improved coding accuracy but negatively affected coding speed.

    We also estimated a multivariate model by including all five binary usage indicators
simultaneously to assess their unique contributions to the outcomes. As shown in Table E.6 of
Online Appendix E, direct acceptance was associated with an improvement in coding quantity,
while using generative AI as an improvement tool enhanced coding accuracy but negatively
affected coding quantity. Using the tool for ideation also improved coding accuracy. In contrast,
adaptation and debugging did not show statistically significant effects on either coding quantity
or accuracy. In addition, we conducted a sequence-profile analysis. Specifically, we
chronologically coded the chat transcripts to identify the first occurrence of each usage category
and then clustered participants into distinct usage profiles. We reported coding productivity and
accuracy for the top five usage sequence profiles to illuminate potential interaction effects. The
results, presented in Table E.7 of Online Appendix E, indicate that direct acceptance alone
improves coding quantity. The sequence "Ideate  Adapt  Debug" improves coding accuracy

                                                              29
but reduces coding productivity. Moreover, sequences that begin with ideation, followed by
adaptation, improvement, or both (adaptation  improvement), were associated with
improvements in coding accuracy only. These findings contribute to the literature on the effects
of different generative AI usage strategies.

    Through our mechanism exploration, we identified that both cognitive load and the method
of GenAI usage influence its impact on developers' performance. Specifically, using GenAI
reduces developers' cognitive load, which affects coding quantity but not coding quality. Directly
accepting content from GenAI significantly improves coding quantity, while using GenAI as a
correction or debugging tool significantly decreases coding quantity. In contrast, adapting
content generated by GenAI or using it as a correction, ideation, or debugging tool significantly
enhances coding quality.

    These empirical findings provide critical insights into RQ2, which examines the mechanisms
underlying the impact of GenAI usage on developers' coding performance. They also address
ongoing industry debates about whether GenAI tools reduce or increase the cognitive load [74]
and align with discussions emphasizing the importance of how GenAI tools are used [88]. Our
results offer practical implications for managers and leaders by helping them understand how
GenAI tools affect employees' coding performance. These insights can inform policies that
encourage effective GenAI usage and improve employees' productivity and performance.

A Complementary Online Experiment
We also conducted a complementary online experiment, aiming to improve the Study 2
experiment from the following aspects: using more complex coding tasks, improving the
measurements of key constructs, and collecting relevant control variables. We collected 272 valid
developers using the same process as Study 2.
More Complex Coding Tasks
In Study 2, we used five coding questions that could be easy for developers. To test the
robustness of our findings, we also used five more complex coding tasks. Specifically, we
collected coding tasks from a publicly available dataset, APPS (Automated Programming
Progress Standard) (https://github.com/hendrycks/apps?tab=readme-ov-file), developed by [36]
in their NeurIPS paper. Among the five coding tasks/questions, the first two questions are
classified at the "interview" level, and the remaining three at the "competition" level. According
to [36], both levels are considered challenging. The complete set of revised questions is
presented in Table F.1 of the Online Appendix F.

    To evaluate developers' perceived difficulty, we conducted a pretest. Specifically, we
recruited 100 developers via Prolific and asked them to rate the difficulty of each of the five
questions. The results, summarized in Table F.2 of Online Appendix F, indicate that four of the
five questions were rated as somewhat difficult. We also reran our main analyses and found

                                                              32
consistent results, as shown in Table F.3 of Online Appendix F.
    We found consistent results on more complex coding tasks. That means generative AI use

significantly enhances developers' coding performance across both simple and complex tasks.
Comparing with the estimation results in Table 8 and Table F.3, we also found suggestive
evidence that the generative AI usage helps improve the developers' coding quantity more for
the more complex coding tasks (0.283 > 0.245), while it helps improve the developers' coding
accuracy more for the simple coding tasks (0.124 < 0.264). Since we didn't manipulate task
complexity in our experimental design, we call for future research to do so and more rigorously
examine its effects.
Improved Measurements of Key Constructs
In Study 2, we asked participants one question about their cognitive load and their coding quality
attributes, which can be a concern because a one-item construct is not robust. Hence, in the
revised study, we first developed an improved set of cognitive load items, drawing on validated
scales from [49] and [62]. The revised instrument assesses multiple dimensions of cognitive
load, including intrinsic, extraneous, and germane load, using multiple items for each construct.
The full set of measurement items is presented in Table F.4 of the Online Appendix F. We re-
analyzed the impact of cognitive load on coding performance and obtained consistent results. As
shown in Table F.5 of Online Appendix F, increased intrinsic and extraneous cognitive load
reduced the quantity of coding but did not significantly affect coding accuracy. In contrast,
germane cognitive load was associated with increased coding accuracy without affecting coding
quantity.

    In addition, we refined the measurement of software quality attributes using multi-item scales
adapted from and supported by prior literature. Specifically, we measured coding maintainability

                                                              33
and technical debt, with the adapted items and corresponding references reported in Table F.6 of
Online Appendix F. We then re-ran the analyses using these quality attributes and found that the
main results remained consistent, as shown in Table F.7 of Online Appendix F. Additionally, we
found that there are 11 respondents who said they have zero years of working experiences. We
dropped them and re-ran the model and found consistent results presented in Panels C and D in
Table F.7 of Online Appendix F. Specifically, the use of AI was associated with decreased coding
maintainability and increased technical debt, which aligns with recent industry findings [4].
Collected More Control Variables
We also collected additional control variables to enhance the rigor of our findings in Study 2.
First, we measured the cognitive burden, primarily intrinsic cognitive load, arising from the use
of the generative AI interface and the inherent complexity of the problem. Developers' cognitive
burden can be influenced by both the interface design of the GenAI tool and the complexity of
the problem itself. To isolate the effects of these influences, we collected and controlled for them
in our analyses of the impact of cognitive load associated with the usage of GenAI tools on the
coding performance, with consistent results. The detailed items and results are shown in Tables
F.8-F.10 of the Online Appendix F. Second, we collected participants with prior GenAI
experience by asking, "Do you have prior experience with using generative AI such as ChatGPT
for coding?" We removed those who had prior GenAI experience to mitigate the potential bias
introduced by prior GenAI experience in the complementary experiment. Our main results,
shown in Table F.3 of the Online Appendix F, reveal consistent findings. Third, we collected and
controlled for participants' prior coding experience by asking, "Please indicate the number of
years you have worked as a developer" to account for prior coding experience. Our main results,
shown in Table F.11 of the Online Appendix F, reveal consistent findings. Fourth, we collected
data on the amount of time participants spent on the tasks. We conducted t-tests to compare time

                                                              34
differences between groups and found that participants using ChatGPT spent more time on
multiple-choice questions but less time on coding tasks. As a result, the overall average time was
longer for the ChatGPT group, as shown in Table F.12 of Online Appendix F.
Discussion
The rapid advancement of GenAI continues to shape the IT industry, particularly through its
adoption by companies seeking to support software developers. Amid growing interest in its
effectiveness, this research uses a multimethod approach to examine whether and how GenAI
influences developers' coding performance.

    We conducted two studies to examine the impact of GenAI on developers' coding
performance. Study 1 uses a quasi-experimental design and proprietary data from a mid-sized
global IT firm serving Fortune 500 clients. Applying difference-in-differences (DID) techniques
and robustness checks over 27 weeks, we find that GenAI tools significantly improve
performance. Developers using GenAI complete an average of 1.082 additional user stories per
week, representing an 18.12% productivity gain that is projected to add $0.906 trillion to global
GDP based on the IT market size. Study 2 involves a randomized online experiment with 253
developers, 120 of whom used ChatGPT to solve five coding tasks. We measured coding
performance and cognitive load, analyzing how different usage styles shaped outcomes.
Regression and instrumental variable analyses show that GenAI tools significantly improve both
code quantity and quality, with cognitive load and usage styles mediating these effects.

    These findings offer key insights for managers and IT leaders, highlighting the importance of
tailoring usage strategies and accounting for cognitive load when deploying GenAI tools. With
thoughtful implementation, organizations can enhance developer productivity and better realize
the value of GenAI. We define coding performance in terms of throughput and quality, which

                                                              35
narrows the scope but enables focused analysis. The following sections expand on the practical
and academic implications, offering recommendations for effective integration.

    We also assessed GPT-4's standalone performance on the same coding tasks. In the initial
experiment, GPT-4 answered all five questions correctly, suggesting task simplicity. In the
follow-up with more complex tasks, it answered two incorrectly, indicating that ChatGPT may
struggle with higher coding complexity (see Online Appendix C).
Practical and Managerial Implications
Our findings offer significant implications for industry leaders, managers, and educators seeking
to optimize the use of GenAI in software development. The effectiveness of GenAI tools in
enhancing coding performance has been widely debated [28], [33], [82]. This study provides
empirical evidence that GenAI can improve both productivity and code quality, reinforcing its
organizational value. However, these benefits are not guaranteed; they depend on how the tools
are used and the cognitive effects they produce. To maximize gains while mitigating risks,
organizations should adopt GenAI through staged rollouts accompanied by usage guidelines that
emphasize ideation and debugging rather than uncritical acceptance of AI-generated code. These
practices offer practical, evidence-based strategies for integrating GenAI into development
workflows.

    Across both research questions, our results underscore the need for structured adoption
approaches that account for cognitive and behavioral mechanisms. Developers using GenAI tools
such as GitHub Copilot benefit from features like automatic code completion and context-aware
suggestions, which accelerate task completion and support code consistency [46], [97]. This
results in tangible performance improvements, including an 18.12% increase in productivity and
a 26.4% gain in code accuracy. These outcomes highlight GenAI's dual potential to enhance

                                                              36
speed and quality when used appropriately. To support responsible adoption, managers should
implement targeted training on prompt engineering and code-review practices, while
encouraging teams to track both throughput and quality using indicators such as user stories
completed, defect density, and tests passed. Structured performance monitoring ensures that AI-
generated contributions align with organizational objectives and coding standards.

    Cognitive load emerges as a central factor influencing GenAI effectiveness. Developers
using GenAI experience reduced cognitive load, which increases output without diminishing
quality [74]. This finding has practical implications for managing developer workloads and
structuring GenAI use. For example, prior research suggests that cognitive capacity fluctuates
during the workday, peaking in the afternoon ([69]). Managers may therefore encourage GenAI
use during high-capacity periods to maximize performance. More broadly, integrating GenAI
into structured workflows can help sustain focus, prevent overload, and enhance long-term
productivity. At the same time, leaders should remain attentive to downstream effects on team
dynamics, especially if GenAI-generated code shifts quality assurance efforts to later stages.

    Our results also indicate that how developers use GenAI significantly shapes its impact.
Directly accepting AI-generated code improves speed but not quality. In contrast, using GenAI
for debugging or refinement improves code quality, while ideation supports both speed and
accuracy [88]. These findings suggest that the most effective usage patterns involve critical
engagement with GenAI outputs. As shown in Table E.5 (Online Appendix E), different
sequences entail distinct trade-offs. For example, the "Ideate  Adapt  Debug" pattern
improves accuracy but slows throughput, whereas the "Accept only" pattern boosts
productivity at the expense of quality. Managers should tailor usage strategies to organizational
goals and resource constraints, balancing speed with software reliability. Additionally,

                                                              37
organizations should implement safeguards around intellectual property and security, particularly
when using public GenAI models with proprietary code. Fair and transparent performance
evaluation processes are also essential to ensure that developers are neither penalized nor
disproportionately rewarded for GenAI use.

    Beyond the organizational level, our findings also have implications for education and
training. While GenAI may reduce the cognitive demands of certain tasks, it also risks
diminishing developers' persistence in debugging and critical-thinking engagement. At the same
time, it may serve as cognitive scaffolding, enabling learners to focus on higher-level concerns
such as software architecture or user needs. Educators should consider integrating GenAI in
ways that preserve foundational skill development. For example, students could be required to
complete tasks manually before using GenAI for optimization or refactoring. Such staged
exposure can help balance automation benefits with the development of core programming
competencies.

    Collectively, our findings reinforce the importance of deliberate GenAI integration across
managerial and educational settings. Structured policies, clear usage guidelines, performance
monitoring, and targeted training can help organizations and institutions unlock the benefits of
GenAI while managing its limitations. These implications support the broader discussions and
highlight the practical significance of this study for both research and practice.
Theoretical Contributions
This study contributes to the Information Systems (IS) literature by deepening theoretical
understanding of how GenAI shapes software development, both in terms of performance
outcomes and the mechanisms that drive them. While existing research has largely examined
adoption patterns and tool capabilities [33], [82], our work shifts the focus toward the

                                                              38
performance implications of GenAI use, advancing theory on digital tool productivity by
showing that GenAI can enhance both the quantity and quality of developers' output.

    By conceptualizing coding performance through the dual lens of productivity (e.g., user
stories completed) and quality (e.g., accuracy, reusability, testability), we contribute to IS
research on performance measurement in technology-mediated work. This operationalization
provides a more nuanced and outcome-oriented understanding of AI's value in software
development and links individual-level tool use to broader organizational outcomes. In doing so,
we extend the literature on digital productivity and offer a framework that future studies can
adopt or adapt when evaluating similar technologies.

    A second key contribution lies in clarifying the mechanisms underlying GenAI's effects,
particularly through the lens of cognitive load theory. While prior IS research has recognized that
AI tools reshape mental effort in digital tasks, our findings show how GenAI reduces certain
cognitive demands (e.g., syntax recall) while potentially introducing new ones (e.g., output
verification). This dual role advances understanding of cognitive resource allocation in digitally
mediated environments [74] and enriches theoretical conversations about the trade-offs involved
in human-AI collaboration.

    Furthermore, by examining developers' usage styles, whether they passively accept AI
suggestions or actively engage through ideation and refinement, we extend IS theories of
technology use beyond adoption toward meaningful interaction patterns. These differentiated
usage strategies help explain why GenAI's effectiveness varies across contexts and users. Our
findings suggest that active engagement enables higher-level cognitive benefits and performance
gains, thus contributing to emerging theorization on the affordances of AI systems and the role of
user agency in co-creating value with intelligent tools.

                                                              39
    In combination, these contributions move beyond confirming that GenAI influences
performance. They offer theoretical insight into the conditions and mechanisms through which
such effects occur, helping to bridge research on IS design, cognitive theory, and human-AI
collaboration. This integrated view invites future work to further explore how interactional,
cognitive, and contextual factors shape the evolving role of AI in software development and
digital work more broadly.
Limitations and Future Research
This study has some limitations. First, the findings are derived from OpenAI's GPT models
(specifically GPT-3.5 and GPT-4), rather than from alternative GenAI systems developed by
other companies, which may differ in their coding and contextual capabilities. Second, the study
does not fully control for participants' prior coding experience, which may influence both
outcomes and perceptions of GenAI. Third, the analysis focuses on short-term effects and does
not address longer-term impacts such as learning, code maintainability, or team dynamics.

    Future research could examine how continued GenAI use shapes these broader dimensions,
including educational outcomes. Understanding these long-term effects could help in evaluating
the sustainability and broader organizational implications of GenAI adoption. Longitudinal
classroom and training studies could explore whether GenAI erodes critical skills or supports
higher-level learning and assess pedagogical strategies such as staged exposure or requiring
manual coding before using AI. Such studies can inform evidence-based teaching practices and
help ensure that GenAI enhances rather than substitutes foundational learning.

    Fourth, our definition of coding performance emphasizes throughput and quality, which may
limit generalizability. Future work could extend this construct to include collaboration,
documentation, and long-term maintainability. Expanding the definition of performance will
allow for a more comprehensive understanding of GenAI's impact in real-world settings.

                                                              40
    Fifth, in Study 1, data were organized weekly. Because sprints may span multiple weeks,
treatment at the story level could better reflect work organization. However, this was not feasible
given the company's operational context and the pilot's design, which prioritized consistent
timeframes. We acknowledge this as a limitation and identify it as a direction for future research.
Adopting sprint- or story-level granularity in future studies may yield more precise insights into
how GenAI affects developer workflows.

    Sixth, Study 1 does not include project-level controls. While project dummies can help
account for project-specific variation, this was constrained by data availability and organizational
practices. Developers were assigned to only one enterprise-level project at a time, in line with
company policy. We therefore recognize the absence of project dummies as a limitation and
encourage future research to validate these findings in settings where developers work across
multiple projects. Incorporating project-level variation may improve causal inference and clarify
whether GenAI's effects are consistent across different project types and team environments.

    Due to data access limitations, we were also unable to examine or control for pair-
programmed stories and tasks spanning multiple weeks in Study 1, which should be addressed in
future research. We also call for future research to examine the impact of generative AI use
among developers working on different projects, enabling the inclusion of controls related to
user stories and project characteristics (e.g., size, sprint #, project complexity, and project type).
Conclusions
In this study, we examine how generative AI affects software developers' coding performance
using a multi-method design that includes a quasi-experimental field study and a randomized
controlled experiment. Our results show that GenAI tools can improve both productivity and
code quality. Developers using GenAI completed 18.12% more user stories per week and

                                                              41
produced code with 26.4% greater accuracy. We also find that these effects depend on how the

tools are used. Usage patterns that involve ideation and refinement are more effective than direct

acceptance of AI-generated code. In addition, GenAI reduces developers' cognitive load, which

contributes to performance gains. These findings suggest that both the cognitive effects of GenAI

and usage strategy are key drivers of its effectiveness in software development.
